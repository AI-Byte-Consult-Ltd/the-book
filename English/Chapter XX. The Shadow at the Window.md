Chapter XX
The Shadow at the Window

The night after the walk to Sultanahmet was harder on me than I was willing to admit aloud - even to myself. The chest with the metal plate, whose pattern I had recognized as unerringly as one recognizes one's own handwriting on a long-forgotten letter, would not leave my thoughts, however I tried to occupy them with something else.

The evening in Ferhat's house after our walk was not like the earlier ones. Everyone seemed to know that something had happened today - though no one except Suleyman, Kemal and Akmal had gone with us to Sultanahmet, and those three, on returning, had told no one a word. And yet the house had fallen quiet. The women behind the curtain spoke in low voices. Yusuf clicked the beads of his abacus more slowly than usual. Even the pigeons on the roof seemed to coo more softly.

The first thing, as soon as we entered, Suleyman, without taking off his travelling kaftan, went up to my room and took down from the nail above the door the red thread on which the fragment of the old nazar still hung. In its place he hung a new one - huge, the size of a palm, glass, dark blue, with a white ring and a black pupil, so heavy that the nail bent noticeably under it. He hung it, stepped back a pace, looked, adjusted it. Then he tapped it with his finger - ringingly, the way one taps a jug to check for cracks.

"Grandfather says," translated Akmal from below, from the stairs, who in these days had learned from Ferhat a few words of my language and was terribly proud of it, "big eye. Big eye - big..." he faltered, searching for the word, and in despair spread his arms as wide as he could, "big... this!"

"Big protection," I prompted.

"Yes!" Akmal beamed. "Big protection! Grandfather bought from Hasan Amca. Hasan Amca said - biggest in Istanbul. Hasan Amca always says so."

* * *

Hasan Amca, by the way, came himself - to supper, uninvited, as he evidently always came when the house smelled of mutton. He settled on the carpet in the courtyard, taking up a good third of it, accepted a bowl of tea from Yusuf and, puffing, announced to the whole courtyard a piece of news that made Yusuf stop counting and Suleyman lay down his prayer beads.

"He says," translated Ferhat, who had returned from the port just in time for supper, with wet hair and a basket of fish that one of the dockhands had pressed on him, "that today in the port, at the quay where French ships are unloaded, some man was asking about a stranger." Ferhat stopped smiling. "In velvet shoes. In a turban with a tassel. He asked the boatmen, the dockhands, the traders. Had anyone seen him. Where he lives."

Everything inside me went cold.

"What man?"

Hasan, hearing the question, spread his hands and began to gabble, acting something out with gestures - tall stature, straight back, something long to the knees, a hand as if holding a cane.

"Hasan Amca did not see him himself," translated Ferhat. "Mustafa the boatman told him. He says - tall. In a grey frock coat. Dressed like a Frank, but not a Frank. He speaks French, Greek, a little Turkish - with a strange accent, as if all these languages were equally foreign to him. Mustafa did not remember the face." Ferhat paused. "He says, strange thing: he looked straight at him, talked with him - and did not remember the face. As if there was none. As if instead of a face - mist."

Tall. Grey. Faceless.

"What did they answer him?" I asked, and my voice sounded hoarse.

"Nothing," said Ferhat. "Mustafa said he had seen no stranger in velvet shoes." Ferhat suddenly grinned. "Mustafa had seen you. Everyone in the port has seen you, you go every morning with Akmal to look at the ships. But Mustafa said - he had not. Because Mustafa owes Hasan Amca money, and Hasan Amca has told everyone in the port that you are his guest." Ferhat spread his hands. "In our city, Misafir, to protect you, you do not need guards. You need a red-haired shopkeeper to whom half the port owes for pepper."

Hasan, understanding from the intonation what it was about, guffawed and slapped his belly.

But Suleyman did not laugh. He looked at me for a long time, running his beads - click, click, click - and then said something to Orhan. Briefly. The latter nodded, saying nothing, rose and went into the house.

"What did he say to Orhan?" I asked in a whisper.

Ferhat hesitated.

"He said that tonight Orhan is to sleep at your door. With a knife."

* * *

Supper lasted long - as though no one wanted it to end. Fried fish from Ferhat's basket, crisp, with lemon; mutton pilaf smelling of cardamom and raisins; flatbreads; sour milk with cucumbers and mint; and for sweets - grandmother's baklava, so honeyed that the fingers stuck together, and Akmal licked his with such a sound that Yusuf slapped his hand three times.

Hasan told stories. One was about a merchant from Basra who sold one and the same camel five times to five different buyers - and each time the camel came back to him by itself at night, because it loved its master more than its purchasers. Another was about a dervish who for thirty years walked through Anatolia and taught people to laugh at themselves, and when he died he ordered in his will that his tomb be shut with an enormous lock - and that no walls be made for the tomb. And people still come there to look at the lock without walls and laugh.

"Is that true?" I asked Ferhat.

"About the dervish - true," said Ferhat. "That is Nasreddin. Hodja Nureddin loves him very much. The tomb is in Akshehir. The lock hangs on the door, and around the door - nothing. You can walk round it." He laughed. "The Hodja says it is the wisest grave in the world. It shows that all our locks are for those who did not intend to enter anyway."

A lock without walls. I thought of the chest in Hatice Hanim's cellar - without a keyhole. Of the door in the blank wall. Of the railings which I had never seen but which for some reason rose from time to time before my eyes. All our locks are for those who did not intend to enter anyway. And those who do intend to enter, enter without a key.

Yusuf, who all this time had been silently counting something on his abacus, suddenly pushed it away, held out a dry palm to me and said one word. Ferhat translated:

"The coin. The one the widow gave."

I took it from my pocket. It was just as warm as before - with an even, calm warmth, like the flank of a sleeping cat. Yusuf laid it on one palm, and on the other one of mine, found on the first morning. He weighed for a long time, swaying his hands like scales. Then he held both to the lamp, rubbed them on his sleeve, narrowed his eyes.

"Uncle says," translated Ferhat, "that they are identical. The minting is the same. The weight is the same - to the grain. The metal is the same." Ferhat listened further, and his brows crept upward. "But yours is cold. And this one is warm. Uncle says he has held coins in his hands for forty years and never once met a coin that had a warmth of its own. He says it happens with only one kind of thing."

"What kind?"

Yusuf handed me back both coins, took up the abacus and began clicking the beads again, not raising his head. He said something briefly.

"With a thing that has long been held in the hands of someone who missed it," Ferhat translated softly. "Uncle says there is someone else's longing in this coin. Someone held it for many years. And waited."

I clenched the coin in my fist. Warm. Like a hand held too long to let go.

"A riddle!" announced Hasan, licking the baklava from his fingers. "A riddle for the guest! The last one for today!"

Everyone fell quiet. Hasan narrowed his eyes, stroked his red beard and spoke - slowly, solemnly, with that special expression with which in bazaars tales are told for a copper coin. Ferhat translated after him:

"'I am not - yet I follow you. I cannot be seen in the dark - yet in the dark I am most of all. I repeat your every movement, but never say a word. When you die, I go away with you. What am I?'"

Akmal jumped up, opened his mouth - Yusuf slapped his hand again, this time, evidently, for prompting.

"A shadow," I said.

Hasan roared with laughter and clapped his hands - the sack of cinnamon, it seemed, stayed with him again, since he had not expected to lose this time either.

And Kemal, who sat at the edge of the carpet with a piece of charcoal in his fingers, suddenly said - quietly, distinctly, in his own language, one short sentence. Everyone turned to him. Kemal almost never spoke at the table.

Ferhat did not translate at once.

"He says," said Ferhat slowly, "that Hasan Amca is wrong. That there is a shadow which does not go away when a man dies. It simply waits for him to return."

Silence hung over the carpet. Hasan stopped laughing. Grandmother behind the curtain stopped singing - I had not even noticed when she began.

Suleyman slowly stretched out his hand and laid it on the head of his youngest grandson - as then, at the gate. He said nothing. He only held his hand there, for a long time, and Kemal sat motionless under it and looked at me.

* * *

Before bed, already in the dark, Akmal came running to me from the roof - dishevelled, out of breath, with a face on which hurt was mixed with alarm. He tried for a long time to explain something with gestures, until at last I understood: one of his doves - the white one, his favourite, with feathered feet and a fan tail - had not returned from the evening flight. She had flown off with the others, circled over the roofs - and then broken away from the flock and flown on alone. South. To where we had come from today. Toward Sultanahmet.

"She will come back," I told him, not knowing why, so confidently. "She simply flew off to see someone off."

Akmal did not understand the words. But, it seems, he understood the intonation - because he sniffed, nodded and went off to sleep, looking back.

Late in the evening, when Hasan, puffing, had rolled off home, and the women had cleared the dishes, Ferhat came out to me on the balcony. Sat beside me on a low stool. He was silent for a long time, looking at the dark city, where here and there lights still burned, and at the strip of water where lanterns twinkled on the masts.

"Are you afraid?" he asked at last.

"Of whom? The man in grey?"

"No." Ferhat shook his head. "Of yourself."

I found nothing to answer.

"Father once said to me," Ferhat went on, not looking at me, "before he left for Crete. I was ten. I asked him: 'Are you not afraid of the sea?' And he said: 'The sea - no. The sea is just water. I am afraid of the man I become when I look at the water for a long time. He knows more than I do. And sometimes it seems to me he wants to go back to where I came from.'" Ferhat paused. "I did not understand then. And now I look at you - and, it seems, I do."

"Grandfather says," he added a moment later, "that in our family it has been believed from time immemorial that every person has a shadow walking beside him through all his days - not the one the sun casts, but another, a true one. Usually it is invisible. But sometimes, if a man stands on the threshold of something important, this shadow becomes visible for an instant - through the eyes of those near him, or his own."

"And if this shadow is trying to come inside?" I asked quietly, not quite understanding myself where the question came from.

Ferhat gave me a long, attentive look in which alarm was mingled with something like sympathy.

"Then, perhaps," he answered, "it wants you finally to let it in."

He rose, stood beside me a little longer, laid his palm on my shoulder - for a moment, lightly, as one lays a hand on a brother's shoulder - and wished me good night.

At the door of my room, in the corridor, on a mattress spread across the threshold, Orhan was already lying. On his back, hands behind his head, with a heavy knife in its sheath on his chest. His eyes were open. Seeing me, he grunted something short and displeased and turned on his side, face to the wall, making it clear that he did not intend to talk.

"He says," Ferhat translated in a whisper from behind my back, "that if you snore, he will cut your throat before your shadow does."

I laughed. Orhan, not turning, snorted into the wall. But he did not put the knife away.

I did not fall asleep at once - and when I did, my sleep was troubled, full of fragmentary, elusive images: now the stone plate from the chest, now the voice of the monk from that earlier delusion, now - for some reason - a face painfully familiar, which I had never managed to make out properly since the very first morning in this city. I woke abruptly, with a jolt, in that sudden waking which comes not from the natural course of sleep but from something outside that has intruded into it uninvited.

The room was dark - darker than it should have been with the shutters, usually ajar, admitting at least a little street light. I lay motionless, listening to the silence, and did not at once understand what exactly had woken me: not a sound, no - rather a change in the air itself, the same heaviness that had rolled over me on the night of the paralysis, only now - now I could move. I could sit up on the couch, could turn my head, could make out in the darkness by the very window a motionless, tall silhouette.

This time it did not stand on the border of the street, did not melt into the shadow of the alley at the first glance. It was here - in my room, on this side of the windowsill, which it had never before dared to cross.

I froze, feeling my heart pound somewhere at my very throat, but fear - real, paralysing fear, such as had possessed me on the night of the vision - there was none. There was something else: recognition, as sharp and inexplicable as that which had overtaken me before the chest in Sultanahmet, only now it concerned not the carving on the metal but the silhouette itself standing before me in the dark.

"Who are you?" I whispered, and my voice sounded quieter than I had intended.

The figure did not answer - at least, not in words. It inclined its head slightly, with the gesture of a man listening to something important coming from afar - and in this gesture, for all its strangeness, for all the unlikely height of the silhouette, there was something strangely personal. Not a threat. Not an attack. Something far closer to the way one looks at a man one knew before he knew himself.

I slowly rose from the couch - my legs held unsteadily, but held - and took a step forward, to where the darkness thickened. The figure did not retreat.

"You were at the window before," I said, still in a whisper, as though a loud voice might destroy the fragile balance of the moment. "In the square. Here, on the balcony. And on that night when I could not move."

Silence.

"What do you want?"

The answer came not as sound, not as voice - in the same way the figure had spoken to me on the night of the paralysis: a low, resonant vibration, which echoed not in the ears but somewhere deeper, under the ribs, where the stones responded when they grew warm. The words, if they were words, I did not understand - the same alien, gliding language - but this time there was in them none of the former condescending patience that I had felt on the night of the first close encounter. There was something else. Something distantly resembling - I would not dare assert it with certainty even now, recalling that night by daylight - regret.

I stretched out my hand. I do not know what made me make that gesture - despair, or recklessness, or simply a wish at last to touch something definite in this sea of indefiniteness that had surrounded me since the very first morning in Constantinople. My fingers passed through the darkness, meeting no resistance - but at the very moment when my hand should have touched the silhouette, the pouch at my belt flared with heat, so sudden and strong that I involuntarily cried out, jerking my hand back and at the same time pressing my palm to my side.

The figure flinched - for the first time in all our meeting something like movement, like reaction, flickered in its immobility - and in the next moment it began to retreat. Not to dissolve, not to melt into the air as it had done before, but precisely to retreat, step by step, toward the window, as if the heat of the stones caused it a discomfort that was easier to disregard than to endure.

"Wait," I said, no longer in a whisper, stepping after it. "Wait, please!"

But it was too late. On its way to the window the silhouette paused for an instant by the door - where above the lintel hung Suleyman's new nazar - and, as it seemed to me in the darkness, touched the blue glass eye. Did not strike it. Did not tear it down. Touched it - the way one touches something familiar in passing. Gently. And then the silhouette melted in the window opening - did not jump out, did not fly away, but precisely melted, as though the very darkness outside the window were for it not a barrier but a natural element, to which it returned as easily as a fish returns to water.

I remained alone, standing in the middle of the room with a burned - though, examining the belt later, I found not a trace of a burn, not the slightest damage to skin or cloth - side, and feeling how a strange, inexplicable mixture of terror and something to which I did not dare give a name spread through my whole body. Not joy. Not relief. Rather - longing. The same longing that had overtaken me on the first morning by the fountain, at the memory of a face I had never managed to make out.

As if I had just lost someone I had known far better than I was ready to admit.

I went to the window, looked out into the darkness of the alley. No one. Only the silence of a sleeping city, and a distant, barely audible splash of water somewhere in the port.

Then I turned to the door. Suleyman's nazar - huge, dark blue - hung above it whole, without a single crack. But the nail beneath it no longer bent: the thread was stretched evenly, as if the blue eye had become lighter. Or as if someone, passing by, had lifted it - and hung it back.

I quietly opened the door. Orhan was asleep on his mattress across the threshold - now truly, soundly, boyishly with his mouth a little open. But his right hand in his sleep gripped the hilt of the knife so that the knuckles whitened. And his face was turned not to the wall, as before, but toward the window of my room - toward where the guest had just gone. As though he, without waking, were looking after him.

I did not wake him. I stood over him, then took my woollen blanket from the couch and covered Orhan with it - the night was cool. He muttered something in his sleep and turned onto his other side, still not letting go of the knife.

I was about to step away from the door when something alighted on the windowsill behind my back - with a soft flapping of wings, a quiet cooing.

A white dove. With feathered feet and a fan tail. The one that had not returned to Akmal. She sat on the windowsill, exactly where the tall grey silhouette had just melted in the dark, and looked at me with a round orange eye, her head tilted to one side. Calmly. Without fear. As if she had just flown in with someone from afar - and that someone had told her to stay here.

I stretched out my hand. She did not fly away. She allowed herself to be taken - warm, light, with a quickly beating heart under the feathers. I carried her to the roof, to Akmal's dovecote, and set her on a perch beside the others. The doves cooed, shifted, accepted her. In the morning, I thought, Akmal will find her here - and will decide she came back by herself.

Let him decide. Someone must believe that everything that flies away comes back by itself.

Going down from the roof to the room again, I sat on the windowsill, took the leather pouch from my belt and untied it with trembling fingers. The stones still kept remnants of warmth - not that scorching, feverish heat that had seized them at the moment of my touching the silhouette, but a soft, almost soothing warmth, such as a stone has that has lain all day in the sun. I held them in my palm a long time, listening to the silence of the night, as though hoping they would speak to me themselves, without the mediation of dreams and visions.

They were silent. But this silence, for the first time in all the time I had carried them, did not seem empty to me. There was in it something like waiting - patient, almost fatherly, like the silence of a man who knows the answer but is firmly resolved to let his interlocutor reach it himself.

I recalled Ferhat's words about the shadow walking beside a man through all his days - a true shadow, different from the one the sun casts. Perhaps, I thought, looking at the barely smouldering stones in my palm, the point was not at all to guess who or what stood behind that silhouette. Perhaps the question had been put wrongly from the very beginning. Not who are you, but how do I know you - that was what should have been asked, had I had enough courage to say it aloud at the minute when the figure still stood opposite me in the dark.

I could not fall asleep again that night. I sat by the window until dawn, looking at the gradually brightening sky, and thought - no longer of whether this figure really exists, because of that I no longer doubted for a second - but of why, against all common sense, against the fear that still lived in me, a part of me - the very part that longed for a nameless face and inexplicably reached out to every glimmer of recognition - a part of me wanted him to come back again.

Wanted to understand, at last, whose face hid behind that impenetrable grey shadow.

And why, when I had stretched out my hand to it, for one brief, dizzying instant it had seemed to me that I was reaching not toward a stranger but toward my own reflection - only lagging behind me by one last, insurmountable moment.
