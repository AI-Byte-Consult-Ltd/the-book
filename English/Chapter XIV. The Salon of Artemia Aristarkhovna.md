Chapter XIV
The Salon of Artemia Aristarkhovna

"They found you by the grille," said Konstantin. "Sitting on the floor. With your back to the wall. With the candle burnt out in your hand and your eyes open. You were looking at the grille and smiling, mon cher. I swear to you - smiling. As if you had just heard the funniest joke in the world."

We stood at the window in the small drawing room while footmen arranged chairs for the evening gathering, and Konstantin spoke in an undertone, not looking at me, as though telling of the weather. Four days had passed. Four days I had lain in bed under the supervision of Doctor Strauch, who felt my pulse, shone a candle into my eyes, made me count to a hundred and back, and each time, having finished the examination, wrote something in a little black notebook which he showed to no one.

"Who found me?" I asked.

"I did," said Konstantin. "Who else. You did not cry out. But I heard you all the same." He paused. "Do not ask how. I do not know myself. I sat in the hall, Lizonka was playing her third romance, and suddenly my head began to ache. Here." He touched his temple with his fingers. "As if someone had called. I rose and went. Straight to the left wing. I did not even take a candle. I walked in the dark and knew where to go. As though someone were leading me by the hand."

"And the General?"

"The General came after." Konstantin at last turned to me, and his face was unusually grave. "He went faster than I, though he is sixty. He knew where the grille was. Knew the way better than I. And when he saw you by the wall - he was not surprised. Do you understand? Not surprised. He simply bent down, lifted you like a child, and said..." Konstantin faltered.

"What did he say?"

"'Again.'" Konstantin looked me straight in the eyes. "He said: 'Again.' Quietly. To himself. And then he saw that I was near, and did not say another word."

I was silent. Outside the window it was growing dusk; in the garden, among the rose bushes, the stooped figure of Stefan with a lantern flickered - the gardener was making the rounds of his domain before night, as the housekeeper Agafya Tikhonovna made the rounds of the locks.

"I am not asking you what was there," said Konstantin, turning back to the window. "You will tell when you wish. Or will not. I want to know only one thing." He paused. "Have you come back? Whole? Are you - you?"

The question was so strange that I laughed. But Konstantin did not laugh.

"I am," I said. "Who else."

"Good," he said quietly. And, after a pause, added in his former voice, with his former mockery: "Because your aunt has today gathered half the province, and if you faint again in the middle of the lecture, Kutasova will decide it is from love of Lizonka. And I shall have to fight a duel with you. And I, mon cher, shoot abominably."

* * *

The salon of Artemia Aristarkhovna opened at eight. By half past eight the small drawing room - which was called small only in comparison with the great ballroom, and in fact held some forty persons without any crowding - was full.

Candles burned in tall candelabra and in crystal girandoles on the walls, reflected in the dark windows and in the mirrors so that it seemed the drawing room continued beyond the glass into infinity. It smelled of wax, perfume, hot chocolate and the asters which Stefan had brought from the garden and set in Chinese vases in the corners. By the fireplace tea was being poured; at the piano someone of the young set was idly picking at the keys; in the pier between the windows a footman in white gloves held a tray of punch, and Konstantin had already twice managed to approach it.

My aunt received her guests at the doors - in dark-blue velvet, with the same unchanging string of pearls, erect, calm, with a smile which I had seen on her a thousand times and which today for some reason seemed to me a mask. Twice during the evening I caught her gaze turned on me - long, anxious - and both times she at once looked away.

The guests were mostly familiar. The General - in an armchair by the fireplace, with a cup of tea and with that impenetrable expression with which he had no doubt listened in his time to both diplomats and Turkmen elders. Evdokia Gerasimovna - beside him, in the next chair, in a silver-grey gown and a little hat on which perched a whole stuffed hummingbird with an emerald throat. Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich - already telling someone about the Caucasus. Vera Anatolievna - quiet, in lilac. Lieutenant Alexei - hovering around my aunt. Doctor Strauch - in a corner, with a cigar which he was not allowed to smoke in the drawing room and so simply held in his fingers like a talisman. Nadezhda Porfirievna Kutasova with Lizonka - of course in the front row, nearer to the General.

But there were new faces too.

"Who is that?" I whispered to Konstantin, nodding at a lady by the window.

"Ah," Konstantin winced. "That is Madame Blanchard. Joséphine. A medium. She came from Paris last winter and now turns tables in all the drawing rooms of the capital. They say that at Princess Velskaya's - no, not that one, the other - she summoned the spirit of the late husband, and he asked that it be conveyed that he had hidden the money in an old boot. The boot was found. The money - no." Konstantin snorted. "But the princess is delighted all the same."

Madame Blanchard was a lady of indeterminate age - somewhere between forty and eternity - in a black silk gown, with a high coiffure in which two heron feathers were stuck, and with so many bracelets on her wrists that at every gesture she jingled like the bell on a goat's neck. Her face was pale, powdered, her eyes heavily lined with kohl and looked so intently, so languidly, that it seemed she saw behind your back someone whom you did not see.

"And that one?" I nodded at a young man by the piano - thin, pale, with long, shoulder-length fair hair and a velvet jacket of the kind artists wear.

"A poet," sighed Konstantin. "Apollinary Treskov. Writes verses about death, the moon and unrequited love. Mainly the latter. His mother is a distant relation of Evdokia Gerasimovna, which is why he is tolerated. Tonight he will read. Prepare yourself."

"And this one?"

By a column, with a cup of chocolate, stood a lady in a lavish lilac gown, with an enormous ostrich-feather fan and with such a greedy, tenacious, all-seeing expression on her face that I involuntarily stepped back a pace.

"Oh!" whispered Konstantin with unfeigned horror. "That is Olga Kirillovna Shelestova. The most dangerous person in the province. Do not go near her. Do not look at her. Do not breathe in her direction. She knows everything about everyone - who with whom, who to whom, who how much. And what she does not know she invents, and in such a way that afterward it proves true. They say three weddings and two duels in the province in the past year were her doing."

It was too late. Olga Kirillovna had already noticed me, snapped her fan shut with a ringing click and sailed toward us like a frigate under full sail.

* * *

"Ah! There he is!" she sang out, stopping before me and running her eyes over me from head to foot as though buying a horse. "Our poor invalid! They say you fainted in the left wing? At night? Alone?" She lowered her voice to a theatrical whisper. "They say you were found by that very grille!"

"They say," I said.

"And what were you doing there, in the left wing, at midnight, eh?" She playfully poked me in the chest with her folded fan. "Ah, youth! Do not answer, do not answer, I understand everything. There, they say, the White Lady walks. Did you see her?"

"No."

"A pity!" Olga Kirillovna sighed. "And my cousin, Varvara Petrovna, stayed here in the time of Artemia Aristarkhovna's late husband and swears she saw her. In the mirror. A white dress, she says, and no face." She waved her fan. "However, Varvara Petrovna after her third glass of cordial sees even worse things."

Konstantin beside me made a strangled sound.

"And tell me," Olga Kirillovna bent to me, and her eyes suddenly became sharp as needles, "tell me, my dear, one thing. I have come to Artemia Aristarkhovna's evenings, God help my memory, for twelve years. Twelve years, every season. And, kill me, I do not remember when you appeared here. You were not - and here you are. And when, from where..." She narrowed her eyes. "You are her nephew, are you not? Son of a sister? Or of a brother? She had, I know for certain, neither sisters nor brothers..."

The world around me swayed for an instant. The candles trembled. I opened my mouth - and understood that I did not know what to answer. I truly did not know. A sister? A brother? I had never asked. My aunt had never said. When had I appeared here? I remembered this house, these corridors, this drawing room - but when had I first seen them? Yesterday? Ten years ago? Always?

"Olga Kirillovna."

My aunt's voice sounded behind my back - even, quiet, with that special velvet steel at which servants' knees gave way.

"You, my dear, forget a great deal," said Artemia Aristarkhovna, taking me by the arm. "For example, that I forbade your cousin Varvara Petrovna to appear in this house after she broke the family mirror in the hall. And that you yourself promised me at Easter to return a French novel which, if I am not mistaken, still lies with you." She smiled - dazzlingly, mercilessly. "And my nephew has been here as long as I remember myself. Is that enough?"

Olga Kirillovna crimsoned to the roots of her hair, muttered something about a novel, opened her fan and sailed off to the column.

My aunt did not release my arm. Her fingers gripped my elbow so hard that it hurt.

"Do not listen to her," she said quietly, not looking at me. "Never listen to those like her. They trade in others' secrets because they have none of their own."

"Auntie," I said just as quietly. "Is it really so that you had neither sisters nor brothers?"

The fingers on my elbow loosened.

"The lecture is beginning," said Artemia Aristarkhovna. And walked off to her chair without once looking at me.

* * *

Professor Sventsitsky proved nothing like what I had imagined.

I had expected a dry, grey, tedious scholar in a pince-nez - and onto the dais by the fireplace stepped a man of about fifty, sturdy, sunburnt, with a short, peasant-trimmed beard, in a frock coat that had clearly seen more roads than drawing rooms, and in boots polished but trodden down. His eyes were clear, light and merry, and his hands large, calloused, with an ink stain on the middle finger of the right. At his belt, under the frock coat, hung a canvas bag from which the edges of notebooks stuck out, and from time to time he slapped it with his palm as one slaps the flank of a faithful dog.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he began, and his voice was loud, sonorous, accustomed to shouting down village assemblies and fairground noise. "I thank Artemia Aristarkhovna for the invitation. I warn you at once: I do not know how to give lectures. I know how to tell. For thirty years I have walked through villages, from Arkhangelsk to Bessarabia, from the Carpathians to the Urals, and written down what old people tell me. Tales. Omens. Charms. Songs. What our learned colleagues call superstitions, and I call - the memory of the people, which it keeps longer than any archive."

"Memory or folly?" Doctor Strauch asked loudly from the corner, twirling an unlit cigar in his fingers.

A titter ran through the drawing room. Sventsitsky, not in the least abashed, smiled into his beard.

"Ah, Karl Fyodorovich! I have heard of you. You, they say, treat with cold rubdowns and Karamzin." The titter grew louder. "I shall tell you thus, Doctor: folly does not live a thousand years. Folly dies together with him who devised it. But what an old woman in the Olonets province tells her grandson word for word just as her great-grandmother told her grandmother - what has outlived the Tatars, the Time of Troubles, Peter and the French - that is no longer folly. That is something that very much wished to be preserved. The only question is - why."

"And why?" asked the General from his chair. Quietly, but all turned to him.

Sventsitsky looked at the General - attentively, a little longer than courtesy required.

"Because, Your Excellency," he said, "a people, like an individual, sometimes remembers what has not happened to it. Or what happened so long ago that it itself no longer knows whether it truly was. And this memory it hides in tales. Because in a tale one can tell the truth, and no one will call you mad."

The General slowly lowered his cup to the saucer. I saw his hand for a moment lie on the pocket of his uniform - the one where the wooden raven lay.

* * *

Sventsitsky spoke long - and no one grew bored.

He told of house-spirits, who in every province are fed differently: in one - with porridge at midnight, in another - with milk by the stove, in a third - with tobacco, because the house-spirit there, they say, smokes. Of the bath-spirit, who does not like it when people bathe after the third steaming, and may strangle for it. Of mermaids, who in Trinity week come out of the water and swing on birches - and who, as one old woman on the Volga told him, are not drowned maidens at all but "those who are not yet born and do not know whether they wish to be born." Of werewolves in Polesie. Of vampires in Bessarabia. Of "pledged dead" - those who died not their own death and cannot find the way to the other world, and so wander between.

"Between what?" asked Lizonka Kutasova. Timidly, almost in a whisper - and at once blushed, because everyone turned to her.

"An excellent question, mademoiselle!" Sventsitsky beamed. "The best question of the whole evening. Between what do they wander?"

He held a pause - long, as only good storytellers know how to hold.

"You see," he said, "among our people - not among bookmen, not among priests, but among simple folk, among whispering grannies, shepherds, healers - there is a very old notion of the world. So old that no one can any longer properly explain it. It was explained to me by one old healer near Vologda - half blind, toothless, they said she was past a hundred. She said thus: 'The world, dear sir, is not one. There is Yav - that is what is seen. Where we sit, where bread grows, where the sun rises. And there is Nav - that is what is not seen. Where the dead are, the unborn, dreams, those who were and will be. And between them - a threshold. Thin, thin. Like the film on milk. Most live on one side and know nothing of the other. But there are those who stand on the threshold. One foot here, the other - there. They are few. They were always few. It is hard for them. They do not remember where they came from, because they came from that side. And do not know where they are going, because they go to that.'"

The drawing room fell silent. So silent that I heard the candle gutter in the candelabrum nearest me.

"Two worlds," said Sventsitsky, as though summing up, as though in passing, as though it were the most ordinary phrase in the world. "Yav and Nav. And a man who stands between two worlds is neither a saint nor a madman. He is simply a man who has lost his way. Or found another."

Two worlds.

I do not know what happened to me at that moment. These words - the simplest, most ordinary words - entered me as a key enters a lock which you have never seen but which, it turns out, was all this time within you. Somewhere in the very depth something turned. Clicked. And for one brief, dazzling moment it seemed to me that I heard - not with my ears but with something beneath my ribs, on the left - the distant peal of bells. And a long-drawn call pouring from a slender tower. And the rustle of sand under someone's steps between stone columns.

I clutched the back of a chair. Konstantin beside me instantly straightened.

And at that very moment something fell in the doorway of the drawing room. Loudly, with a rustle and a thud.

All turned.

In the doorway stood Stefan. The gardener. In a coarse canvas shirt, with earth under his nails - he must have been carrying fresh flowers into the drawing room, but the basket of white roses had fallen from his hands and lay now on the parquet, spilling the flowers in a fan at his feet. He did not bend to pick them up. He stood motionless and looked - not at Sventsitsky. At me.

Long. Silently. With such an expression as I had never seen on any human face: not fear, not surprise, but something like recognition and grief at once. Thus one looks at a grave long sought and at last found.

Then he bent, gathered the roses into the basket - slowly, one by one - muttered something in his own language and left without another word.

"What is the matter with him?" Kutasova asked loudly.

"Bulgarians," the Colonel shrugged. "A superstitious people. The professor must have reminded him of something from his village."

Sventsitsky, however, did not shrug. He gazed after Stefan with lively, keen interest. And then turned his gaze to me - and in his eyes flickered something that made me uneasy.

* * *

Then there was an interval, and tea, and pastries, and conversations, and Konstantin thrusting into my hand a glass of punch with the words "drink, you are pale as the White Lady" - and, at last, the General, whom the ladies, as always, begged to tell "something interesting from his travels."

"Only not about the Caucasus!" Kutasova implored. "Mikhail Ivanovich has already told us about the Caucasus. Twice."

"Thrice," the Colonel corrected with dignity.

"About Japan, Pavel Nikolaevich!" Lizonka suddenly asked - and was herself frightened by her boldness. "They say you were in Japan. Is it true that they eat raw fish there?"

The General looked at her - and, to general surprise, smiled. Truly, warmly, without his usual icy courtesy.

"True, mademoiselle. They do. And very tasty, if one does not think what one is eating." He set down his cup. "Very well. About Japan."

He told how, as a no longer young colonel, he had been attached to the embassy which the Sovereign sent to Japan on the frigate Pallada, and afterward on the frigate Diana - to negotiate about trade and the border on Sakhalin and the Kurils. How for long, for months, they stood in Japanese harbours, and the Japanese officials - in wide silk garments, with two swords at the belt, with shaven foreheads and little tufts of hair on the crown - came every day to the frigate, bowed to the floor, drank tea, spoke civilities and decided nothing.

"A Japanese will never say 'no' to you," said the General. "He will say: 'It is very difficult.' And he will go on saying 'it is very difficult' for half a year. I knew in China a mandarin who did the same. It sometimes seems to me that they studied under one teacher."

"And the Japanese themselves - what are they like?" asked Vera Anatolievna.

"Polite, madam. Polite so that it frightens. Clean - in every village they have a bath, and they bathe every day, in very hot water, all together, men and women, and see nothing wrong in it." A shocked whisper passed through the drawing room, Kutasova covered Lizonka's ears with her fan. "Their houses are of wood and paper. Walls of paper, imagine. They slide apart. And the floors are covered with mats, and to enter a house in shoes is the same as for us to spit on an icon."

"And what happened to the Diana?" asked Konstantin. "I heard she perished."

The General's face changed.

"She perished, Lieutenant." He paused. "We stood in the harbour of Shimoda. In the morning - quiet, sun, not a cloud. And suddenly - the earth shook. Right beneath the water. The frigate rocked like a boat. And then the sea..." he fell silent, choosing words, "the sea went away. The water withdrew from the harbour as if someone had pulled out a plug. We saw the bottom. Fish thrashing on the sand. And then the water returned. A wall. Higher than the masts. It passed through the town and carried it off as a wave carries off children's sand castles. Houses, temples, boats, people. Our Diana was spun in the harbour like a chip forty-two times - I counted the turns, because I could do nothing else. Forty-two. She was smashed on the bottom, her rudder torn away, her hull stove in."

In the drawing room no one breathed.

"We lost almost no one," said the General quietly. "By a miracle. The Japanese - the very ones who for half a year had said 'it is very difficult' - pulled our sailors from the water with their bare hands. And afterward, when the Diana had sunk completely, they helped us build a new schooner. In a little village by the sea. Japanese carpenters and our sailors - without a common language, by gestures - built a ship in three months. We named it after that village. On it part of ours returned home."

"And you?" asked Evdokia Gerasimovna. So quietly that, it seems, only I and the General heard.

"And I stayed another half year, Dunya," said the General just as quietly, forgetting, it seems, that before all one must say 'Evdokia Gerasimovna.' "To sign the treaty. We signed it in a temple. A small, wooden one, on a hill above the harbour, where debris from the wave still lay about." He paused. "There was an old monk there. He watched us sign, and then came to me and gave me this."

The General reached into an inner pocket - not the one where the raven lay, but another - and took out a small paper object. A paper crane - white, worn, yellowed with time.

"He said through the interpreter: 'The Japanese believe: if one folds a thousand cranes, one wish will come true. I have folded nine hundred and ninety-nine. The last I give to you. Your wish will come true when you find one who will fold it back into a sheet.'"

"Back into a sheet?" Konstantin repeated. "How is that?"

"I do not know, Lieutenant." The General put the crane back in his pocket. "For twenty years I have carried it and do not know."

* * *

"And Australia?" asked the poet Treskov suddenly, who until then had been silent and gazing at the ceiling with the air of a man composing an elegy. "Have you been to Australia, Your Excellency? There, they say, everything is the reverse. Summer in winter. Beasts that hop. Black swans."

"I have," said the General. "Briefly. On our corvette, some ten years ago. We called at Melbourne."

And something in me quivered.

I could not have explained what. The word "Australia" - merely a word, the name of a distant, almost fabulous land at the other end of the world, where convicts are exiled and where, they say, gold is found right in the ground. I had never been there. I could not have been there. And yet at the sound of this word something in my chest contracted - sweetly and painfully, as it contracts at a smell remembered from childhood, though one does not remember the childhood itself.

"In Melbourne we were received as if nothing had happened," the General went on. "Although, as we learned afterward, there had been a panic in the city before our arrival: they thought the Russians had come to attack. English newspapers wrote for a whole week that our corvette was the first ship of an enormous fleet which was about to bombard the city." He smiled into his moustache. "And we had come for water and coal. And so that the sailors could stretch their legs."

"And what is it like there?" I asked. My voice sounded strange, hoarse.

The General looked at me - attentively, with a long look.

"It is bright there," he said slowly. "That is the first thing I remembered. The light there is different. Not as with us and not as in Asia. White, brilliant, as if the sky were higher. The sky is enormous. The stars are foreign: the Great Bear cannot be seen, but there is the Southern Cross, four stars like a kite. The city is new, as though built yesterday: wide streets, stone houses, banks with columns - gold there, gold, everyone goes mad over gold. And beyond the city - eucalyptus forests. The trees shed bark, not leaves, and stand white, bare as bones. And smell - smell so that the head spins, of honey and medicine at once. And at night in these forests a bird laughs."

"Laughs?" Lizonka repeated.

"Laughs, mademoiselle. Like a man. Loudly, rollingly, to tears. The first time I seized a pistol - I thought it a drunken vagrant. I was explained - a bird. The kookaburra. It laughs at dawn and at sunset. The natives say - it wakes the sun and sees it off."

Laughs at dawn. Wakes the sun.

The baboons of Thoth raise their hands to the rising sun. The thought came from nowhere - I did not know what the baboons of Thoth were, I had never heard of them. The thought flickered and vanished like a spark.

"And the natives?" asked Sventsitsky, leaning forward with lively interest. "I have read that the Australian savages have remarkable beliefs."

"Remarkable, Professor." The General turned to him. "I spoke with one old man. Through an interpreter - a shepherd who had lived among them twenty years. The old man was quite black, grey, almost naked, with a spear, with white stripes of paint on his chest. He told me that his people have lived on this land since the beginning of time - from that time which they call by a word which our shepherd translated as 'the Dreaming.' When the ancestors sang - and from their songs mountains, rivers, stars appeared. And that the land still remembers these songs, and if one knows how to sing, one can walk the path of the ancestors across the whole country - from sea to sea - not knowing the way, simply following the song."

"Songlines," murmured Sventsitsky, feverishly writing something in his notebook.

"And he also said..." The General fell silent.

"What, Your Excellency?"

"He looked at me and said that I was not the first white man to come to him and ask about this. That before me another had come. Long ago. Alone. Without a ship. 'He came out of a dream and went into a dream,' said the old man." The General paused. "The interpreter laughed, said the old man had lost his wits. And the old man looked at me and said: 'When he comes again - tell him that the land remembers his song.'"

In the drawing room it grew quite still.

The General was looking not at Sventsitsky, not at the ladies. He was looking at me.

"I, of course, passed on nothing of the sort to anyone," he said, looking away. "To whom should I have passed it? The old man was raving."

I sat without stirring. And in my chest everything contracted and contracted - that sweet, aching feeling, as for a home in which you have never been.

* * *

"Enough of frightening stories!" My aunt rose and clapped her hands, and her voice sounded a little louder and a little sharper than was needed. "Gentlemen, charades! We play charades. Konstantin, you set the first."

All stirred, brightened, shifted their chairs. Charades were the favourite game of my aunt's evenings - with them, it seems, all the salons in the province ended. Konstantin, not in the least abashed, came out to the middle of the drawing room, cleared his throat, thrust a hand into the lapel of his uniform like Napoleon in portraits, and declaimed:

"My first syllable is a preposition, you find it oft at the start of words where road or path is told.
My second is on bull and aurochs and all the goat-kind.
And the whole - across it one steps into the house from the porch."

"Porog - the threshold!" cried Lizonka, clapping her hands. "Po-rog!"

"Bravo, mademoiselle!" Konstantin bowed to her so low that Kutasova beamed.

"Too simple, Lieutenant," snorted the poet Treskov. "You yourself gave it away - 'from the porch.'"

"I did it on purpose," Konstantin answered imperturbably. "So that Mademoiselle Kutasova should win. She deserved it."

Lizonka blushed. Kutasova looked at Konstantin with new, appraising eyes. Konstantin, catching that look, paled and hastily retreated to me.

"I seem to have just made a fatal mistake," he whispered. "Now she will hunt me."

"Threshold," I said quietly, not knowing why.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Threshold. Thin as the film on milk. One foot here, the other there.

Next, Olga Kirillovna set one - and her charade, of course, proved spiteful, ambiguous and obviously hinting at someone in the drawing room, at which Lieutenant Alexei turned purple and went out "for air." Then the Colonel set a charade about a cannon, which no one guessed because the Colonel muddled the syllables. Then Doctor Strauch set one in German, and only Evdokia Gerasimovna guessed it.

And then Sventsitsky rose from his place.

"Permit me too," he said. "Only mine is not a charade. Mine is a riddle. A folk one. It was set to me by that same old woman near Vologda. She said that it is set only once in a life - to him who stands on the threshold." He smiled. "I, I confess, never found the answer. Perhaps one of you will."

And he pronounced it slowly, in a chant, in the old manner:

"'Two stones at the threshold lie: one - like night, the other - like snow. One holds the door, the other shows the way. Who lifts both - he will go home. Only his home is not here and not there.'"

I did not hear a cup break. I only saw: on the carpet by Artemia Aristarkhovna's chair lay shards of thin porcelain, and across the pile a dark stain of tea was slowly spreading. My aunt sat perfectly still, looking at Sventsitsky, and her face was white as paper.

The General rose from his chair.

"Professor," he said evenly, "it is late. Artemia Aristarkhovna is tired. I thank you for a splendid evening."

It was not a request. It was an order, given in the tone in which orders are given on a battlefield. And all - all forty persons in the drawing room - understood it.

* * *

The guests dispersed hurriedly and awkwardly. Kutasova led Lizonka away, glancing back at Konstantin. Olga Kirillovna left last, with so pleased a face as though she had been given a whole casket of other people's secrets. Madame Blanchard, the medium, who had been silent all evening, suddenly stopped at the very doors, turned - and looked at me with her kohl-lined eyes. Then she came up - quickly, jingling her bracelets.

"Mon petit," she said quietly, in French, so that none but I should hear. "I am a charlatan. Everyone knows it. I move tables with my foot and rap on them with my heel." She bent closer. "But in thirty years I have learned to see one thing. With some people someone always stands behind them. With you - he stands. Tall. Grey. He stood behind you all evening. And when the professor said 'two worlds,' he laid a hand on your shoulder."

She straightened, jingled her bracelets - and went off without waiting for an answer.

I stood in the middle of the emptied drawing room. Footmen were putting out the candles. Konstantin was speaking quietly with the General about something by the fireplace. My aunt had already gone to her rooms.

"Young man."

I turned. Sventsitsky stood at the door - in an overcoat, with his bag over his shoulder, a cap in his hand.

"Forgive me if I upset your aunt," he said. "I did not know. Truly, I did not know." He paused, looking at me with his clear, merry eyes which now were not merry. "Allow me to ask. Where are you from?"

I opened my mouth.

"I..."

And fell silent. Just as I had been silent today before Olga Kirillovna. Just as I had been silent four days ago before the monk in burgundy who asked what year it was.

Sventsitsky nodded - slowly, without surprise.

"You do not remember," he said. Not a question. "So I thought."

He put on his cap.

"I am staying at an inn in the district town," he said, already at the very threshold. "I shall remain until the end of the week. If you wish to talk - of Yav and Nav, of stones at the threshold, of anything - come. I, you know, have for thirty years been writing down tales. Sometimes it seems to me that it is not I who seek them." He smiled. "Sometimes it seems to me that it is they who seek one to whom to tell themselves."

He left. The door closed behind him. A footman put out the last candle at the exit.

Konstantin came up to me and silently stood beside me. We stood in the half-dark drawing room, where it smelled of wax and cooling tea, and looked at the dark window, beyond which in the garden Stefan's lantern was still flickering - he was for some reason walking round the spot where under the drawing-room windows the white roses grew. In circles. Again and again.

"He said 'two worlds,'" said Konstantin quietly, not looking at me. "You went white. Your aunt broke a cup. Stefan dropped the roses. The General drove out the professor." He paused. "Mon cher, I am, of course, a simple lieutenant. But even I see: everyone in this house knows something. Everyone but you and me."

"Perhaps we know too," I said. "Only do not remember."

Konstantin turned and looked at me - long, gravely.

"If you remember," he said, "tell me first. All right? Not your aunt. Not the General. Me."

"All right."

"Do you promise?"

I remembered a riddle that I had never heard: "What can be broken even without touching it?"

"I promise," I said.

He nodded, clapped me on the shoulder - a little longer and a little more lightly than friends clap - and went away, leaving me alone in the dark.

And outside the window, in the garden, Stefan's lantern went on circling and circling around the white roses - as though the gardener were looking for something in the dark. Or guarding something.
