Chapter VI
Cracks in the Earth

The earth of the Valley was now cracking not only along the distant canals, where water had reached only sparingly even in the best of years - the cracks were creeping across the most fertile fields, those that had fed the capital since time immemorial. I had seen this with my own eyes the day before, touring the nearer lands in a closed litter: the black, once fat soil of the Delta had become covered with a net of narrow, deep fissures into which peasant children, with a grim, un-childlike curiosity, lowered sticks without finding the bottom.

Three years without a full flood. Three years during which the god of the river, whose name even a Pharaoh was not permitted to utter in vain, had kept silence, in spite of all the sacrifices, all the prayers, all the processions that we sent to his banks with an enviable, desperate regularity.

This morning the audience hall was full as it had not been full for a long time - not with dignitaries, not with ambassadors of distant lands, but with simple petitioners, whose faces, scorched by the sun and wasted by hunger, merged for me into a single, faceless mass of grief. The guards could scarcely hold back the pressure at the entrance; the scribes barely managed to enter names and complaints into scrolls whose length grew with every hour.

Among the scribes seated at the foot of the throne I noticed a new one - a youth whom I had not seen in this hall before. Slender, dark-eyed, with a fine, boyishly grave face, he wrote faster than the rest and, unlike his elder colleagues, kept raising his head, peering into the faces of the petitioners so attentively as though committing to memory not only their words but their grief. When our eyes met by chance, he became confused, hastily lowered his gaze to the papyrus - and I, not knowing why, held my look on him a little longer than simple curiosity required.

"Who is that youth?" I asked in an undertone of the senior scribe standing beside me.

"Iti, my Pharaoh. Junior keeper of the Library. Scribes are short today, so he was sent to help. Clever, though at times he thinks too much for his years."

I nodded without answering and returned to the petitioners.

"My Pharaoh," the first of them addressed me, an old man with hands trembling whether from age or from hunger, "the water in the canal of Hapi-Re dried up at the very beginning of the season. The cattle are dying. The children..."

He did not finish, but there was no need to finish. I saw that phrase, that unfinished thought, written on hundreds of faces around me that morning.

"Grain from the royal granaries will be distributed to your village before sunset," I answered, trying to make my voice sound firm, though inwardly I already knew: those granaries emptied far faster than we managed to replenish them, and my promise today would save one village at the price of tomorrow's hunger in another.

The old man fell on his face, muttering thanks which, I confess, I did not wholly deserve - and he was at once replaced by a woman of middle years leading by the hand a gaunt boy of about seven, whose eyes, too large for his wasted face, looked at me with that calm, reproachless submission that frightens far more than any tears.

"My Pharaoh," she said, "my husband died a month ago on the construction of the new canal. A wall collapsed. I have nothing to feed my son with."

"Your name will be entered in the lists for the widow's ration today," I answered, and Iti at the foot of the throne hurriedly scratched across his papyrus, "and for the boy, when he grows up, a place will be provided in the temple school of scribes, if he so wishes."

She bowed so low that her forehead touched the scorching flagstones of the floor, and the boy, still holding her hand, repeated his mother's bow with that gravity with which children repeat adult gestures, not fully understanding their meaning but sensing their weight.

After her came a flax merchant whose warehouses, by his account, had stood empty for the third month running; then a whole crowd of fishermen from the southern shoals, where the shallowed river had left their boats stuck in the cracked silt; then a priest of a minor temple, seeking funds to repair the collapsed roof of his sanctuary, who let slip, as if in passing, that the High Priest had already refused him such a request. I listened, promised, ordered - and with each new voice realised ever more clearly that the royal granaries, however full they had seemed at the beginning of this ill-fated year, were by no means bottomless, and the number of those in need, it seemed, would know no end.

Toward noon the keeper of the royal treasury took the floor, a spare old man named Nebamun, who had served at court more than one Pharaoh before me.

"My Pharaoh," he said in an undertone, drawing me aside for a minute from the general hall, "I am bound to report: if grain is distributed as we are distributing it now, the granaries of the capital will be empty by the beginning of the next flood at the latest. If, of course, the flood comes at all this year."

"Do we have a choice?" I asked, though the question, in essence, required no answer.

"We do," answered Nebamun with that bitter frankness which only servants who have served long enough no longer to fear disfavour allow themselves. "To feed the people now and risk famine in spring. Or to cut the distribution now and risk revolt today. Neither of these paths, my Pharaoh, can be called good."

I dismissed him without an answer, because I had, to tell the truth, no answer either for him or for myself.

* * *

Senusret found me in the interval between audiences, in the shade of the colonnade of the inner courtyard - the stocky, no longer young commander of the army, whose face, scored by years of campaigns, seldom expressed anything but a restrained soldierly impassivity. Today, however, that impassivity seemed to cost him some effort. He was old enough to be my father and had served the throne longer than I had sat upon it - and in all that time he had never once given me cause to doubt his loyalty, however uncomfortable his frankness sometimes proved.

"My Pharaoh," he said, bowing exactly as far as custom required, "I have tidings which I would rather not bring you on such a day. But to postpone them further would be, I believe, a still greater mistake."

"Speak," I said, feeling the weariness of recent weeks settle on my shoulders like one more sack of grain - of the kind my subjects now so lacked.

"Among the standard-bearers and junior commanders a murmur is growing," he said, lowering his voice, though around us at that time there was not a soul but two guards at the far entrance, too far away to catch a word. "Not open mutiny - nothing of the kind, at least for the present. But ever more often speeches are heard that the gods have turned away from the throne, and not from the Temple. That if the High Priest stood nearer to power, this drought might perhaps have receded sooner."

"Do these speeches arise of themselves," I asked, though the answer was in essence clear to me in advance, "or are they made to arise?"

Senusret was silent, weighing his answer with the caution with which a soldier weighs an accusation that can cost someone his head.

"I have no direct proof, my Pharaoh. But the coincidence is too consistent to be called accidental: these speeches are loudest in those detachments that stand closest to the temple lands. Where Nefer-Ka's priests dispose of the bread and beer that go into the warriors' rations."

I involuntarily clenched my teeth. Hunger struck not only the peasants - it struck the army too, and a hungry army, as any commander knows, listens not so much to the oath given to the throne as to the one who first offers it bread.

"What do you propose?" I asked.

"To feed the detachments from the royal granaries, not the temple ones," Senusret answered without delay, as if the answer had been ready in him long before I could ask the question. "It is costly. But cheaper than a mutiny in the midst of a drought."

"Do it," I said. "Quietly, without heralds, so that Nefer-Ka should not have time to interpret it as an admission of his own rightness."

Senusret nodded curtly, giving himself, it seemed, full account of how thin was the edge along which I now had to tread in every decision - and had already turned to go when I called after him once more.

"Senusret. You have served the throne for more than a decade. Tell me honestly - do you believe that the gods have truly turned away from us? Or is it only the language in which people are accustomed to speak of their own fear?"

He looked at me with a long, impenetrable look - the look of men who have seen enough in their lives to unlearn surprise at such questions.

"I believe, my Pharaoh," he said at last, "that a hungry man will always find someone to blame. Whether the gods fear us as we fear their silence - that I cannot know. But people need a culprit before they need an answer. And until they appoint you the culprit, I shall perform my duty with all the zeal of which I am capable."

This answer, for all its soldierly frankness, did not bring me the comfort on which I, perhaps, had secretly counted.

* * *

Toward evening of that same day tidings spread through the whole city: the High Priest had announced a great prayer service at the walls of the main sanctuary, to which all were summoned who wished to come, regardless of rank - an event so unusual for so closed a place, ordinarily open only to the chosen, that the news flew around the capital faster than the ink could dry on the temple scrolls.

I went there myself, secretly, unrecognised, accompanied only by two trusted guards dressed in plain clothes - a decision for which my counsellors would have scolded me without mercy had they learned of it in advance.

The square before the sanctuary was filled with people so densely that to squeeze to the very steps was not possible even for me, forced to content myself with a place in the back rows of the crowd, among simple townsfolk, whose faces shone with that peculiar, desperate hope that seizes people ready to believe in anything, so long as they might believe in something.

Nefer-Ka appeared on the upper step of the sanctuary in full vestments, with a staff crowned by the image of the sacred Eye - and when he spoke, his voice, caught by the echo of the stone walls behind him, spread over the crowd with that resonance that seems to the crowd itself almost a divine omen.

"My children!" he cried, stretching out his arms to the sky, cloudless and pitilessly clear. "The gods hear you. The gods see your sorrow. And the gods - through me, their unworthy servant - promise you: this silence will not be eternal!"

A murmur ran through the crowd - whether a groan of relief or a sob - and dozens, if not hundreds of hands shot up toward the sky, as though straining to seize that promise before it should slip away. Beside me an elderly woman in a ragged kerchief burst into loud weeping, repeating the name of the deity again and again, as though repetition alone could hasten the fulfilment of what had been promised. The man on my left, judging by his calloused palms and the smell of fish - one of those very southern fishermen who had stood that morning in the audience hall - clenched his fists until his knuckles whitened, and whispered something to his neighbour at which both nodded grimly.

"But," Nefer-Ka went on, and his voice at that word fell almost to a whisper, forcing the crowd to lean forward lest it miss a sound, "the gods demand not only sacrifices on the altar. They demand that the scales be set right again. That those who stand closest to the heavens should again receive a voice commensurate with their nearness to the divine."

I froze, standing in the back rows of that faceless, hope-seized crowd, and felt the cold - the very one that had overtaken me more than once in recent months - creep from somewhere beneath my ribs, on the left, to where, it would seem, there should have been no wound at all.

The scales. He had already spoken to me of scales - alone, in my own chambers, in the same insinuating voice. Now he was repeating the same words to thousands.

Nefer-Ka named not a single name directly. He uttered not a word of direct reproach to the throne. And nonetheless everyone in that crowd - I saw it in their faces, in the whispers that rolled in a wave from the front rows to the back - understood his words exactly as he had calculated: the gods are angry not without cause. The gods are angry because power is distributed wrongly.

"Hear that?" the fisherman muttered to his neighbour, loudly enough that I, standing a pace behind, made out every word. "There it is. It's not the Pharaoh who will save us. The priests. I always said so."

His neighbour nodded in agreement, and I, listening to this exchange of words between two strangers, judged it a far more eloquent testimony to Nefer-Ka's success than any reports of my own counsellors.

Already leaving the square, I looked back one last time - and saw the High Priest, having finished his speech and receiving the bows of the crowd, turn his head almost imperceptibly to the south, to where, many days' journey up the river, the columns of Luxor stood. A fleeting gesture, accidental, perhaps meaning nothing at all. And yet it lodged in me like a splinter which I could not extract until night.

* * *

Returning to the palace, I found at the entrance to the inner chambers an unexpected visitor - a young priestess, whose name, as it turned out later, was Khemu, waiting for me with that nervous, ill-concealed agitation which usually betrays a person who has resolved on an act frightening to herself.

"My Pharaoh," she whispered, bowing hurriedly, almost clumsily, "forgive my boldness. But I must tell you something which I cannot entrust to anyone else in the whole Temple."

I stopped, feeling the weariness of that long, exhausting day recede for a moment before a strange, inexplicable wariness that seized me at the sight of her face.

"Speak," I said quietly, glancing round to see whether there were any superfluous ears nearby.

"Not here," she whispered, casting a quick, frightened glance toward the colonnade. "Not now. But soon, my Pharaoh. What I have seen... what has been revealed to me... concerns you directly. The columns, my Pharaoh. I see columns - and darkness between them. And I fear that if I do not tell you everything in time, something will happen that can no longer be put right."

Before I could ask even one of the dozen questions crowding on my tongue, she vanished into the darkness of the corridor as hurriedly as she had appeared - leaving me standing alone beneath the stars, feeling the cracks that had split in this year the very earth of the Valley begin, it seemed, to spread through a foundation far more fragile than any soil.
