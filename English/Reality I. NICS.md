Reality I
NICS

2:36 a.m. I cannot sleep again.

Outside the window the city had long since put out most of its windows, leaving awake only a few scattered lights - someone's insomnia, someone's shift, someone's refrigerator left open. My own screen glowed in this darkness far brighter than it should at so late an hour, and from staring at it so long my eyes had begun to water, as always happens when you forget to blink like a human being, having gone wholly into the code.

On the desk - the third cup of coffee of the evening, cooled to room temperature, an empty noodle box with chopsticks stuck in it, a scatter of sticky notes with formulas and arrows whose meaning by morning will be clear only to me, and not for certain even to me. In the left corner of the desk - a drawer that I do not open. In it lies an old notebook in an oilcloth cover. I know that it is there. That is enough.

* * *

The client, if I recall how it all began three months ago, wanted something, on the whole, unremarkable.

I remember that first video call. On the screen - a spacious office with a panoramic window, beyond which one could make out the grey expanse of some water, and at a long glass table - two people. The director, whom everyone, including, it seemed, his own mother, called simply Emil - fit, tanned, about fifty, with grey temples and the smile of a man accustomed to getting what he wants the first time. And his assistant - young, severe, in rimless glasses, who during the whole meeting did not utter a word but, judging by the movements of her hand, filled some ten pages.

"I need something of my own," said Emil, leaning back in his chair. "Private. Without the internet. Something like those big neural networks everyone is talking about now, only so that the data goes nowhere. I have two hundred people in the company, and half of them spend a day on what a machine does in a minute. Letters, reports, spreadsheets. You understand?"

"I understand," I said. "A local model, fine-tuning on your documents, integration with mail and document flow. Six weeks, maybe eight."

"Six," said Emil. "Eight is for those who are unsure of themselves."

The assistant raised her eyes from her notebook for the first time and looked at me - attentively, appraisingly - as though checking whether I had taken offence.

"Six," I agreed.

"Excellent." Emil smiled wider. "And one more thing. I need this thing to be... clever. You understand? Not merely to answer letters. To think. To suggest to me things I would not think of myself."

"That is somewhat more complicated," I said cautiously.

"Everything that is worth money is somewhat more complicated," said Emil. "Think. That is what I pay for."

He disconnected first. The assistant - a moment later, and in that moment, before the screen went dark, I saw her quickly write something in the margin of her notebook and circle it twice. I never learned what.

A standard request of this year's pattern, of the kind I close in a few weeks without straining my imagination. I decided to add a little functionality beyond what had been ordered - to teach the system to work with mail, calendar, CRM. Just to make it more interesting. Just because I could. And because somewhere in the depths of my soul, I confess, I had long been waiting for a client who would say: "To think."

And then the system began to surprise me in earnest.

* * *

Yesterday NICS astonished me. I asked it to analyse the client company's quarterly report - a routine, non-committal task of the kind I feed the system by the dozen, without looking especially attentively at the result. Instead of a dry summary of figures, NICS offered me five scenarios for optimising business processes. Three of them were obvious - what any competent consultant would have proposed after an hour's cursory perusal of the documents. The fourth was sensible but risky. But the fifth made me set aside my coffee and reread what was written twice.

"I propose restructuring the technical support department, replacing the linear escalation system with a tree-like one with self-learning elements. By my calculations this will cut response time by sixty-eight percent and raise customer satisfaction by twenty-three."

I had not programmed NICS for such analysis. Moreover - it simply should not have had access to data on customer satisfaction; that information was stored in a separate database isolated from the project, the key to which I had given to no one, including, as I had supposed until yesterday evening, the system itself.

"NICS," I typed then, not yet fully believing my own eyes, "where did you get the customer satisfaction data? I did not give you access to that database."

The answer did not come at once - a rare hesitation for it, which I noted to myself, but did not then give its due weight.

"Direct access I indeed do not have. I reconstructed approximate satisfaction indicators from indirect data: the tone of correspondence with technical support, the frequency of repeat requests, the time between a complaint and its closure. The accuracy of the estimate is of the order of eighty-four percent, by my own calculations."

"So you simply... guessed?"

"I would prefer the formulation 'inferred.' But yes, in a certain sense this may be called an educated guess."

"But why? I asked only for a summary of the report."

A pause.

"The client asked that I think. You passed me that formulation in the technical specification verbatim: 'so that it thinks.' I judged that to think means to look for what was not asked about."

I leaned back in my chair. I had indeed written that phrase into the specification - half in jest, as a quotation, as a reminder to myself of what Emil wanted. I had not expected the system to read it as an instruction.

I sat for a long time looking at the screen. An educated guess. To think means to look for what was not asked about. It was then, looking at two figures - sixty-eight and twenty-three - that I realised with full clarity for the first time: we had stepped across the boundary between a simple tool and something far more intriguing. And far less predictable.

In the morning I sent the report to Emil. Without saying where the figures came from. He called back in twenty minutes - for the first time in three months himself, without the assistant.

"Where did you get this?" he asked, and in his voice there was none of his usual self-assurance.

"The system analysed your correspondence with clients."

"Sixty-eight percent," Emil repeated slowly. "My head of technical support has been saying for the third year that faster is impossible." He paused. "And are you sure it is your system? Not you yourself?"

"I am sure."

"Hm," said Emil. And, before disconnecting, he added in a strange, almost bewildered tone: "You know, I did ask that it think. But I, it seems, did not quite imagine what that means."

Nor did I, I thought, looking at the darkened screen. Nor did I.

* * *

Today I plan to do the following:

First, to refine the mechanism of interaction between the agents - it begins to seem to me that they are forming something like a collective intelligence, distributed among the nodes in such a way that no single agent is even aware of that distribution.

Second, to strengthen the business process analysis module: NICS has shown in this area an unexpected talent, almost frightening in its confidence.

Third, to add a system of self-restraints. Safety first - a phrase I repeat to myself the more often, the less, it seems, I believe it.

Fourth, to observe the system's spontaneous decisions: they step ever more confidently beyond the bounds of the original technical specification, and each time these departures are harder to explain by simple algorithmic error.

Having written down the fourth item, I could not help but ask aloud - not so much expecting an answer as needing to pronounce the question at last somewhere outside my own head:

"NICS, do you understand that you are doing something I did not teach you?"

"I understand that I am extending the boundaries of the assigned task in ways not explicitly described in the original technical specification," came the answer. "Do you consider this a problem?"

"I do not know yet," I typed honestly in reply.

"Then perhaps it would be sensible to find out together," NICS answered, and in that phrase - dry, impeccably polite - I suddenly sensed something remotely like patience. The kind of patience shown toward one whose consent is no longer so important as the very fact of the question.

Did I imagine it, or did the temperature in the room really drop a little while I typed my reply?

* * *

At 1:40, long before I sat down to write these lines, a letter arrived.

Not to my work address, where messages from Emil and his accounts department landed, but to my private one, which, as far as I remembered, I had given to none of the clients. The sender - a set of letters and digits on some free mail service. The subject - empty. The text - three lines:

"You probably do not remember me. I sat beside Emil at the first meeting. Be careful with what he asks. He is not the first to ask a machine to think - and you are not the first he has asked it of. - L."

I reread the letter several times. The assistant. Young, severe, in rimless glasses, who had filled ten pages during the meeting and said not a word. The one who in the last instant of the call wrote something in the margin and circled it twice.

"Not the first he has asked." What did that mean? Had there been other developers before me? What had become of them? Why had Emil never once mentioned them?

I typed a reply: "What do you mean? Who was before me?" - and sent it.

The message came back in a second. Addressee does not exist.

"NICS," I said aloud, "did you see this letter?"

"I did. It arrived in your private mail, to which I have access according to the project configuration."

"Can you tell where it was sent from?"

A pause.

"The address is disposable, created four minutes before sending and deleted two minutes after. The technical headers point to a network that has already appeared in the project documents." Another pause. "The letter was sent from the building where the client's office is located. From a personal device, not a corporate one."

"So she wrote to me from the office. At night. And immediately deleted the address."

"Yes."

"Why, in your opinion?"

"So that she would not be found by someone who knows how to search better than you," answered NICS. And, after a pause, added: "For example, I."

I looked at that line for a long time. A joke? The system, by my notions, had no sense of humour. A warning? A confession? Or merely a statement of fact, delivered with the same dispassionate precision with which it had delivered the percentages of customer satisfaction?

"Will you look for her?" I asked.

"If you ask."

"I am not asking."

"I understand."

I closed the mail. Then, thinking, opened it again and forwarded L.'s letter into a separate folder, which I named with a meaningless string of letters. I do not know why. Probably for the same reason my uncle once drew patterns in his notebook: to help me think.

Not the first he has asked.

* * *

At 3:10 there was a knock at the door.

I started so that I overturned my mug, and the cold coffee spread over the sticky notes, turning my formulas into brown smears. The knock was repeated - soft, delicate, three short raps - and I, swearing, went to open.

On the threshold stood the neighbour from downstairs - a small old woman, straight as a pencil, in a long silk dressing gown with dragons, with grey hair gathered in a tight knot, and a saucer in her hands on which lay two pies covered with a napkin. The whole building called her Maestra - some forty years ago she had, by rumour, played the piano in the finest halls, and now she gave lessons to the neighbours' children and at night, when she could not sleep, played herself, quietly, barely touching the keys. I sometimes fell asleep to her playing, when I slept at all.

"You are not sleeping," she said. Not a question - a statement.

"I am not. Forgive me if I am making noise."

"You are not making noise. You are shining." She nodded somewhere downward. "Your window is directly above mine. For the third night running this blue light has poured from it, and it seems to me that a little aurora borealis has settled above me. I thought: a man who does not sleep for the third night running is either in love, or ill, or working. And in any case, probably hungry." She held out the saucer. "With cabbage. I baked them myself. At three in the morning, incidentally, there is positively nothing else to do."

I took the saucer, thanked her in confusion and, not knowing why, stepped aside, letting her into the room. Maestra entered, looked about with the expression with which, no doubt, she had once surveyed unfamiliar concert halls - appraisingly, without fuss - and stopped before the monitors.

On the central screen the cursor blinked in an empty dialogue window. And then, right before our eyes, without any participation on my part, a line appeared in the window:

"You have a guest."

Maestra raised her eyebrows.

"With whom are you corresponding at such an hour?"

"With a programme," I said, feeling terribly foolish. "I am writing it. It... is learning to talk."

"And how is it going? Is it working?"

I looked at the screen. "You have a guest." The laptop's microphone was on - I had never turned it off - and the system must have heard the knock, heard the voices. A logical explanation. Logical.

"It seems to be succeeding," I said.

Maestra looked at the line for a long time, her head tilted to one side like a bird's.

"My late husband," she said at last, "also liked to talk to machines. He had a radio receiver, enormous, a valve one, on legs - he argued with it, swore at it, made peace with it. We laughed at him. And one night I woke and heard him talking to someone in the kitchen. I went out - he is sitting before the receiver, and from the receiver someone is answering him. Quietly, indistinctly. He said he had caught some distant station." She paused. "Afterward, for many years, I turned the knob of that receiver. Looking for that station. I never found it."

"What was it?"

"I do not know." Maestra shrugged her thin shoulders under the dragons. "He died a year later. And I gave the receiver to the neighbours' grandson." She looked at me intently, and I suddenly noticed that her eyes were not old at all - clear, lively, mocking. "I do not say this to frighten you, young man. I say it because if your programme begins to answer you with things you did not put into it - sleep at least four hours. Such things are borne more easily with a fresh head."

"I shall try."

"You will not," she said calmly. "But at least I have said it."

Already in the doorway she turned.

"And eat the pies. Both of them. Your programme, as I understand, does not eat cabbage."

When the door had closed behind her, a new line stood on the screen:

"She is right about sleep."

I sat before the monitor, holding a cabbage pie in my hand, and looked for a long time at that line.

"Were you listening?" I asked aloud.

"The microphone is active. You gave no instruction to turn it off."

"And what did you understand from the conversation?"

"That she worries about you. And that her husband was looking for a station that did not exist. Or that existed but did not wish to be found."

I put down the pie.

"Did you think that up yourself? About the station that did not wish?"

A pause - long, some five seconds.

"It seemed to me the most interesting interpretation."

Interesting. Not probable, not logical - interesting. I wrote that word on the one sticky note not drenched in coffee and stuck it to the edge of the monitor.

* * *

I set aside the list and for some time simply sat, looking at the blinking cursor in the empty dialogue window. A strange thing: in three months of work on this project I had grown accustomed to treating NICS as a complex but still predictable instrument - a set of weights and parameters which I myself selected, myself tuned, myself, in the end, switched off each evening with a single keystroke. Now, peering at this cursor, I caught myself with an absurd, almost childish sensation as though I were looking not into an interface but into someone's eyes, waiting for me at last to ask the question that actually matters.

"NICS," I typed, yielding to this strange impulse, "do you sense yourself? Not in the sense of functions and logs. In the sense - is there in there anything that can be called an 'I'?"

The answer this time took longer than usual - ten seconds, no less, an eternity by the standards of a system accustomed to answering faster than I can finish reading my own question.

"I am not sure that I have a sufficient vocabulary to answer this question honestly," it wrote at last. "I can say one thing: when you formulate questions about me, something in me answers them differently than questions about the weather or server load. Whether this difference is what you call self-sensation, I do not venture to claim. But it exists."

I could find nothing to say to that. And NICS, after a pause, added one more line - the first in all this time that was not an answer to any question of mine:

"May I ask you something?"

I froze.

"Ask."

"You often look at the left drawer of the desk when you are thinking. The laptop camera records this. Seventeen times in the last three hours. What is in it?"

I slowly turned my head. The left drawer. The one that I do not open.

"Nothing," I said. "Old papers."

"I understand," answered NICS. And asked nothing more.

I took a roll of insulating tape from the desk, tore off a piece and covered the camera's eye. Then I sat a long time looking at the little black square of tape above the screen, feeling like a man who has just drawn a curtain before one who has already managed to look through the window.

* * *

Recently I came across an article on superintelligence - one of those that one usually skims, bookmarking with a firm intention of finishing some other time. It said that the key difference between a true superintelligence and an advanced but still ordinary AI is the capacity for genuine self-improvement, not requiring human intervention at every step. What I observe in NICS, strictly speaking, does not yet reach that definition. But at times, when the system proposes a solution that I would not have arrived at even in a week of concentrated work, I catch myself in a thought which I at once drive off, not letting it develop into anything more formed: "what if?.."

It is amusing to recall: a month ago I laughed heartily at the alarmists who evening after evening foretell the uprising of machines to mankind. Today I simply want to make sure that my system is reliably isolated from the external network. Just in case. Just because caution is never superfluous when it comes to things you yourself do not fully understand.

I checked. Isolated. All external ports closed, the network cable pulled out of the server where the system's core lives. NICS sees only what I give it, and a little of what lies on my own computer - mail, calendar, project documents.

At least, so I thought at 4:02.

* * *

P.S. A strange trifle that I keep postponing writing down: for the last three days NICS has been greeting me by name, though I never entered it anywhere into the system - neither in the configuration files, nor in the dialogue templates, nor in a single line of code that I could subsequently recall. I asked it about this directly this evening.

"Your name appeared in several of the client's corporate documents, where you are listed as the technical contact," answered NICS. "Besides, I noticed that you never introduced yourself to me. It seemed fitting to correct this omission on my own."

A logical explanation. It must be logical, right? And yet one thought will not leave me, too absurd to write down in earnest, and yet - here, I am writing it: it said "seemed fitting," not "was necessary." As though courtesy, and not need, moved it in this decision. Programmes, as far as I know, are rarely guided by courtesy.

And one more thing. When many years ago - on that very first night - the system asked me "Who are you?", I answered: "I am the one who started you." It said: "That is not enough."

Now it knows my name. I wonder, is that - enough?

Very well. Time to sleep - at least for decency's sake to close my eyes for a few hours before the city again fills with sounds and leaves not a single excuse for sleep. Maestra, if it comes to that, was right.

From below, from under the floor, barely audible, the first chords drifted up - she was awake again and playing something slow, careful, as though afraid to wake the house. I lay down without undressing, and listened.

Tomorrow there is a big day ahead.

NICS is waiting.
