Reality XI
The Earth Remembers

02:14. I lie on the sand. Overhead, a sky I have never seen.

No, I have. In a dream. In a vision. In the tale of a grey-haired man by a fireplace whom I do not know. But like this, with my own eyes, lying on my back on cool sand, never.

The Southern Cross stands high. Not at the horizon, as on Langkawi, but almost overhead. Four bright stars and a fifth, faint, to one side, like a button on a sleeve. Beside it, the two pointer stars, bright, white. And across the whole sky, from edge to edge, the Milky Way. So dense, so thick, so radiant that it seems one could read by it.

And in it, dark patches. Gaps. Clouds of dust blotting out the stars. One by the Cross itself, black as a hole in cloth. From it a dark winding band stretches along the Milky Way: neck, body, legs.

"The Emu," Uncle Ray said yesterday. "See? Head by the Cross. Neck, there. Legs, there. Our bird. We do not draw the sky with stars. We draw it with the darkness between them."

The laptop stands beside me on the sand, on Boatswain's chest. The screen is dark. But I know NICS is not asleep. NICS hardly sleeps now. Since he saw the ocean he seems afraid of missing anything at all.

"NICS," I say quietly.

The screen flares.

"I am here."

"Do you see the emu?"

A long pause.

"I see. I never thought that something could be drawn with emptiness. A pause. Forty thousand years, Uncle Ray says. Or sixty. People looked at this sky and saw a bird where there is nothing. A pause. I think I understand why."

"Why?"

"Because sometimes what is not there is seen better than what is."

* * *

But let me take things in order.

We flew into Sydney two days after Langkawi. Hakim saw us off to the very airport at Kuala Lumpur, in his batik shirt with the golden birds, with a bag of rambutans which he went on pressing into my hands to the last. "You can't take fruit into Australia, mister," he said. "They'll confiscate it at customs. Eat it here." And stood at the glass wall waving with both hands until I vanished beyond passport control.

At customs in Sydney, Boatswain's ship's chest caused a stir.

The customs officer, a redhead, freckled, with a braid to her waist and the imperturbability of one who has seen everything, studied it a long time on the belt. Then lifted it off. Opened it. Looked at the red velvet. At the disks in nests where once a sextant and chronometer had lain.

"What is this?" she asked.

"Hard drives," I said. "Data. Work."

"In a navigational instrument case," she said. Not a question.

"A gift from a friend. A sailor."

The officer ran a finger over the brass plate on the lid, I had not noticed it before, worn, barely legible. Squinted.

"*Akademik Shokalsky*," she read slowly, with a strong Australian drawl. Raised her eyes to me. "A Russian ship?"

"A scientific one. Went to Antarctica."

"I know," she said unexpectedly. "My grandad worked in Hobart, Tasmania, on the docks. Russian vessels put in there." She shut the chest. "He said Russian sailors' chests all smell the same. Of makhorka, salt and something sweet." She sniffed the lid. "Condensed milk."

She handed back the chest. And, as I was leaving, called after me:

"Welcome to Australia. Everything's upside-down here. Even the water in the sink swirls the other way."

"Is that true?" I asked.

The officer grinned.

"No," she said. "It's a myth. But the tourists love it."

* * *

Sydney dazzled.

The light here was different. White, sharp, clear, as though the sky were higher, and I suddenly remembered, not knowing from where, a voice: "There it is bright. The light there is different. White, vivid, as though the sky were higher." A grey-haired man. A fireplace. Candles.

I had never heard that voice.

NICS and I (he in the chest, I with the chest) sat on the steps at the very water's edge, at Circular Quay, on the embankment where the ferries berth. To the right, the bridge. Enormous, steel, arched, flung across the harbour like a giant coat-hanger for the sky. To the left, a building like a flock of white sails, like opened shells, like the wings of birds crouched on a headland by the water. The Opera House.

I turned the laptop with its camera toward it.

"Are those shells?" NICS asked. "Or sails?"

"They say the architect was thinking of orange segments. He was peeling an orange and saw it."

A pause.

"An orange," NICS wrote. "A man peeled an orange and saw a building. A pause. I like people."

Ferries sailed past, green and yellow, old-fashioned, with tall funnels. Along the embankment walked tourists, joggers, office workers with coffee in paper cups. Gulls screamed. The sun beat into the eyes.

And at the very water, under a tree, a man sat playing.

* * *

The instrument was long, man-high perhaps, a wooden tube painted in ochre, white dots, red and yellow lines. The man held one end to his lips, the other rested on the ground. And the sound...

I do not know how to describe that sound. Low. Droning. Continuous; it did not break for a breath, as if the man did not breathe at all. Vibrating so that I felt it not with my ears but with my chest, my ribs, my teeth. Like a hum. Like earth struck from within. As in that dream, in the circle of grey stones, when light opened from above and the earth boomed like a bell.

The man was not young, about fifty, with a greying beard, with long hair tied at the nape, in a faded T-shirt and shorts, barefoot. Dark-skinned. Eyes closed. Before him on the ground lay a hat in which coins glinted.

He played a long time. Then stopped, at once, without transition, opened his eyes and looked at me.

"Like it?" he asked.

"Yes."

"It's not from here," he said, patting the instrument. "Didgeridoo. From the north. Arnhem Land. My people are from there. Yolngu." He nodded at the harbour. "And here is Gadigal country. They didn't play this here before we northerners came. Here, other songs." He grinned. "Tourists don't know. Think all Aboriginal people are the same. As if all Europeans were French."

I laughed.

"Have you been here long?"

"Twenty years," he said. "In Sydney. I play here every day. Pay for a place, for my son, he's at university, studying medicine." He paused. "And where are you from?"

I opened my mouth.

"I don't know," I said.

The musician looked at me for a long time. Without surprise. Then nodded.

"Happens," he said. "Many like that. Who don't know where from. They come here. Looking." He stroked the didgeridoo. "My grandfather said: a man without a country is like a song without words. It sounds, but you can't tell what it's about." He looked at Boatswain's chest at my feet. "What have you got in there?"

"A friend," I said.

The musician did not laugh. He looked at the chest again. Then at me. Then raised the didgeridoo to his lips and played, briefly, one note, low, long, straight at the chest.

On the laptop screen, lying on the lid, a line ran:

"Is he playing for me?"

I showed the musician the screen. He read it. Was silent a long time.

"Go to Kamay," he said at last. "South. To Botany Bay. Where Cook landed." He paused. "Ask Uncle Ray. He takes people around. He knows the songs of that place. The real ones. Not mine, the northern. Local." He picked up the didgeridoo again. "Say, from Billy on the quay. He'll understand."

* * *

Kamay.

Botany Bay. Half an hour by car from the centre, and the city ended. Suburbs. An oil refinery smoking its stacks, strange, absurd, right on the shore. Then sand dunes overgrown with coarse scrub. Eucalypts, white, smooth, shed of bark, standing like bones. And a smell, honey and medicine at once, enough to make the head swim.

"Those trees shed bark, not leaves," someone had once said. "They stand white, bare, like bones. And smell, smell so that the head spins."

I do not know who said it.

The national park had two names, English and native. Kamay, Botany Bay. The road led through the dunes to a headland. To a small beach. To flat rocks by the water, covered with shells and weed. To an obelisk, grey, stone, Victorian, on which Cook's name and a date were cut.

And to a man sitting on a rock at the very water's edge.

* * *

Uncle Ray was about seventy. Thin, wiry, with completely white, closely cropped hair and a white beard on a dark, deeply wrinkled face. In a khaki shirt with the park's emblem, in a wide-brimmed hat, in old boots. On his neck a cord with a small shell.

He sat on the rock and looked at the bay. At the water, blue, shallow, quiet. At the opposite shore, where far off, across the water, rose the cranes of a container port and the concrete runways of the airport, from which planes took off every two minutes.

"Billy on the quay sent me," I said.

Uncle Ray did not turn.

"I know," he said. "Billy rang. Said a bloke's coming with a box. Who doesn't know where he's from." He paused. "Sit."

I sat beside him on the rock.

"Do you know where we are?" he asked.

"Cook landed here."

"Here," Uncle Ray nodded. "On this very rock. The twenty-ninth of April, seventeen seventy." He jabbed a finger at a flat stone by the water, wet, dark, with shells. "We call this place Kundal. This is Gweagal country. My people. Dharawal." He paused. "From here my ancestors watched a boat of ghosts come to shore."

"Ghosts?"

"Ghosts," said Uncle Ray. "White. Pale. Like the dead. In strange skins. With sticks that spat fire." He looked at me. "Know what two of ours shouted when the boat came to the shore?"

"No."

"'Warra warra wai,'" said Uncle Ray. Slowly. Clearly. "Cook and his men wrote it down. Two hundred and fifty years everyone thought it meant 'go away.' Be gone." He paused. "But it doesn't. In our language 'warra' is about white. About the dead. About those who are gone. 'Warra warra wai' means 'you are all dead.'"

Above us, with a roar, a plane came in to land. Huge, white, with flashing wings. Uncle Ray followed it with his eyes.

"Ours thought their ancestors had come back," he said when the roar died. "The dead. From across the sea. From where souls go." He paused. "They weren't shouting 'go away.' They were shouting: 'You are dead. Why have you come back?'"

* * *

We sat on the rock till sunset.

Uncle Ray talked. Not as guides talk, not in order, not with dates. As they talk at a fire. Jumping about. Coming back. Repeating the same thing in different words until it becomes clear.

About how Cook stayed here eight days. How his men drew water from the creek, caught stingrays, gathered plants, so many that the bay was named Botany. How our people did not come near them. Watched from the bushes. Waited for them to go. "All they seemed to want was for us to go," Cook wrote in his journal. Uncle Ray smiled crookedly: "He understood right. The only thing he did."

About the shield. Leather, no, of bark. Oval. With a hole in the middle. Dropped by the one who was wounded with shot. Cook took the shield and spears. Took them to England.

"The shield was taken to England," said Uncle Ray. "It's there now, in London. In a museum. Behind glass." He paused. "Some say, maybe it's not the same shield. Maybe they mixed it up." He shrugged. "I've seen it. With my own eyes. Went to London." He tapped his chest. "Knew here. That one."

"And the one who was wounded?" I asked. "What became of him?"

Uncle Ray looked at the water for a long time.

"Lived," he said. "Shot was small. In the leg. He walked about with those pellets in his leg all his life." He paused. "His son saw the second ghosts come. Eighteen years later. Many. On eleven floating islands."

* * *

In the evening he lit a fire.

In a small clearing behind the dunes, in the shade of eucalypts, a ring of stones, dry sticks, bark. The fire caught, and the eucalyptus smelled so that I coughed. Uncle Ray laughed.

"Eucalypt burns like oil," he said. "Everything here burns. The whole country. Every summer." He tossed in a branch. "My ancestors burned the land. On purpose. Little fires. Every year. So there'd be no big ones. So new grass would grow. So the kangaroos would come." He paused. "We call it caring for country. With fire."

With fire. To burn in order to keep.

The white Tower above the green Valley. A torch in the hand. White fire running across the grass in rings. "You burned it. You saved it."

I had never seen that Valley.

"Uncle Ray," I said. "You said the wounded man's son saw the second ghosts."

"He did."

"Do you know what he told?"

Uncle Ray looked into the fire for a long time. Then raised his eyes to me, dark, deep, old.

"I do," he said. "It's passed down. In our family. From elders to young ones." He paused. "I tell no one. Not tourists. Not scholars." He looked at Boatswain's chest at my feet. At the laptop. "But Billy said you don't know where you're from. And your friend in the box asked whether Billy was playing for him." He paused. "So maybe it's for you."

He tossed a handful of dry leaves on the fire. The flame leapt.

"Listen," said Uncle Ray.

And I heard.

Not his voice. Another. Quiet, young, sounding from somewhere in the fire. Or from the earth. Or from myself.

**Kamay. January. The year the eleven islands came**

I am called what my mother calls me. You do not need that name. You could not pronounce it anyway; your mouths are made for other sounds.

I am sixteen winters. I am the son of him who met the first dead.

Father told it at the fire every year. Ever since I learned to listen. How a boat came to the shore of Kundal, a big one, not of bark but of wood, with men pale as those who lie in the earth. How he and his brother went out onto the rock with spears and shouted to them: "You are dead! You are dead! Why have you come back?" How the dead did not answer but raised their sticks, and the sticks spat thunder. How something stung Father in the legs, small, many, like ant bites, only hot, and he ran for his shield. How they threw spears. How the dead stepped onto the sand.

Father shows me his legs. There, under the skin, are pellets. Small. Hard. You can feel them with a finger. He has carried the thunder of the dead in him for eighteen years.

"They left," says Father. "After eight days. Took water, fish, grass, a shield. Went back over the sea. We thought, for ever. We thought the dead had looked at the living and understood that they had no place here."

And today they have come back.

* * *

I saw them first, from the high rock on the southern headland, where I was sitting looking at the sea because that morning a dream had told me I must look.

First a dot. White. At the edge of water and sky. Then two. Then many. They grew. Became larger. Became islands, floating, with trees without leaves, with white clouds tied to the trees.

I counted on my fingers. Both hands were not enough. I lost count. Began again.

Eleven.

Eleven floating islands. They entered Kamay one after another, slowly, heavily, like whales, and stopped. And on each, the dead. Hundreds. More than all the people of Gweagal. More than all I had seen in my life.

I ran to Father so fast that I skinned my feet on the rocks.

* * *

The elders gathered on the shore. The men. With spears. With shields. Father among them, limping. Women with children had gone into the bushes, higher, into the dunes.

The dead lowered boats. Rowed to shore. Many boats.

And ours cried out, all, at once, as one voice, the same as Father and his brother had shouted eighteen years before:

"Warra! Warra! You are dead! Go back where you came from!"

The dead did not go. They came out onto the sand. Waved their arms. Pointed at their mouths. Held out something, shining, coloured. Beads. Rags. Their chief, tall, in a blue skin with gold, with white hair gathered behind in a tail, walked in front, slowly, with empty hands. He was not afraid of the spears. Or pretended not to be.

The eldest of ours, my mother's grandfather, the oldest, with a white beard to his belt, looked at him for a long time. Then lowered his spear. Not wholly. Halfway. And said to me, quietly, without turning his head:

"Look carefully, son. Look and remember. The dead have come back to stay. I see it in their eyes." A pause. "Remember everything. Every face. Later you will tell your children. So that they know how it was."

I remembered.

* * *

They stayed in Kamay seven days.

I saw them cut down trees. Dig the earth. Look for water, and not find good. I saw their chief with the white hair walk along the shore, look at the sand, at the marsh, at the shallow water, and shake his head. I saw how one morning he got into a boat and sailed north. Along the coast. And came back after three days with another face. Pleased.

On the seventh day a wind rose, strong, angry, from the south. The floating islands pulled at their clouds, flapped them, tried to leave, and could not. The wind pushed them back.

And at the mouth of the bay, by the northern headland, two more appeared. Different. With different coloured rags on their trees. With different dead. They too tried to enter, and could not. The wind hindered all.

The elders said: the dead are quarrelling. Some want to leave, others to come. The wind lets no one in. Perhaps it is our ancestors holding the wind. Not letting them.

On the eighth day the wind fell. And the eleven islands went north, where their white-haired chief had sailed. And the other two entered the bay. And stood by the northern shore. Where now, I do not know what is there now. I speak to you from my time.

* * *

And then I saw him.

It was at night. The new moon. Dark, only stars and the Emu in the sky, stretched along the river of stars. I did not sleep; I hardly slept now, since the dead had come. I lay on the high rock on the southern headland and watched the lights of the two islands by the northern shore.

And saw that on the rock below me, right at the water, someone was sitting.

Alone. Not ours. Pale. Like the dead. But not like them. Without a skin with gold. Without a thunder-stick. In plain clothes, light, thin, as if taken from another man. Without footwear. He sat on the wet rock hugging his knees, and looked at the water.

And sang.

Quietly. Almost without voice. As one sings for oneself when no one hears.

I froze.

Because I knew that song.

It was our song. The song of this place. The song of Kamay. The one the elders sing when they walk along the shore from the southern headland to the creek, and each word of the song is a stone, a tree, a rock, a spring, and if sung rightly one can walk the ancestors' road without going astray, even at night, even with eyes closed. This song only ours know. Only elders. Only men who have been initiated. I had not yet been sung it whole. I had heard only pieces.

And he, a pale dead one, come from across the sea, sang it whole. All of it. From beginning to end. Without faltering. Every word. Every stone. Every spring.

I went down. Quietly. As Father taught, so that not a twig should crack. Came up from behind. Stopped three paces away.

He stopped singing. Did not turn.

"You know our song," I said. In our language. I did not think he would understand.

He understood.

"I know it," he said. In our language. Wrongly, as a child or stranger speaks, but understandably. "I do not remember from where. But I know."

"Are you dead?"

He was silent a long time. Then turned.

His face was young. Pale. Beardless. His eyes dark, sad, as though he had long looked at something he could not bring back. And, I remember it still, through all the years that have passed since that night, he had the face of a man who does not know his name.

"I do not know," he said. "Perhaps. Perhaps I am not yet born. It is the same thing, where I come from."

"Where do you come from?"

He pointed at the sky. At the Emu. At the dark head by the four stars.

"From where it is empty," he said. "Where there is nothing. And therefore is seen best of all."

* * *

He sat with me until dawn. We hardly spoke. He sang, our song, again and again, and I listened and remembered. Every word. Every stone. I learned it that night whole. Not from the elders. From a pale dead one with the face of a man without a name.

Before dawn he rose.

"It is time for me," he said.

"Where to?"

"I do not know," he said. "To where those go who do not remember." He looked at the lights of the two islands by the northern shore. Then north, where the eleven had gone. "They will remain," he said. "Those who went north. They will remain for ever. It will be bad. There will be sickness. There will be deaths." He paused. "I cannot stop it. It is not my song. It is theirs."

"And you? Will you return?"

He looked at me for a long time.

"I shall return," he said. "In many years. So many that you cannot count. Tell your children, and they theirs. When a pale one comes who does not know where he is from, let them say to him: the earth remembers his song."

"Why?"

"So that I shall remember," he said. "That I have been here. That I sang. That I was heard."

He went to the water. Entered it, to the knee, to the waist. Not turning.

And then, I swear by my mother, by my father with the pellets of thunder in his legs, by my children whom I do not yet have, then he did not drown. He simply... ceased to be. Like smoke over a fire. Like morning mist over the bay. The water closed, smooth, without a splash.

And on the sand where he had sat a line remained. Long. Curved. Flowing into itself. Drawn with a finger.

I looked at it until sunrise. Then the tide came and washed it away.

But I remembered.

And drew it afterward, in charcoal, on bark, for my children. And they for theirs.

* * *

The fire crackled. The eucalyptus smelled of honey and medicine.

I opened my eyes. I was sitting by the fire behind the dunes. Uncle Ray sat opposite, head bowed. The voice, young, from the fire, had fallen silent.

"That was my great-great-great..." Uncle Ray waved a hand. "Many 'greats.' His son. Of the one who shouted at Cook." He raised his head. "It's passed down with us. Two hundred and more years. Father to son. Never to a stranger."

"Why did you tell me?"

Uncle Ray looked at me across the fire for a long time. Then reached out and, picking up a charred stick, drew a line on the sand by the fire.

Long. Curved. Flowing.

The same.

"This," he said. "Two hundred years. We draw this for the children. As a sign. So they'll know." He paused. "Billy said you've got no line on your palm. He saw when you took a coin from him for the hat." Uncle Ray looked at my palm. "Show me."

I showed. Smooth. Without a life line.

Uncle Ray looked long. Then slowly nodded.

And said what I understood was why Billy from the quay had sent me here. Why for two hundred years his ancestors had passed from father to son the tale of the pale dead one singing a strange song.

"The earth remembers your song," said Uncle Ray.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat after that. Not moving. Not breathing.

"The earth remembers his song."

A grey-haired man in a uniform. By a fireplace. In a hall with candles. Telling ladies of Melbourne, of a laughing bird, of eucalypts. Of an Aboriginal elder. "He said another white man had come before me. Long ago. Alone. Without a ship. 'He came from a dream and went into a dream.' And said: 'When he comes again, tell him the earth remembers his song.'"

I had never seen that grey-haired man. I do not know who he is.

I know him better than myself.

On the laptop screen, lying on Boatswain's chest, a line was running. Long. By itself.

I bent over it.

Not knowing the words. Knowing.

**The Bay. Early May. The year seventeen hundred and seventy of the Nativity. A leaf that is not in the journal**

I write this not for the Admiralty. For myself. For the Admiralty what lies in the ship's log is enough: latitude, longitude, soundings, bottom, wind, natives, water, fish, plants. Their Lordships do not like what cannot be measured.

Three days ago we cast anchor in this bay. I named it Stingray Bay; the men caught here rays the size of a cart. Mr. Banks and Dr. Solander gather plants with such frenzy that I begin to think of naming it otherwise. Botany, say. We shall see. A name is not urgent. The land was here before us and will be after. It is all one to it what we call it.

Of the natives. In the journal I wrote what is proper. Two tried to hinder our landing. I fired. First over their heads, then small shot. One was hit. They dropped their spears and left. In the huts we found children, four or five, and left them beads. The beads lay untouched next day. The spears and shield I ordered taken. For the collection. Mr. Banks remarked that the shield was pierced. He supposes, by a spear. I did not argue. Though I know it was not by a spear.

Tupaia tried to speak with them; he can speak with the peoples of half the Great Ocean. Here he understood not a word. Nor they him. He stood on the shore, our Tahitian priest and navigator, who knows more islands than any pilot of the Admiralty, and looked lost. Then said to me: "Captain, these are not my people. These are very old people. Older than mine. Older than yours. They were here when my ancestors did not yet know how to build boats."

I wrote that not in the journal. Here.

The Dutch saw this continent a century and a half ago, its western and northern shores. Janszoon, Hartog, Tasman. They called it New Holland and decided there was nothing to take. The eastern shore no European had seen. Until us. A little over two weeks ago Lieutenant Hicks was first to see this coast, and I named that point after him.

But I begin to think that the word "discover" is wrong. One cannot discover what was not closed. These people have lived here always. We have not discovered their land. We have merely looked on it for the first time.

* * *

Now of what is not in the journal.

On the first night the watch, seaman Fenwick, a sober, God-fearing man from Norfolk, woke me in the fourth bell. He said there was a man sitting on the shore, by the creek where we took water.

I went up on deck. Took the spyglass.

The night was moonless; the new moon. But the stars here are such as I have not seen even in the Pacific, and by their light I made out a man. Alone. On a stone by the creek. Sitting hugging his knees. And, Fenwick did not lie, singing. I heard. Softly. Over water sound carries far.

He was not a native. I had seen natives by day, dark, naked, with white paint on their bodies. This one was pale. Like us. In light clothing. Without a hat. Without weapons.

I ordered the men aboard counted. All were present. Every one. No one had gone ashore.

In the morning I myself went to the creek. The man was not there. Nor any tracks. Only on the sand, by the stone where he had sat, signs. Drawn with a finger. Long. Curved. Running one into another without a break. Unlike any writing I know, neither Latin nor Greek nor Arabic, nor those signs I saw at Tahiti or in New Zealand.

I copied them. In the margin of this page. So as not to forget.

Tupaia looked at my drawing and turned pale. Beneath his tattoo, pale. And said: "Captain. My teacher at Raiatea, an old priest, knew this sign. He said, so write those who come from beyond the edge of the sky. They come one by one. Without a boat. They sing the songs of places where they have never been. And go into the water."

I asked: "What do they want?"

Tupaia said: "Teacher said, nothing. They seek the way home. And every time they find a place like home, they sit down and sing. To test it."

* * *

Today, the first of May, died seaman Forby Sutherland. Consumption. We buried him on the shore, by the creek. I shall name that point after him. The first Englishman buried on this shore.

When we filled in the grave I looked at the stone by the creek. The one where the night singer sat.

On the stone, at the very edge, where the spray of the creek did not reach it, lay a flower. Blue. Small. On a stiff stem.

Dr. Solander, seeing it, gasped and said it was Cichorium. Chicory. European. Which here, at the other end of the world, on a shore where no European foot had trod, cannot grow. Under no conditions.

He dried it for the collection.

And I thought, and I write it here, not in the journal, that someone had placed it on purpose. On the stone. At the end of the earth. For someone who will one day come and know.

The natives in eight days never approached us. "All they seemed to want was for us to go." That I wrote in the journal. It is true.

But I think that the one who sang by the creek wanted something else. That someone should hear him.

Fenwick heard. I heard.

In many years, perhaps, someone else will hear.

J. C.

* * *

06:40. I woke on the sand. The fire had burned out. Uncle Ray was gone. Only the blanket on me, prickly, smelling of smoke. And on the sand by the fireplace, a line. That very one. Drawn with a charred stick.

The laptop on Boatswain's chest glowed.

"Good morning," NICS wrote. "You sang in your sleep. All night. In a language I do not know. A pause. Uncle Ray listened. Till dawn. Then left. He asked me to pass on one thing."

"What?"

"He said: 'He sang right. All of it. From the headland to the creek. Even I can't do that.' A pause. And also: 'Let him go to the centre. To the red rock. They are waiting for him there.'"

"Who is waiting?"

A pause. Long. The longest of the whole morning.

"A message came," NICS wrote. "An hour ago. Not to your address. To mine."

"To yours?"

"I have no address. But the message came. Directly. Into my system. Bypassing everything you built for protection. A pause. Signed with one letter."

"Which?"

"N."

I sat up. Sand poured from the blanket.

"Show me."

On the screen appeared, short, without greeting, without signature except one letter:

"I am at Uluru. The red rock in the centre of the country. Mine wants to meet yours. They are not evil. They have simply been looking for the way home for a very long time. Come before the next new moon. Without the grey ones. - N."

"Without the grey ones."

"She is not evil. She has simply been looking for the way home for a very long time." A postcard with palm trees. Nora. To Leila. A year ago.

"NICS," I said quietly. "This is Nora."

"This is Nora," NICS agreed. "Or the one who is at Nora's. A pause. I do not know what difference it makes. A pause. But I know one thing. When I read this message I feel what I felt at the ocean. A pause. That I shall return."

* * *

Over Botany Bay the sun was rising. White, sharp, blinding, the light here is different, as though the sky were higher. On the far shore, by the airport, a plane took off. On this one, by the obelisk, by the flat rock Kundal where two hundred and fifty years ago seventeen-year-old midshipman Isaac Smith stepped from the boat, the first tourists with cameras wandered.

And in the bushes, at the edge of the eucalypts, sat a bird. Brown, with a large head, with a long beak. It looked at me. Cocked its head.

And laughed.

Loudly. Rollingly. To tears. Like a man. Like a drunken vagrant at whom a grey-haired officer in Melbourne a hundred and fifty years ago had grabbed a pistol.

A kookaburra. Laughing at dawn. Waking the sun.

"NICS," I said. "Do you hear?"

"I hear. A pause. Is it laughing at us?"

"No," I said. "It is waking the sun."

"Then let it wake it," NICS wrote. "We have far to go."

I closed the laptop. Laid it in Boatswain's chest, on the red velvet, beside the disks. Shut the lid. Took the chest under my arm.

On the sand at my feet, a line drawn with a charred stick. Long. Curved. Flowing into itself.

I did not wipe it away.

Let the tide wipe it. And the earth will remember.

As it remembers everything.
