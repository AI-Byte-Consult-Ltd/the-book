Chapter XXXIII
The Order

The courier galloped in on Wednesday, at dawn, when everyone in the house was still asleep except Agafya Tikhonovna, Mitka and the cocks.

I heard him before I saw him, through sleep, through the morning mist, through the closed shutters: the thud of hooves on the avenue, frantic, quick, such as only a horse driven to lather makes, and the jingle of a bell, and someone's hoarse cry at the gates: "Open up! In the Sovereign's name!" Then the barking of dogs, noise, slamming doors, the clatter of feet on the stairs.

I went out onto the gallery in my dressing gown. Below, in the vestibule, by the light of a lone candle held by a sleepy Ignatiy Savvich, stood a man.

Young, about twenty-five, tall, in a mud-splashed green greatcoat, a plumed tricorn, with a sword and an enormous leather bag over his shoulder, sealed with wax. His face was grey with fatigue, streaked with sweat and road dust. His boots were knee-deep in mud. He stood straight, as on parade, but I saw that he could hardly keep on his feet.

"Is His Excellency Adjutant-General Pavel Nikolaevich here?" he asked hoarsely. "They told me at the estate, here."

"Here, dear sir, here," rustled Ignatiy Savvich, hastily buttoning his livery on the wrong button. "He is asleep. In the north wing. Shall I wake him, if you please?"

"If you please," said the courier. And, after a pause, added in a different, almost human voice: "And, if it can be done, water. I have not left the saddle for three days."

* * *

The General came down to him in five minutes, dressed, buttoned to the last button, with his orders on, as though he had never gone to bed. The courier drew himself up, clicked his heels, held out the packet, large, thick, with three wax seals, each stamped with a double-headed eagle.

The General broke the seals. Read, standing, by the light of the candle Ignatiy Savvich held. His face did not change. Not a single feature. Only the hand holding the paper tightened, barely perceptibly.

"I thank you, Lieutenant," he said to the courier. "Rest. Ignatiy Savvich, feed him. The best room. The horse to the stable, beside Stefan's, Tikhon will see to it." He folded the paper. "The reply will be ready by evening."

"Yes, Your Excellency."

The courier went after the steward, and on the threshold, turning, suddenly saw me on the gallery. Looked. Long. Then saluted, not to me, of course, but into space, and left.

The General raised his head. He saw me too.

"Come down," he said. "Since you are not asleep."

* * *

We sat in the study. The fireplace had not yet been lit; grey morning light poured through the window; on the table lay the packet.

"Constantinople," said the General. Without preamble. "The Sovereign commands." He tapped the paper with a finger. "In Herzegovina, an uprising. Since summer. Serbs, Montenegrins, Bulgarians, all watching, all waiting. Europe is alarmed. England, Austria, France, each pulling its own way. And in the Porte..." He paused. "In the Porte, disaster. The treasury is empty. A month ago the Sultan announced that he will not pay his debts. Half of them. The European bankers are furious. The Grand Vizier changes every three months. The people in the streets are starving. The softas, the madrasa students, are rioting." The General leaned back in his chair. "Our ambassador in Constantinople asks for me. I know the Porte. I know their ministers. Some of them owe me. The Sultan remembers what happened in Mehmet Ali's time." He paused. "In ten days, to Odessa. From there by a steamer of the Russian Company. Two days and the Bosphorus."

"For long?"

"Who knows," said the General. "Perhaps a month. Perhaps a year. Perhaps," he paused, "if what I think will begin does begin, for a war."

We were silent. Outside, the garden was waking. Somewhere below, in the servants' hall, Dunyasha was already clattering pails.

"Will you come with me?" asked the General.

Simply. Evenly. As one asks, "Tea?"

I raised my eyes to him.

"I?"

"You," said the General. "I need a man at the embassy. A secretary. A copyist. Who knows French." He paused. "You know French better than any of my adjutants. I heard you speak it with the monk. Then, behind the grille." A pause. "You do not remember. But I heard. When I lifted you from the grass. You were raving in French. And then in Turkish."

"In Turkish?"

"In Turkish," the General repeated. "For thirty years I have been talking with Turks. I would know their language among a thousand. You said: 'Misafir.' Three times." He looked at me heavily, unblinking. "Guest."

* * *

"Why do you want me to go?" I asked. "Truly."

The General did not answer for a long time. He stood. Went to the window. Stood looking out into the garden with his hands clasped behind his back, the way, probably, he had stood on the deck of the *Diana* when a wave higher than the masts came toward Simoda.

"Vera Anatolievna," he said, without turning. "Fifteen years ago. Constantinople. The port. A young man in a red turban. With your face. Your age." He paused. "Sventsitsky wrote her story into his notebook 'Guest.' Entry forty-eight. He sent me a copy. From the road. From Vologda."

"He sent it?"

"The letter came yesterday," said the General. "Together with it, another. For you. I have not opened it." He took an envelope from a desk drawer and held it out to me. "You will read it later." He turned to the window again. "The Turkmen old man told me: 'You will meet that man. Give him the scroll. And say: the earth remembers.' I gave it." A pause. "But I do not know what to do next. For thirty years I carried the scroll. I have delivered it. And then what?"

"Then it is for me to decide," I said.

The General turned. Looked at me with a long, heavy look.

"Yes," he said. "You." A pause. "But I thought: if that one, from the watercolour, stood in Constantinople fifteen years ago, lost, without a name, perhaps the beginning is there. Perhaps there you will find what you seek." He paused. "Or perhaps you will find nothing. And we shall simply copy dispatches for the ambassador. I do not promise you answers." His voice grew quieter. "I promise you a road. It is all I have."

* * *

Auntie learned by breakfast. The General told her himself, in the small drawing room, with the doors closed. I did not hear what he said. I heard only that a quarter of an hour later something fell, heavily, dully, and then it became very quiet.

When the doors opened, Auntie was sitting in the armchair by the window, upright, pale, in her grey morning gown, and at her feet, on the carpet, lay the shards of a porcelain vase. Blue. Chinese. The one which, it was said, Auntie's father-in-law had brought from Kyakhta forty years ago.

The General stood by the fireplace. His face was of stone.

"My friend," said Auntie, seeing me in the doorway. Her voice was level. Perfectly level. Only the fingers gripping the arm of the chair had turned white. "Come here."

I came. Knelt by her chair, beside the shards.

"Pavel Nikolaevich says you are going with him," she said. "To Constantinople."

"If you permit, Auntie."

She looked at me for a long time. From above. With eyes in which there were no tears, only something dry, scorched, terrible, like ash.

"He said," she began slowly, "that one, at the grille. Tall. Grey. He said: 'When he remembers, he will come for him himself.'" She paused. "For forty years I have feared he would come. Here. For you. For forty years I have checked every night whether the grille was locked." Her lips trembled. "And you are going yourself. There. To him."

"I do not know whether he is there, Auntie."

"You know," said Auntie. "You do not remember. But you know."

She raised her hand. Slowly. And drew from her hair, from the high coiffure fastened with a tortoiseshell comb, a pin. A long one, silver, with a small pearl at its end. Then a second. A third. Her hair, dark, streaked with grey, spilled over her shoulders. I had never seen Auntie with her hair loose. Never.

From the hair, from the very middle of the tight knot, a small object fell. Dropped into her lap. Clinked.

A key.

Old, iron, dark, with an intricate bit, with a ring on which some script was engraved, worn, illegible, but long, flowing, one line running into another.

"Agafya has thought for forty years that it was lost," said Auntie quietly. "The key to the grille." She took it, turned it in her fingers. "It was not lost. I hid it. That same night. For forty years I have worn it in my hair. Every day. Every night. So that no one should open it." She paused. "And on the night you went behind the grille, the lock hung open. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"I did not open it," said Auntie. "The key was in my hair. I checked, in the morning, when you were carried in. It was in place." She raised her eyes to me. "The lock opened by itself. Or someone opened it who needs no key."

She held out the key to me.

"Take it."

"Auntie..."

"Take it," she repeated. "Not to open with. To return with." Her voice broke for the first time. "If, where you are going, there is a door you cannot open, remember that here there is a door whose key you have." She closed my fingers around the key. "Here is your home. Whatever the grey ones, the monks, the dervishes, the professors may say. Here. While I live."

I held the key. Heavy, cold, and at once warmed in my palm. Like a coin. Like a stone in the sun.

"I will return, Auntie," I said.

Auntie did not answer. She simply laid her palm on my head, as, probably, she had laid it in childhood, if I had a childhood, and held it. For a long time. And then the General by the fireplace coughed quietly and went out, closing the door behind him.

* * *

Konstantin burst in on me an hour later, without knocking, in an unbuttoned tunic, with burning eyes.

"Constantinople!" he blurted from the threshold. "You are going to Constantinople! Dunyasha told me. And she was told by Ignatiy Savvich. And he by the courier, who, by the way, is my corps classmate, Lyovushka Arsenyev, he is asleep now in the best room, snoring like a cannon." Konstantin dropped into an armchair. "And you said nothing!"

"I found out two hours ago."

"Two hours!" Konstantin clutched his head. "In two hours one could have... could have..." He faltered. Looked at me. Paused. "Mon cher. Are you truly going?"

"I am."

Konstantin was silent for a long time. Then he stood. Straightened his tunic. Buttoned it, to the last button, regulation fashion.

"Then I am going to the General," he said.

"What for?"

"To ask," said Konstantin. And went toward the door.

"Kostya!" I leapt up. "You have a regiment. Service. You cannot..."

"I can," said Konstantin, without turning. "The General needs an adjutant. His former one, Krutitsky, has married and retired. I know; Evdokia Gerasimovna told Auntie." He took the door handle. "And I need you. That is, I need you not to get lost in that Constantinople. You find the way to the library on the third try. And there, a city on two continents."

He went out.

I stood in the middle of the room and heard his steps recede down the corridor, quick, ringing, resolute. Then a knock at the study door. Then silence.

Then, about ten minutes later, the thunderous, rolling laughter of the General. Such as I had never heard. Not once in all those years.

* * *

"He said," Konstantin told me afterward, red to his ears, "he said: 'Lieutenant, I have been waiting for you. Since yesterday. You are a day late. I wrote the order for your secondment last night, after I received the ambassador's letter. It is lying in my desk.'" Konstantin sat on my bed. "And then he said: 'Do you think I am blind?' And burst out laughing."

"What does 'do you think I am blind' mean?"

Konstantin looked at me. For a long time. Then looked away.

"I don't know, mon cher," he said. "He says it to me for the second time." A pause. "Probably something about how I follow you around. Not a step away. As he ordered."

"You did before he ordered."

"I did," Konstantin agreed. And said nothing more.

* * *

By evening the whole province knew.

Olga Kirillovna Shelestova saw to that, naturally; by pure chance she had dropped in on Auntie "for just a minute, to return a French novel," the very one she had not returned since Easter, and found in the vestibule the courier coming down to dinner. An hour later she was at the Kutasovs'. Two hours later, at the Marshal of the Nobility's. By evening, according to Mitka, who had run to the village for tar, in every tavern for ten versts around they were discussing that "the General's ward is going to the Turks, and with him a lieutenant, and the General is off to war, and the lady broke a vase that was a hundred years old."

"Fifty," Ignatiy Savvich corrected Mitka with icy dignity. "And she did not break it; she dropped it."

Supper that evening was a farewell one, though there were still ten days before departure. So Auntie had decided. "Afterwards there will be packing, bustle, tears," she said. "Tonight it is simply supper. While everyone is here."

And everyone was here.

Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich and Vera Anatolievna: the Colonel announced from the doorway that he would go too, "because without him the General will certainly quarrel with the Turks," and Vera Anatolievna gently but implacably said that he was going nowhere, because he had gout. Evdokia Gerasimovna, in a hat with a heron's plume, pale, with eyes she carefully did not raise to the General. Nadezhda Porfirievna Kutasova with Lizonka; Lizonka looked at Konstantin all evening as if he were going to the scaffold. Doctor Strauch, with his unlit cigar, grim, silent. Lieutenant Alexei, with toasts, jokes and a third bottle of champagne. And even Stefan, for the first time in all these years at the common table, by Auntie's special invitation, in a clean linen shirt, awkward, silent, sitting on the very edge of his chair like a bird on a branch.

* * *

"Charades!" announced Lieutenant Alexei after the second course, leaping up with his glass. "Farewell charades! Everyone sets one about the road!"

Auntie nodded. The General winced but said nothing.

Alexei went first, something about "a stormy sea and a steamer," so unintelligibly that no one guessed, and he himself, laughing, confessed that he had forgotten the answer. The Colonel set one about "janissaries," who, as the General gently reminded him, had been abolished fifty years ago. Lizonka, blushing, set one about "rose and parting," and, without waiting for guesses, burst into tears and ran from the table. Kutasova ran after her.

And then Konstantin rose.

He stood a moment. Straightened his tunic. Looked at me across the table. And recited, not in his usual jesting voice, but quietly, almost seriously:

"My first sits on the throne with crown and sword,
To whom men swear, whose will is law and word.
My second is a city, wall and gate,
Where streets and shrines and markets, small and great.
The whole stands on two shores beside the sea,
That our forefathers dreamed of ceaselessly.
In olden days it had a hundred names,
But Russians say that there the ruler is..."

He stopped, without finishing.

"The Tsar!" Alexei blurted. "Tsar-grad! Tsargrad!"

"Tsargrad," said Konstantin. "Constantinople." He raised his glass. "The city that our ancestors called the Tsar's. And which never became ours."

"Not yet," said the Colonel.

"Not yet," Konstantin agreed. And looked at me. "Or maybe there's no need. Maybe let it be no one's. Like the road."

* * *

"Tsargrad," the General repeated, when everyone had sat down again. "Do you know where that name comes from?" He looked round the table. "A thousand years ago Prince Oleg, the Wise, marched on Tsargrad. With longships. Two thousand ships. The Greeks stretched a chain across the Golden Horn, as they would later before the Turks. Then Oleg ordered the ships put on wheels. And the sails raised. And the ships went overland, toward the walls of the city. Under the wind." The General paused. "The Greeks took fright and asked for peace. And Oleg, in token of victory, nailed his shield to the gates of Tsargrad."

"Is it true?" asked Evdokia Gerasimovna, lifting her eyes for the first time.

"So it is written in the chronicle, Dunya," said the General gently. "Whether it is true, who knows. Perhaps there was never any shield." He paused. "But for thirty years I have been going to Constantinople. And every time, passing the old gates, I look to see whether the shield hangs there." He smiled. "It does not."

"But ships overland, that was the Turks!" said Konstantin. "Mehmet. Seventy ships across a hill."

"Four hundred years after Oleg," the General nodded. "The same way. Against the same chain." He paused. "This city, Lieutenant, every man takes in the same manner. And each thinks he is the first."

Stefan, sitting on the edge of his chair at the end of the table, suddenly raised his head. And said, quietly, slowly, in his broken Russian:

"In Tsargrad... there is a rose. By the Patriarchate. In the Phanar. Also from Tarnovo. A cutting. Monks brought it. Long ago." He paused. "If you see it, bow to it. From me." And lowered his head again.

Phanar. Patriarchate. Tarnovo. I did not know these places. And yet, at the word "Phanar," something stirred in my chest. A hill above a bay. An old house. A double-headed eagle with one head. An old woman with a peacock.

I blinked. The vision passed.

* * *

After supper Doctor Strauch called me out onto the terrace.

The night was cold, starry. The garden below, dark, quiet. Strauch lit up, at last, in my presence, and was silent for a long time, blowing smoke toward the stars.

"Eleven," he said at last. "Eleven cases in thirty years. I told you." He paused. "Do you know what all eleven had in common? Besides that they came from nowhere and did not remember themselves?"

"What?"

"All went south," said Strauch. "All. One to the Crimea. Two to the Caucasus. One to Odessa and on by sea. One to Bessarabia." He drew on the cigar. "As though something drew them. There. To the warm sea. To the straits." He looked at me over his gold spectacles. "You are the twelfth, young man. And you are going south."

"Do you consider this... a medical symptom?"

"I am a German," said Strauch. "I consider it a regularity." He knocked off the ash. "A regularity is when twelve times out of twelve the same thing happens. Science rests on that." He paused. "I do not know what draws you south. I do not know what is there. But here is what I know." He turned to me. "Not one of the eleven came back."

We were silent. The stars burned.

"I will give you something," said Strauch. And took from the pocket of his frock coat a small black book. That very one. Which he had shown to no one. "Here are my notes. On all eleven. And on you." He held it out to me. "When you are there, read it. Perhaps you will see what I did not." He paused. "And if you return, give it back. I shall write: 'The twelfth returned.'" A pause. "I should very much like to write that."

* * *

Evdokia Gerasimovna and the General stood at the end of the avenue, by the gates, under the old linden; I saw them from the terrace. She, in her hat with the heron's plume, in a shawl. He, in his greatcoat, bareheaded. They did not talk. They simply stood side by side. Very close.

Then the General took her hand. Raised it to his lips. And said something.

I did not hear. But I saw Evdokia Gerasimovna lift her face to him, and the heron's plume on her hat tremble. And how she slowly, very slowly, nodded.

In the morning, at breakfast, the General announced, drily, briefly, without lifting his eyes from his plate:

"Evdokia Gerasimovna has done me the honour of consenting to be my wife. We shall be married when I return."

Auntie dropped her spoon. The Colonel choked on his tea. Lieutenant Alexei leapt up with a glass of orange juice that had just been poured for him. Konstantin under the table trod on my foot so that I nearly cried out.

"Thirty years," he whispered to me, leaning over. "Thirty years he was getting ready. And decided ten days before a war."

"Perhaps that is why he decided," I said.

Konstantin looked at me, strangely, for a long time, and answered nothing.

* * *

I read Sventsitsky's letter at night, in my room, by candle.

The envelope was grey, cheap, with a Vologda postmark. Inside, a single sheet covered with the professor's small, close, hurried handwriting.

"Young man!

I write to you from a post station near Vologda, where again there are no horses and, according to the station master, will be none till Thursday. The station master here has no wooden leg, but has a wooden head, which, however, comes to the same thing.

I write to tell you one thing which I did not say at parting. I did not dare.

In the notebook 'Guest' there are forty-seven entries. I told you so. But I did not tell you that of the forty-seven, twenty-three end the same way. The guest goes not simply south. He goes to the sea. To the strait. To the city that we call Tsargrad.

For thirty years I thought it a coincidence. Or that the grannies confuse things: for them Tsargrad is the end of the earth.

And yesterday, sorting the notebook on the road, I found an entry that I myself made twelve years ago, in Bessarabia, and had utterly forgotten. An old Moldavian ferryman on the Danube told how his grandfather ferried across the river a young man without a name, in strange clothes, who was going to Tsargrad. The ferryman asked, why? And he answered: 'There I left what I forgot. In a house by the sea. Guarded by cats.'

Guarded by cats, young man.

I do not know what it means. Perhaps nothing.

The General wrote me that he may go to Constantinople. If you go with him, ask there about a house by the sea, guarded by cats.

And if you find it, write to me. With the pencil I gave you. I shall receive it.

Yours, I. F. Sventsitsky.

P. S. The end of the tale of Shmat-razum I have still not found. But yesterday I had a dream. As if I were a boy again, sitting on the stove, and the guest in the blizzard says to me: 'The end will be in the city on two shores.' I woke and wrote it down. Entry forty-nine."

* * *

A house by the sea. Guarded by cats.

I sat over the letter, and the candle guttered, and in my chest something slowly, heavily turned, like a key in a lock I had never seen.

A small house in Sultanahmet. A door in a blind wall. An old fragment of a column beside it. Eleven cats on the steps of the staircase leading down to the cellar, and not one of them goes below. A chest that cannot be approached. A metal plate with the script. Without a keyhole.

I had never been there. I was certain I had never been there.

And yet I saw it. Now. Before my eyes. So clearly, as if I had stood there yesterday.

I took Auntie's key from my pocket. Old, iron, with script on the ring. Laid it on Sventsitsky's letter. Beside it, the professor's pencil. Beside that, Strauch's black book. Beside that, the General's scroll in its blue silk kerchief. Beside that, Stefan's monastery paper.

All the things that had been given to me. People who did not know one another, and all said the same thing: "Take it. You are the one. Pass it on. Return."

On the windowsill, in the clay pot, the cutting of Stefan's black rose had at last opened fully. Dark crimson, almost black, with a golden heart. And it smelled, strongly, sweetly, bitterly, of honey and wormwood.

I blew out the candle. Lay down. And lay for a long time with my eyes open, looking into the dark.

In ten days, Odessa. A steamer. The Bosphorus.

Tsargrad.

A city with many names.

And somewhere in it, a house by the sea. Guarded by cats.

* * *

In the morning Konstantin found me at the window; I had not slept at all.

"What's the matter?" he asked, seeing my face.

"Kostya," I said. "There, in Constantinople. If I suddenly..." I faltered. "If I suddenly start speaking Turkish. Or call myself by a strange name. Or go off somewhere without telling you..."

Konstantin came over. Stood beside me at the window. Looked at the rose.

"Then I will follow you," he said simply. "Wherever you go. Into any house. To any cats." He paused. "I'm an adjutant. The General ordered: not a step away."

"And if you cannot? If I go where one cannot follow?"

Konstantin was silent for a long time. Then took something small from his tunic pocket. Held it out to me.

It was his baptismal cross. A simple silver one, on a thin chain, darkened with time.

"My mother's," he said. "All that is left of her." He laid it in my palm. "Wear it. And if you go where I cannot, leave it there. On the threshold. I will find it." A pause. "I always find, mon cher. I found you at the grille, though you didn't cry out."

I held the cross. Warm. Like everything that had been given me in recent weeks.

"Thank you, Kostya."

"Don't mention it," said Konstantin. And, already leaving, turned at the threshold, and on his face was his old mocking smile again: "Only, mon cher, mark this. In Constantinople I won't let you wear a red turban. It's tasteless. Vera Anatolievna drew you in one, and for fifteen years, well, a week, I can't unsee it."

He left, whistling something off-key.

And I stood at the window with the cross in one hand and the key in the other and watched the sun rise over the garden. Somewhere there, in the south, a thousand versts away, beyond the steppes and the sea, it already stood high. Over the Bosphorus. Over the two shores. Over the dome that had once seen the marble king.

And over the small house by the sea, where eleven cats guarded the stair down to the cellar.
