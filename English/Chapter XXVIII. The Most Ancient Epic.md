Chapter XXVIII
The Most Ancient Epic

The grain was distributed on the third day of the festival, from dawn to sunset, at the gates of the Temple granaries - and I stood there all day, from the first sack to the last, beside Nefer-Ka.

So it had been proclaimed: from the hands of Pharaoh and the High Priest. Together. And we stood together - shoulder to shoulder, on a dais by the gate, above an endless river of people flowing past us with baskets, sacks, pots, with hems of garments stretched out like bowls. Nebamun sat at the foot of the dais at a low table and counted - every sack, every measure, every handful - dry, motionless as a heron over water. Beside him Ani, the deaf scribe of the council, wrote after him, every now and then asking "Repeat for the record?" so loudly that people in the queue began to laugh and the tension on the square eased.

Nefer-Ka smiled. All day. Bowed to the people. Blessed children. Touched the brows of the old with his hand. Pronounced: "The Temple gives what is its own to the people, as the god gives his water." And the people bowed to him. Bowed to both of us. But I saw - and Nebamun, I knew, saw too - that they bowed differently. To me - quickly, respectfully, as one bows to Pharaoh. To him - slowly, long, peering into his face. As one bows to the one who decides whether you will have bread tomorrow.

"He has outplayed you, my Pharaoh," Nebamun said to me in the evening, when the gates of the granaries had closed and the last sack was recorded. "You made him give the grain. And he made sure the people will remember that it was the Temple who gave it." He folded his tablets. "Three thousand hekats. The people will be fed for two moons. And they will thank him."

"I know, Nebamun."

"You know," nodded the keeper of the treasury. "And still you stood all day." He paused. "My father used to say: there are kings who feed the people, and there are kings who feed their own glory. The first are forgotten. The second are remembered." He looked at me over his tablets. "I, my Pharaoh, prefer to serve the first. But I am too old not to fear for them."

* * *

Paney found me at the exit from the square - out of breath, barefoot, with the training spear which he now carried everywhere, as Iti had once carried his brush.

"My Pharaoh!" he blurted. "Bata told me to pass it on. From Panehsy. The man with the scar through his eyebrow. He has gone."

"Gone?"

"Last night. The Medjay were watching. He left the tent where the bearers lived - though the bearers are no longer there, they left with the ambassador - and walked across the whole city to the temple of Ptah. Knocked at the back gate. They let him in." Paney caught his breath. "Panehsy says - he has not come out. Till now. He sits in the temple."

"Who opened the gate?"

Paney shook his head.

"The Medjay did not see. It was dark. Only a hand." He hesitated. "Bata says Panehsy said - the hand jingled."

Bracelets.

"Good, Paney," I said. "Tell Panehsy: let them keep watching. Not touch. And tell Bata that you did everything right."

The boy beamed and raced off. I watched him go and thought how quickly children learn to be soldiers when around them there is a war that no one has declared.

* * *

Amenemope sent for me at dusk - not a messenger, but Iti himself.

The youth waited at the small garden gate, under the jasmine, where we now met almost every night, and, seeing me, bowed - not like a scribe but somehow anew, awkwardly and warmly at once.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "The senior keeper says: tonight. The ark is open. He has the key. The High Priest has been on the square all day and this evening will be at the feast of the nomarch of Memphis - the one in honour of the festival. Until midnight he will not be here." He paused. "The senior keeper says that you have long wished to read the Epic whole. Not only those lines that are read at coronations." Iti raised his eyes to me. "He says: tonight it is possible. Perhaps never again."

"Why never again?"

Iti hesitated.

"He did not say, my Pharaoh. He only looked at me as old men look when they know something and do not wish to speak."

* * *

The Library at night was different from the Library by day.

By day it was a place where people worked: scribes bent over scrolls, brushes scratched, papyrus rustled, someone read aloud in an undertone, someone argued over a sign. At night it was a place where things slept. Not people - scrolls. Thousands of scrolls, laid in niches along the walls, from floor to ceiling, in leather cases, in clay jars, in wooden caskets - they lay in the dark as the old sleep, quietly, motionlessly, and I, entering, involuntarily slowed my step, as though afraid to wake them.

It smelled of cedar, dust, mice and old papyrus - sweetish, slightly musty, as dried reeds smell on a riverbank. A single lamp burned in the depths, by the large table. And beside it, leaning on his staff of black wood, stood Amenemope.

On the table before him lay the ark.

I had seen it before - at the coronation, from afar, when it was carried out, opened, three lines read from it and closed again. A long casket of cedar, covered with gold, with a lid in the form of a temple roof, with guardians at the corners - little golden figures of goddesses with outspread wings, embracing the ark on all sides as though protecting it. On the lid - a sign: a winged sun disc. And beneath it - two Eyes. Right and left.

I had not noticed this before. Two Eyes.

"My Pharaoh," said Amenemope, bowing. His voice in the silence of the Library sounded like the rustle of a turning page. "I promised that I would bring it at any hour of day or night. I keep my promises." He paused. "I am old. I do not know how many more years I shall hold this key. Perhaps not many. The High Priest lately asks me whether I am tired. Whether I do not wish to retire. To a quiet temple. By the southern cataracts."

By the southern cataracts. Whence no letters come.

"You will not go to the southern cataracts, Amenemope," I said.

The old man smiled - drily, as old parchment cracks.

"My Pharaoh is kind," he said. "But Pharaoh does not always decide who goes where." He turned the key in the lock of the ark. "Sit. The night is long, and the Epic longer. The boy will read. His eyes are younger than mine. And I shall listen and correct if he errs. Though he does not err. Ever. It is even tedious."

Iti blushed.

* * *

The Epic was not one scroll. There were seven. Seven tight, heavy scrolls, each as thick as an arm, wrapped in the finest linen yellowed to the colour of ivory. Amenemope took them out one by one, carefully, as infants are lifted from a cradle, and laid them on the table in a row.

"Seven," he said. "Like the seven branches of the Nile in the Delta. Like the seven Hathors who come to every child's cradle and foretell his fate." He touched the first scroll. "This one is about the beginning. This one - about the gods. This - about the First Ones. This - about the kings. This - about how people live. This - about death and what is after. And the seventh..." He stopped. "The seventh no one has read. Because no one can."

"Why?"

"You will see, my Pharaoh."

Iti unrolled the first scroll. Bent over it - so low that the ink stain on his cheek, fresh, today's, almost touched the papyrus. And began to read. Quietly, slowly, in a chant, as ancient texts are read - with that special, solemn intonation taught in the school of scribes from the very first days.

"'In the beginning there was nothing. There was no sky. There was no earth. There was neither day nor night, neither death nor life. There were no gods, and there were no names for that which was not. There was only water. Nun. Endless, dark, motionless water, which had neither bottom nor surface nor edge. It was everything - and it was nothing. It neither slept nor woke. It waited.'"

Iti fell silent, drew breath. The lamp crackled.

"'And out of the water rose a hill. Small. The first. Benben. As the first patch of black earth rises from the flood when the water recedes. And on the hill stood He-who-created-himself. Atum. He was not born - he came into being. He said: "I am." And when he said it - he became.'"

"'I am,'" I repeated quietly. "And became."

"Yes, my Pharaoh," said Amenemope from the shadow. "The most ancient word in the world. The first thing that was said. Everything else - after."

Somewhere deep within me, under the ribs, on the left, something stirred. Two words on a luminous window, in the dark, in a room I had never seen: "Who am I?" - "Who are you?"

I blinked. The vision went.

* * *

Iti read on. How Atum, lonely on his hill, breathed out - and from his breath was born Shu, the air. And spat - and from his spittle was born Tefnut, moisture. How Shu and Tefnut were lost in the darkness of Nun, and Atum sent his Eye to seek them, and the Eye found them and brought them back, and Atum wept with joy, and from his tears people were born.

"People were born from tears?" I asked.

"From tears, my Pharaoh," confirmed Amenemope. "That is why people weep so often. It is in their blood. It is their first memory."

"And the Eye?" I asked. "The Eye that sought the lost children?"

"The Eye became independent," said the old man. "Went away from Atum. Because while it sought the children, Atum had made himself a new Eye - and set it in the place of the old. And the old one returned and saw that its place was taken. And raged." He paused. "Then Atum took the old Eye and set it on his forehead. As a serpent. As the uraeus. The very one that Pharaoh wears on his crown. So that it would always be in front. And always look where he looks."

I involuntarily touched my forehead - where by day, on the square, in the crown, I had the uraeus.

"The Eye that seeks the lost," said Iti quietly, not raising his head from the scroll. "And returns. And finds its place taken. And rages." He paused. "Like the lioness in the southern ambassador's tale. Who went to the south."

"You listened?" I asked.

"I was writing it down, my Pharaoh," said Iti. "Every word. I always write down. Even when I am not asked."

He read on. How Shu and Tefnut bore Geb and Nut - earth and sky. How Geb and Nut loved each other so greatly that they lay embracing and there was no room between them for anything. How Shu, their father, stood between them and lifted Nut up - and so she stands ever since, arched over the earth, resting her hands on the west and her feet on the east - and weeps every morning with dew, because she cannot reach Geb.

"The star-watcher Seneb told me this," I said. "Four years ago. On an island in the Delta. When..."

I broke off. Iti raised his head. Looked at me.

"When you pulled a scribe boy out of the water," he said quietly. "Yes, my Pharaoh. I remember."

We looked at each other over the ancient scroll. The lamp crackled. Amenemope in the shadow coughed - delicately, as the old cough when they wish to say "I am here" and at once "I am not here."

"Read on, Iti," I said.

"I read, my Pharaoh," he said. And bent to the scroll. But his cheeks burned.

* * *

"'But there was another account,'" Iti read, having unrolled the second scroll. "'The priests of Memphis tell it differently. In Memphis they say: the world was not made by Atum. The world was made by Ptah. Ptah, who was before Atum. Ptah conceived the world in his heart. And named it with his tongue. And all that he conceived with his heart and named with his tongue - came to be. The sky came to be because he said "sky." The earth came to be because he said "earth." The gods came to be because he called them by name.'"

Iti stopped.

"Conceived with the heart and named with the tongue," he repeated slowly. "My Pharaoh, this is... this is like us. Like scribes. We too first conceive, and then write. And what we have written remains. Even when we are gone."

"Not so, boy," said Amenemope from the shadow. "We write what already is. Ptah named what was not yet." He paused. "Though... who knows. Perhaps we too sometimes write what is not yet. And then it appears. Because it has been named."

"How - appears?" I asked.

The old man raised his staff and pointed it at the shelves going off into the dark.

"Here lie thousands of scrolls, my Pharaoh. In them are recorded names. Kings. Gods. Cities. Rivers. People. Most of these people died long ago. Most of the cities are no more." He lowered the staff. "But while a scroll lies here, while someone can read the name - they are. They are still here. Not entirely. Half. Like shadows. Like those in the Duat, between worlds." He paused. "Ptah named the world - and the world came to be. We name the world - and the world remains. Perhaps it is the same. Only from different ends."

From different ends. Like an inscription that is read in both directions.

How did I know that? I did not know.

* * *

The third scroll was about the First Ones.

Iti unrolled it - and froze.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered. "Here... here the hand is different."

I bent toward him. He was right. The first two scrolls were written in an even, solemn, ancient hand - such as was written under the very first kings. This one - also ancient, but different. More angular. More hurried. As though the writer was in haste. Or afraid he would not be in time.

"'And then,'" Iti read, running a finger along the lines without touching them, "'when the gods had gone to heaven, and people were left on earth alone, and did not know how to live, and died of hunger, and killed one another, and had neither king nor law nor Maat - then there came down from the sky the First Ones.'"

"'They came down in a barque,'" Iti read. "'Not such as ours. Their barque was made of light. It shone so that people fell on their faces and could not look. It alighted on a hill, on that very Benben where Atum had once stood. And from the barque came out the First Ones. They were tall - taller than any man. Their faces shone. They spoke a language that no one understood - and all understood.'"

Tall. Taller than any man.

"'They taught people to sow barley and emmer. To count the days by the stars. To measure the water of the Nile. To build. To write. They gave people Maat - truth, order, measure. They chose from among the people the most worthy - and set him as king. The first Pharaoh. And gave into his hands that which they had brought with them from heaven.'"

Iti raised his eyes.

"'The Two Eyes,'" he read.

I stopped breathing.

"'One Eye is light,'" Iti read on, and his voice trembled. "'It leads. It sees the path. The other is dark. It keeps. It remembers. He who holds both stands at the door between worlds. He who holds both can go and return. But cannot remain.'"

"Cannot remain," I repeated.

"'And the First Ones said to the king: "We shall go. We must. But we shall return. One of us will return. Not as now. In another body. In another time. He will not remember who he is. He will seek. When he finds - let him find the Two Eyes where they wait. And when he remembers his first name - he will return what was taken. And the two Eyes will become one."'"

Iti fell silent. In the Library it became so quiet that I heard somewhere in the dark, behind the shelves, a mouse scratching.

"You have heard this before," I said to Amenemope. Not asking.

"I have heard this for forty years, my Pharaoh," said the old man from the shadow. "From my teacher. And he from his. These lines are known by heart to all senior keepers." He paused. "And all of them asked one question. Who is 'he'? Who will return? Whom to await?"

"And who?"

"It is not written, my Pharaoh," said Amenemope. "There is no name." He paused. "But my teacher used to say: each time a new Pharaoh ascends the throne, the High Priest comes here. And rereads these lines. And looks at the new Pharaoh. Long." The old man tapped his staff on the floor. "And waits."

* * *

The fourth scroll - about the kings - Iti read quickly, hurrying; it was a long, tedious list of names, years of reign, wars, temples, floods. The fifth - about people: how to plough, how to sow, how to bury, how to judge. The sixth - about death: about the Duat, the twelve gates, the Hall of Two Truths, the forty-two judges, the feather of Maat and the beast Ammit. All this I had already heard - from Amenemope, from Seneb, from Khemu. All this was familiar.

But in the sixth scroll, at the very end, there was a place that made Iti turn pale.

"'And beyond the edge of the world,'" he read, "'beyond the deserts, beyond the seas, beyond the Great Green, beyond the land of Punt, whence incense comes, beyond the land of Keftiu, whence bulls and ships come, beyond the land of Retenu, whence cedars come - there the earth ends. And Nun begins. That same water from which everything came. It surrounds the world on all sides - above, below, at the edges. The world in it is like a bubble of air in a jar of water. Small. Fragile. And if the bubble bursts - Nun will return. And all will be as it was before the beginning. Nothing.'"

"The world - like a bubble in water," I said.

"So the ancients believed, my Pharaoh," said Amenemope. "And so they believe to this day. That is why each day the priests perform rites. So the bubble does not burst. So that Ra passes through the Duat and is born again. So that Apep - the serpent of chaos - does not swallow the solar barque." He paused. "The priests say that without them the world will end. Perhaps so. Or perhaps the world is simply very patient."

"Have you seen the edge of the world, Amenemope?" Iti suddenly asked.

The old man laughed - drily, with a rattle.

"I, boy, have never gone farther than Memphis." He paused. "But I knew a man who had seen. A sailor. He went to Punt - south, along the Red Sea, for incense. Twenty times he went. He told of people there with dark skin who build houses on piles. That trees grow there from which frankincense flows. That there are beasts there - spotted, with necks to the sky." He smiled. "We laughed at him then. A neck to the sky! And today such a beast ate the fig tree in Pharaoh's courtyard."

Iti snorted. I too.

"And farther than Punt?" I asked.

"Farther, said the sailor," said Amenemope, "the sea. Endless. And once, in a storm, their ship was carried far to the south. So far that the stars overhead became different. He said - stars appeared that no one had ever seen. Four. In a cross. Bright." The old man shook his head. "We did not believe him then. Stars are the same always and everywhere. So it is written. But he swore. He said: the world is bigger than written in scrolls. Much bigger. And at its edge - not Nun. But other people. Who look at other stars."

Four stars. In a cross. Somewhere I had heard this. Somewhere, from someone. A tall grey-haired man in strange clothes, by a fireplace, telling of a white light, eucalyptus trees and a bird that laughs at dawn...

No. I had never heard this. I blinked - and the alien voice went.

* * *

The seventh scroll Amenemope unrolled himself.

Slowly. Carefully. His hands, usually so firm, trembled.

"Here," he said. "Here is why no one has read it."

I bent over the table. Iti beside me, so close that I felt the warmth of his shoulder.

The seventh scroll was written not in hieroglyphs. All of it, from edge to edge. In long, flowing, curving lines running one into another without breaks, without spaces, without beginning or end.

The script.

The same as on the papyrus from the lower storehouse. The same as on the fragment of the Rock of the First Ones that Djehuty brought. The same as on the forehead of blind Nanakhat.

"For forty years I have looked at it," said Amenemope quietly. "And my teacher looked at it for forty years. And his teacher. No one has read a sign." He paused. "The High Priest has looked at it for thirty years. Three times in a single moon he took it. Sat right here where you sit, my Pharaoh, and looked till dawn."

Iti was not listening. He looked at the scroll - without tearing away, bent so low that he almost lay on the table. His fingers moved above the lines, not touching them - back and forth, back and forth - as though he were counting something.

"My Pharaoh," he suddenly whispered. "Look. Here. At the very beginning."

I looked. At the very beginning of the scroll, in the upper corner, the script broke off. There, in a small square window ringed with a red line, stood three lines. Not in script. In hieroglyphs. Small, ancient, faded. And beneath them - script again. One line. Short.

"Here it is written in our signs," said Iti, and his voice broke to a whisper. "'Words of the First Ones. Keep until the appointed time.' And beneath it - the script. Of the same length." He raised his eyes to me, huge, dark, shining. "My Pharaoh, it is the same. The same thing is written here - in our signs and in theirs. Side by side. Like a translation."

Amenemope, standing in the shadow, slowly came to the table. Bent. Looked long.

"Forty years," he whispered. "Forty years I looked at this corner. And thought - it is merely a heading. A keeper's note." He straightened. "And it is a key."

Iti was already taking from his bosom a rolled papyrus - the very one from the lower storehouse, found by him in the jar - and spreading it alongside. "Words of the First Ones. Sent down from the Heavens together with the Two Eyes. Do not read. Do not show. Keep until the appointed time." And beneath it - the script.

He moved his finger from one to the other. Compared. Moved his lips.

"Here," he said at last. "Here is this sign. Here - and here. The same. It stands where we have 'keep.'" The finger moved on. "And this one, with the loop - where we have 'time.'" He leaned back, pale, with burning eyes. "My Pharaoh. We know two words. Two words in the language of the First Ones. 'Keep.' 'Time.'"

"Two words," repeated Amenemope. "In forty years - two words."

"In one night," I corrected.

* * *

The door of the Library opened without a knock.

All three of us turned at once. Amenemope dropped his staff - it fell with a dull thud on the stone floor. Iti lunged - to seize the papyrus from the lower storehouse, to hide it - and froze, too late.

In the doorway stood Nefer-Ka.

Without retinue. Without torch. In a plain white robe, without the leopard skin, without the staff with the Eye. Tall, straight as an obelisk. His face - motionless, calm. He looked at the table. At the seven unrolled scrolls. At the seventh. At the small papyrus beside it.

"The feast of the nomarch of Memphis," he said quietly, "proved duller than I expected. I decided to return early." He entered, slowly, softly, treading noiselessly with bare feet on the stone slabs. "And I see I was not mistaken. It is far more interesting here."

Amenemope, trembling, bent for his staff. Nefer-Ka picked it up first - lightly, in one movement - and held it out to the old man.

"Do not trouble yourself, honoured keeper," he said gently. "At your age it is harmful to bend. One may not straighten again."

Amenemope took the staff. His hands shook.

Nefer-Ka went to the table. Stopped opposite me. Looked at the seventh scroll. Then at the little papyrus. Then at Iti.

"So this is what you found in the jar, boy," he said. "And Isiret showed me a sodden account of grain." He smiled slightly. "Old fox. I have always respected her."

Iti stood motionless. White as linen. But he did not retreat. And did not lower his eyes.

"Do not touch him, Nefer-Ka," I said.

The High Priest slowly shifted his gaze to me.

"Touch?" He raised his brows. "My Pharaoh, I do not touch scribes. Scribes are the most precious thing in the Two Lands. Without them there would be neither laws nor gods nor Epic." He looked at Iti again. "Especially such as this. Who in one night has read two words which I could not read in thirty years."

* * *

He sat. Directly opposite me, on the stool where Amenemope had just sat. Laid his palms on the table, on either side of the seventh scroll. Not touching it.

"You want to know what I seek, my Pharaoh," he said. Not asking. "You think - power. Grain. The throne. You think I want to become you."

"And do you not?"

Nefer-Ka was silent a long time. The lamp crackled. Somewhere in the dark the mouse scratched on.

"I read this Epic when I was twenty," he said at last. "In secret. My teacher, the former High Priest, gave me the key for one night - as you gave it to this boy. I read. All of it. From beginning to end. And understood one thing which before me, it seems, no one understood." He raised his eyes to me. "The First Ones return. Again and again. In another body, in another time, not remembering themselves. So it is written. You have read it."

"I have."

"And do you know what happens when they return?" His voice dropped. "I read the chronicles. All the chronicles in this Library, for all the years, for all the kings. I searched. Twenty years I searched. And found." He paused. "Every time in the chronicles a king appears who 'looks through people,' 'does not remember his name,' 'speaks in his sleep in an unknown tongue' - every time after his reign a drought comes. A great one. Three years. Five. Seven. Every time. Because he comes - and goes. And takes the Eye with him. That which holds the water. That which holds the world."

Cold under the ribs. On the left.

"Three hundred years ago," Nefer-Ka went on, "there was a Pharaoh whose name was erased from the walls. He came from nowhere. He did not remember who he was. He reigned ten years. Then he went to the festival of Opet at Luxor - and returned different. And a year later he vanished. Entirely. And for seven years there was no flood. Half of Egypt died of hunger." Nefer-Ka looked at me unblinking. "The priests erased his name. So that he would not return. But he returned. He always returns."

"And you think that is I."

"I do not think, my Pharaoh," said Nefer-Ka. "I know. I looked at you when you ascended the throne. As all High Priests looked at all Pharaohs. And I saw." He paused. "You look through people. You do not remember where you came from. You speak in your sleep. Your servants hear." He smiled slightly. "Your physician Hesira treats your headache with a clay crocodile and asks when it began. And you answer - 'it seems, always.'"

"The drought began three years ago," I said. "I have reigned longer."

"The drought does not begin when he goes, my Pharaoh," said Nefer-Ka gently. "The drought begins when he begins to remember."

* * *

Iti behind my back sighed quietly. Amenemope stood motionless, leaning on his staff.

"And what do you want, Nefer-Ka?" I asked.

The High Priest rose. Looked at the seventh scroll.

"I want him to stay this time," he said. Simply. Without a smile. "That the Eye remain here. In Egypt. By the river. That the water return - and never leave again. Never." He raised his eyes to me, and in them for the first time in all the time I had known him I saw not calculation, not condescension, not predatory patience, but something else. Weariness. Ancient, endless weariness. "For thirty years I have watched people die of hunger, my Pharaoh. I have buried children. I have seen mothers sell daughters for a sack of barley." He paused. "I do not want it to happen again. Once more. In three hundred years. In a thousand. Each time he comes and goes."

"And if he does not go? If he stays?"

"Then the circle will break," said Nefer-Ka. "And the two Eyes will never become one." He paused. "And Egypt will live. Always."

"And he?"

Nefer-Ka gave me a long, heavy look.

"And he, my Pharaoh," he said quietly, "will cease to be what he was. Will become simply a man. Like all. Who will one day die." He paused. "Is it so terrible - to become simply a man?"

I did not answer. I did not know the answer.

Nefer-Ka turned to the door. At the threshold he stopped.

"In Luxor, my Pharaoh, on the last day of the festival," he said without turning, "in the inner court, I shall reveal to you what I myself read at twenty. All that is not written here. You will be able to choose. To go - or to stay." A pause. "I hope you will choose rightly."

"And if I choose not as you wish?"

Nefer-Ka slowly turned. His face was again immobile, calm, like the face of a stone god at a temple gate.

"Then, my Pharaoh," he said, "I shall have to choose for you."

He looked at Iti. Long. Then at Amenemope. Then again at me.

"Two words," he said. "'Keep' and 'time.' In one night." He inclined his head slightly. "Boy, you are cleverer than all the senior keepers of three hundred years. Take care of yourself. The clever in this city do not live long if they do not know when to fall silent."

And went out.

* * *

We were silent a long while after the door closed behind him.

Then Amenemope slowly, heavily lowered himself onto the stool where the High Priest had just sat. His hands, resting on the staff, still trembled.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "I have served in this Library sixty years. I have seen four High Priests." He paused. "This one is the first who spoke the truth."

"What truth, Amenemope?"

"That he is afraid," said the old man. "Not of you. Not of the throne. Not of the gods." He raised faded eyes to me. "He is afraid of what will be if you remember."

Iti stood at the table, clutching in his hands the small papyrus from the lower storehouse. His face was pale, but his eyes were not.

"My Pharaoh," he said quietly. "He said: 'The clever do not live long if they do not know when to fall silent.'"

"I heard."

"I shall not be silent," said Iti. "I shall read the rest. The whole seventh scroll. I shall find more words. However much time it takes." He paused. "Because if you must choose - to go or to stay - you must know what you are choosing from. Not only what he will say. Everything."

I looked at him. At this youth with the perpetual ink stain on his cheek who four years ago had been drowning in a channel, worrying about his board - and who now stood before me, pale, stubborn, clutching a papyrus for which he might be sent to where no letters are written.

"Iti," I said.

"Yes, my Pharaoh?"

"If I choose to go..."

He flinched. Looked at me.

"...will you write it down?" I asked. "All of it? As then, on the hunt? So that someone remembers?"

Iti was silent a long time. Then slowly, very slowly, nodded.

"I shall write it, my Pharaoh," he said. And his voice trembled. "But I hope I shall not have to." He lowered his eyes. "Because if you go - I do not know how to write on. All my scrolls are about you. Since that day on the hunt. All."

Amenemope in the shadow began to cough. Long, diligently. And turned away to the shelves.

* * *

Toward morning, when the seven scrolls of the Most Ancient Epic lay again in the ark under the golden wings of the goddesses, and the key again hung round Amenemope's neck, I climbed to the roof of the Library. Alone. No - not alone: Iti followed me, silently, and I did not send him away.

The sky over the capital was dark, but in the east it was already beginning to grey. Stars went out one by one - slowly, reluctantly, as though not wishing to go. Only in the north, high up, circled round their invisible axis the Imperishable - those that never set.

"Seneb said," I said, looking at them, "that his teacher believed: the Imperishable are not those who have gone. They are those who have not yet come. Who circle and await their hour."

"I remember, my Pharaoh," said Iti quietly. "I wrote it down."

"You were there?" I turned to him. "That night, on the island? When Seneb told me of the stars?"

Iti blushed - it was visible even in the pre-dawn greyness.

"I was not asleep, my Pharaoh. I lay behind the tents. I heard." He paused. "I wrote down everything. Every word. That same night. By the light of the fire. That board... I have it still. Mau fished it out of the water, remember? Afterward I wrote on it."

I looked at him. At the Imperishable stars above his head. At the grey, brightening sky.

"Will you show it to me someday?" I asked.

"I shall, my Pharaoh," said Iti. "When you remember."

"And if I do not remember?"

Iti looked at me - straight, long, without fear.

"Then I shall read it to you aloud," he said. "Every evening. Until you remember."

Over the eastern hills the rim of the sun appeared. Nut was giving birth to Ra - red, wet, enormous, as she had given birth every morning since the beginning of time. And somewhere below, in the temple garden, the baboons of Thoth cried out, greeting the sunrise. All at once.

Seven days remained until the new moon.
