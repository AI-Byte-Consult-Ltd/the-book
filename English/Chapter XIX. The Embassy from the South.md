Chapter XIX
The Embassy from the South

The giraffe entered the palace before the ambassador.

No one had planned this. According to the rules which Ani the Elder, keeper of ceremonies - not the deaf scribe of the council but his cousin, still more ancient and still more stern - had been reading out of scrolls for two days running, the embassy of the southern land was to enter the outer court solemnly, in strict order: heralds in front, then the ambassador, then the gifts, and only at the very end, behind the gifts, the animals. But the giraffe had not read the rules.

I stood on the balcony above the outer court, hidden by a linen awning - I wanted to see the embassy before it saw me - and I saw everything. I saw how into the opened gates there first peered timidly a small spotted head on an incredibly long neck, swaying at a height the spears of the guards could not reach. I saw how the head surveyed the court with an expression of majestic, almost regal bewilderment. And how, following the head, unhurriedly setting down its stilt-like legs, all the rest floated into the court - russet, with dark patches, like a map of unknown lands - dragging behind it on a long rope two desperately resisting drovers.

The guards at the gate shied away. A scribe with a basket of scrolls crashed onto his back, scattering papyri all over the court. The court burst into cries. The giraffe, not in the least abashed, walked straight to the old fig tree growing by the wall, lowered its head to the topmost branch - the one the gardeners had been unable to reach for years - and began unhurriedly to strip the leaves.

"My Pharaoh," came a dry, imperturbable voice behind me. "It seems the southerners have decided to begin negotiations with the most important thing. With refreshments."

Nebamun stood at the door to the balcony, with his inevitable counting-box under his arm, and looked down into the court with the expression with which he must look upon a fire in someone else's granary: without fear, but with lively professional interest.

"How much does a giraffe cost, Nebamun?" I asked, unable to restrain myself.

"A giraffe, my Pharaoh, costs exactly as much as the fig tree it is eating now," answered the keeper of the treasury. "Plus the gate, which will have to be widened if they want to lead it out again. Plus the scribe, who will now spend a week gathering up scrolls." He paused. "But if we speak of the price to the southerners - the giraffe costs more than all the rest of the embassy. A giraffe is not given. A giraffe is shown. So that all may see: they have beasts that we do not."

Below, at the gate, the heralds at last appeared - bewildered, out of breath, plainly having lost the giraffe on the way. Behind them, in a cloud of dust, to the bellow of bronze trumpets and the roll of drums, the embassy entered the court.

* * *

I had seen many embassies - or so it seemed to me; my memory, as always, gave up reluctantly what lay deeper than the last few moons. But this was like none of them.

First came the bearers - tall, dark-skinned, with shoulders gleaming with oil, with skin of such a deep, almost blue colour that the sun slid over it as over polished black wood. On their shoulders they carried gifts. Elephant tusks - enormous, curved like young crescent moons, yellow with age, each needing two bearers. Logs of ebony, heavy as stone. Leopard skins - dozens of skins, spotted, with heads and claws hanging at the sides. Sacks of incense, from which a thick, sweet, bitterish smell at once floated through the court, drowning even the smell of the giraffe. Baskets from which ostrich feathers and eggs the size of a human head peered out. And gold. Gold in ingots, in rings, in little bags, in the shape of small figures - birds, lions, snakes - heaped into open chests so carelessly, as though it had been brought not as a gift but for sale by weight.

After the bearers the animals were led. Besides the giraffe, which was still stripping the fig tree, paying not the slightest attention to protocol - two cheetahs on golden leashes, graceful, spotted, with black stripes from eye to muzzle as if painted tears. A cage with three baboons - grey, huge, in copper collars - at the sight of which the priests in the crowd by the wall began to whisper and make warding signs. And - quite unexpectedly - a little monkey with a long tail, sitting on the shoulder of one of the bearers and pelting everyone with date stones.

And then the ambassador appeared.

He came neither in a chariot nor in a litter - he came on foot, and that was the first thing that struck me. Tall, taller than any of his bearers, straight as a spear, about forty, with skin of the same deep, almost blue shade, his head shaved bare and crowned with a thin golden circlet from which a single golden pendant hung on his forehead - a small ram's horn. He was dressed more simply than any of my dignitaries: a white loincloth, a broad necklace of beads - blue, red, gold - and a leopard skin thrown over one shoulder. On his arms, from wrist to elbow, there was not a single bracelet. But on his face - on the cheekbones, the forehead, the chin - there were fine, delicate scars, as if cut with a chisel, composing a pattern that I could not read but that, I was sure, meant something.

He walked across the court looking neither at the giraffe, nor at the gathered onlookers, nor at the guard, nor at the baboons. He looked up. At the balcony. Straight at the linen awning behind which I was hiding.

And I understood that he knew I was there.

"Irikhet," Nebamun said quietly. "The son of the southern king's brother. They entrust him with the hardest negotiations. They say that last time, when there was a dispute between us over the border wells, he sat twelve days by the king's tent, eating and drinking nothing but water, until our former Pharaoh agreed to divide the wells in half." Nebamun paused. "The former Pharaoh said afterwards that he yielded not from weakness but because he could no longer watch a man starve himself on his threshold."

"And what do you think?"

"I think, my Pharaoh," said Nebamun, "that a man who can go twelve days without eating to gain a well is more dangerous than a man with an army."

* * *

Behind the ambassador, two paces back, came two more.

The first was a short, plump, fussy man in an Egyptian linen robe but with southern beads at his neck, who kept running ahead, looking round, whispering something to the ambassador, bowing to everyone - guards, priests, even, it seemed, the giraffe. The interpreter, I guessed.

And the second...

The second was an old woman. So old as I had never seen - even Merit by the temple pond would have seemed a girl beside her. Small, bent almost double, wrapped in brown cloth so that only her face could be seen - and the face was covered with the same fine patterned scars as the ambassador's, only denser, solid, from forehead to chin, so that the skin seemed carved like the lid of a casket. In her hand she held a staff - long, taller than herself, of white wood, crowned with a carved lioness's head.

She walked slowly, leaning on the staff, and looked at nothing around her. Her eyes were closed. She was blind, I understood - or pretended to be blind. And yet, when she came level with the balcony, she stopped. And raised her face. And opened her eyes.

The eyes were white. Entirely. Without pupils. Like two pieces of ivory.

And they looked straight at me.

"Who is that?" I whispered.

Nebamun did not answer. For the first time in all the time I had known him, the keeper of the treasury did not know the answer.

* * *

The reception was set for noon, in the great throne room - and by noon the hall was fuller than it had ever been, it seemed.

On the right of the throne - Senusret, in full armour, with a heavy bronze sword at his belt, his face stone and impenetrable. Behind him - Paser, leaning on his staff, grey, watchful. Djehuty, just back from the southern outposts, dusty, sunburned to blackness, with a scar on his cheek standing out still more sharply against the weathered skin. Khaemuas - in his costliest bracelets, in his finest linen, smiling at everyone. Vizier Ptahhotep - with a basket of scrolls at his feet. Nebamun - with his box. Merira - with tattooed arms crossed on his chest. And at the far wall, in the shadow of a column, almost unnoticed - Panehsy, commander of the Medjay, in a leather baldric with throwing sticks.

On the left - the priests. Bakenren, round, shaven, radiant. Behind him half a dozen senior priests in white. And - for the first time since the evening when he had spoken to me of the scales and of the death of the junior priest in Luxor - Nefer-Ka himself.

He stood straight as an obelisk, in a white robe, with a staff crowned by the sacred Eye, and did not look at me. He looked at the doors. At the place from which the embassy was to enter. And on his face was an expression I could not read - not alarm, not expectation, but something like the patience of a predator at a watering place.

At the foot of the throne, among the scribes, sat Iti. I had myself told Amenemope to send him - "the best hand in the Library," I had said before everyone, loudly, so that the ears with bracelets would hear: the boy was needed as a scribe, no more. Iti sat with his head bent over the papyrus, brush at the ready, and never once raised his eyes to me. Only as I sat down on the throne did I notice that his cheek was clean. Not a single ink stain. He had evidently washed with particular care before the reception. And from this, I do not know why, I felt a little sad.

* * *

The ambassador entered alone. Without the interpreter, without the old woman, without the bearers. He stopped in the middle of the hall, in a ray of light from the high window, so that the golden circlet on his shaven head flared. He did not bow. He drew himself up still straighter. And spoke.

In our language. Clearly, slowly, with a singsong southern accent, but without a single mistake.

"Son of Ra, lord of the Two Lands. My uncle, king of the land beyond the cataracts, lord of Ta-Seti, sends you greeting. And gifts. And a question."

A whisper ran through the hall. The ambassador did not speak through an interpreter. The ambassador knew our language - and did not hide it, but displayed it from his first words.

"The greeting is received," I said. "The gifts are received." I paused. "The giraffe is received too. Although, I fear, my garden will have a hard time of it."

Someone in the hall snorted - Merira, it seemed. The ambassador did not smile. But something in his face, at the corners of his lips, trembled for an instant.

"A giraffe eats only the upper branches, son of Ra," he said. "The lower ones it leaves to those of lesser height. With us they say: be like the giraffe. Take what others cannot reach. And do not touch what they need more."

"A wise proverb," I said. "And what question does your uncle send me?"

Irikhet held a pause - a long one, so that the silence in the hall drew taut like a bowstring.

"My uncle asks," he said slowly, distinctly, "who rules in your land, son of Ra. You - or those who feed our people with your bread?"

* * *

The hall exploded.

Djehuty leapt up, seizing his sword. Khaemuas paled until he was whiter than his linen. The priests at the left wall stirred, whispered. Bakenren stopped radiating - his face went slack all at once, like dough. Senusret stepped forward, shielding the throne. The scribes at the foot of the throne threw down their brushes.

Only two in the hall did not move. Nefer-Ka - by the left wall. And Iti - at the foot of the throne, bent over the papyrus so low that I saw only the back of his head, and the brush in his hand moved evenly, swiftly, recording every word.

"Silence," I said. Not loudly. But the hall fell silent.

I looked at the ambassador. He looked at me.

"Explain your question, Irikhet," I said.

"I will explain." The ambassador turned - not to me, to the hall, as if addressing all at once. "For three moons we have been receiving bread. Egyptian bread. Barley, emmer, wheat. In sacks. The sacks come on donkeys, by secret paths, across the desert, to our border chiefs. They are brought by men in white robes. With shaven heads." He paused. "They say: this is a gift from those who will soon speak to you in the name of Egypt in place of Pharaoh. They say: take the bread, and for the bread do what we say. Burn the border villages. Let the people of Egypt see that Pharaoh cannot protect them."

No one in the hall breathed.

"My chiefs took the bread," said Irikhet. "Because we have drought too. Not like yours, but drought. My chiefs burned your villages. Because a hungry chief does what the one who feeds him commands." He turned to me. "I do not ask forgiveness for this, son of Ra. I have come to tell you the truth. Because my uncle is a king, not a chief. And a king does not want his chiefs to serve foreign priests. He wants to know: with whom is he to speak? With you - or with them?"

Bakenren stepped forward.

"My Pharaoh!" His voice, always so oily, broke into a shriek. "This is a lie! The southerner lies! The Temple has never..."

"The sacks," said Irikhet, without turning his head, "were marked. With a sign. A triangle, point downward. Beneath it - three waves." He at last looked at Bakenren. "Shall I draw you this sign, priest? Or do you remember it?"

Bakenren opened his mouth. Closed it. Stepped back a pace - and bumped against a column with his back.

I did not look at Bakenren. I looked at Nefer-Ka.

The High Priest stood just as straight. His face did not change. Only one thing in him changed - I noticed it because I was looking for it: the fingers of his right hand, resting on the staff, slowly clenched. So that the knuckles whitened.

And then he spoke. Calmly. Softly. In that same ingratiating, resonant voice with which he spoke to the crowd outside the Temple.

"My Pharaoh. The southerner is right in one thing. The bread of Egypt has gone south." Nefer-Ka made a pause - exactly long enough for the whole hall to catch its breath. "It was stolen. From the Temple granaries. By thieves in white robes who passed themselves off as priests. The Temple has long known of this. The Temple is seeking them." He turned to Bakenren, and steel sounded in his voice. "Bakenren. You were responsible for the granaries. You reported to me that they were empty after the distribution to the people. How then did it happen that their grain turned up beyond the cataracts?"

Bakenren looked at the High Priest as a rabbit looks at a snake. His round face went grey as ash.

"I... I... High One..." he stammered.

"We shall speak of this in the Temple," said Nefer-Ka gently. "Later." He turned to me again and bowed - exactly as far as custom required. "My Pharaoh. The Temple will find the thieves. The Temple will punish the guilty. The Temple thanks the honourable ambassador for opening our eyes."

He stepped back to the wall. Bakenren remained standing by the column - alone, in the middle of the hall, under the gaze of a hundred eyes.

I watched this and understood that I had just seen a snake shed its skin. How the High Priest, without blinking, had thrown his closest servant to be devoured - to save himself. And that Bakenren had understood this too.

"The Temple will find the thieves," I said slowly. "Of course." I paused. "And while the Temple searches - the throne will take up the search as well. Panehsy."

The commander of the Medjay came soundlessly out of the shadow of the column.

"The Temple granaries," I said. "All twelve. Seal them with the royal seal. Today. Before sunset. No one removes a grain without Nebamun's knowledge." I looked at Nefer-Ka. "So that the thieves, if they are still there, do not carry off the rest. For the good of the Temple."

Nefer-Ka bowed his head. His face was impenetrable.

"For the good of the Temple, my Pharaoh," he repeated.

* * *

After the reception the ambassador asked to speak with me alone.

Senusret was against it. Paser too. Even Nebamun pursed his lips in disapproval. But I agreed - and Irikhet came to my chambers at sunset, alone, unarmed, with only a leopard skin on his shoulder.

He stood a long time at the window, looking at the city, at the river, at the scorched fields beyond the river. Then he said, without turning:

"Shall I tell you a tale, son of Ra? Ours, southern. It is told to children among us. And among you, I have heard, it is told to priests. Differently."

"Tell it."

Irikhet turned to me.

"'Long ago, when the gods still walked the earth, the god of the Sun had an Eye. Not an eye - the Eye. A daughter. A lioness. Fiery. She was his strength, his wrath, his protection. But once the god of the Sun offended her. With what, no one remembers now. And the Eye left him. Went south. To our land. Far, beyond the cataracts, into the desert. And there she became wild. She roamed the sands as a lioness, killed all she met, drank blood, and her eyes burned like fire. And the god of the Sun without his Eye grew weak. Became old. His land dried up. The rivers shallowed.'"

He paused.

"'And then the god of the Sun sent for her. He sent his scribe - the one who knows all the words, the one who gathers what has been torn apart. The ibis-headed one. He came into the desert, to the wild lioness, and sat down before her. And she wanted to kill him. And he began to tell her tales. One after another. About her father. About her home. About the river that missed her. About the temples where songs were sung to her. And he told them so long, and so well, that the lioness stopped growling. Lay down. Wept. And her tears fell on the sand and became water. And then the scribe said: "Let us go home." And she went. All the way north he told her tales, so that she would not change her mind. And when she entered Egypt - the river rose. Overflowed. And everything turned green.'"

Irikhet fell silent. The room had grown dark - the sun had set.

"Drought," I said slowly. "Three years without the flood."

"Three years," the ambassador nodded. "Our old men say: the Eye has gone away again. To the south. To us. Or - not quite to us. Somewhere from which it must be brought back. And until it is brought back, the river will not rise." He looked at me directly, unblinking. "Old Nanakhat - you saw her, the blind one with the lioness staff - she says that the Eye will return when the one who carried it away comes. Because only the one who carried it away knows the way back."

"Who carried it away?"

Irikhet was silent.

"Nanakhat says - you, son of Ra," he said at last. "Not this you. Another. The former. The one who was Pharaoh before you. Or the one you were before you became Pharaoh. She says it is the same." He shrugged. "I am not a priest. I do not understand this. I convey what she said."

Cold under the ribs. On the left. Familiar.

"What else did she say?"

"She said she wants to see you. Alone. Without guards. Without priests. Tomorrow. At dawn. By the river." Irikhet went toward the door. At the threshold he turned. "And she also said to give you a riddle. She said you must solve it before the meeting. Otherwise there is no need to meet."

"What riddle?"

The ambassador said it slowly, carefully, as if afraid to distort a single word:

"'Two went out of one house. One went to the sunrise, the other to the sunset. They walked all their lives and never once looked back. And when they came to the edge of the world - they met. Face to face. Where did they meet?'"

He went out without waiting for an answer.

* * *

Djehuty came to me late at night. Without announcement, as Khemu once had - Senusret had evidently let him through himself, because he knew what he was coming with.

He stood at the door - dusty, tired, with a weathered face on which the scar across the cheek seemed still sharper - and held in his hands something wrapped in coarse canvas.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "I should have reported this in council. But Khaemuas was in the council. And the priests." He paused. "I did not tell you everything about the southern outposts."

"Speak."

Djehuty unwrapped the canvas. Inside was a fragment of stone - flat, grey, the size of two palms, with uneven, fresh edges, as if it had been recently knocked off a cliff.

"At the last outpost, right above the cataracts," he said, "there is a rock. Old. It is called the Rock of the First Ones. Herdsmen give it a wide berth. My soldiers were afraid too, but I ordered them to keep watch on the summit, one sees the desert better from there." Djehuty paused. "On the third night the watchman ran to me, shaking. Said the rock was glowing. I climbed up myself." He laid the stone on the table. "It was not glowing. But there were signs on it. Carved. Very old - older than anything I had seen. Lichen had almost eaten them. But that night, by the moon, they could be seen. I knocked off a piece. This one."

I bent over the stone. Brought the lamp near.

On the grey, weathered stone were signs. Not hieroglyphs. Long, flowing, curving lines running one into another without breaks. The same script. The same as on the papyrus Iti found. The same as on the stones in the chest of black wood - if Khemu was not mistaken.

"There are many of them," Djehuty said quietly. "The whole rock. From top to bottom. As if someone had written a whole scroll on it." He looked at me. "I am not a priest, my Pharaoh. I am a soldier. I do not believe in omens. But my soldiers after that night refused to stand watch on the rock. All of them. Even those who went with me against the southerners with one spear to three men." He paused. "And one more thing. Under the rock, in the sand, we found tracks. Many tracks. Fresh. Someone had been digging there. Searching for something. Recently. At night. Men in sandals. In good ones, temple sandals."

"The Temple is searching for something on the Rock of the First Ones," I said.

"It looks that way, my Pharaoh." Djehuty drew himself up. "I ask your word. Let me go back south. I will take my best men. I will set a guard at the rock. I will let no one near - neither priests, nor southerners, nor the High One himself. Until you decide what to do with it."

I looked at this hard, abrupt man, who at the first council had demanded a campaign beyond the cataracts and argued with me before everyone - and thought that Paser had been right: Djehuty simply needed a task.

"Go," I said. "Take the best. And, Djehuty..." He turned. "Thank you for bringing this to me. And not to the council."

Djehuty was silent. Then - for the first time - he smiled, and the scar on his cheek folded into a real smile and not the eternal frozen smirk.

"Old Paser told me, my Pharaoh," he answered, "that I am young because so far I have been lucky. I thought: let the luck go on. But not for me alone."

* * *

Paney came after him - almost running, barefoot, eyes blazing, out of breath. Behind him, unable to keep up, came Bata, and the guardsman's face was anxious.

"My Pharaoh!" the boy blurted, falling to his knees. "My Pharaoh, I saw him!"

"Whom, Paney?"

"Him!" The boy was gasping. "That one! The one who burned! In Little Nekhen! Who..." His voice broke. "Who set fire to our house. When Father told me to run. I saw his face. In the light of the fire. I remember it. I will remember it all my life."

"Where did you see him?"

"Here!" Paney thrust his hand toward the window, toward the place beyond the palace walls, in the outer court, where the embassy's bearers had pitched their tents. "Among the southerners! Among those who carried the tusks! He is there! He was laughing and drinking beer by the fire!"

Bata, who had come up behind him, nodded to me over the boy's head.

"It's true, my Pharaoh. The boy ran to me, trembling. I went to look. Tall, with a scar through his eyebrow, an earring in his ear. Sitting by a fire among the bearers." Bata paused. "Only he is no bearer, my Pharaoh. A bearer has calluses on his hands from tusks. This one's are from a spear."

I was silent for a long time.

Irikhet had come to tell the truth. Irikhet had spoken of the bread and of the chiefs fed by priests. Irikhet had come alone, unarmed. And among his people by the fire sat the man who had burned Little Nekhen.

Did Irikhet know it? Or not? Or had someone placed this man in his embassy - as a foundling is laid in a stranger's cradle?

"Paney," I said at last. "You are a brave boy. You did everything right. Now listen. You will not go near the tents again. Not today, not tomorrow. You will tell no one what you saw. Not Iti, not Bata, not Hesira. No one but me. Do you understand?"

Paney nodded, biting his lip.

"Bata. Find Panehsy. Tell him - a man with a scar through his eyebrow and an earring. Let the Medjay never take their eyes off him. But not touch him. Let them watch whom he talks to."

Bata went out. Paney remained standing in the middle of the room - small, trembling, with eyes in which there were no more tears, only fire. The very fire in which his house had burned.

"My Pharaoh," he said quietly. "When you catch him - may I look at him? Just look. So that he sees I am alive."

"You may, Paney," I said. "I promise."

* * *

Iti found me in the garden after midnight - by the fig tree, under the jasmine, where we had met last time. He brought a scroll - the record of the reception, made by his hand. Word for word.

"I thought you would need it, my Pharaoh," he said, holding out the scroll. "The official record will be with Ani. And Ani..." he hesitated, "half of Ani's words will be 'repeat for the record.'"

I took the scroll. Unrolled it. Iti's handwriting - even, quick, flawless. Every word of the ambassador. Every word of Nefer-Ka. Every pause marked with a dot.

And at the bottom, in the margin, small, in quite a different, hurried hand - a note:

"Nefer-Ka clenched his staff when the ambassador named the sign. Bakenren did not know that Nefer-Ka would give him up. Nefer-Ka knew beforehand. He came to the reception already decided."

I raised my eyes.

"You noticed this?"

"I was watching him, my Pharaoh," said Iti. "All the time. While I wrote." He paused. "I can write without looking at the papyrus. Isiret taught me. She says a true scribe looks at the one who speaks, not at what he writes. Because words lie, but the face does not."

"And you did not look at me once," I said. "The whole reception."

Iti froze. Then lowered his eyes.

"You said, my Pharaoh - before everyone, so that the ears with bracelets would hear - that you needed me only as a scribe." His voice was even, but I saw his fingers tremble. "And I was a scribe. A good one. The most unnoticed. I looked at the priests, at the ambassador, at Khaemuas, at Bakenren." He paused. "And if I had looked at you, everyone would have seen."

"Seen what?"

Iti did not answer. He looked at the jasmine. Then at the scroll in my hands.

"There is something more there, my Pharaoh," he said instead of answering. "On the back. I do not know whether it is important."

I turned the scroll over. On the back, in the corner, there was drawn - swiftly, precisely, with a fine brush - a face. An old woman. Blind. With patterned scars all over her face. And beside the face - a sign. One of the patterns of her scars, redrawn separately, larger.

A long, flowing, curving line. Running into another. Without a break.

"This is on her forehead," Iti said quietly. "Right above the brows. I looked at her as she crossed the court. I redrew it." He raised his eyes to me. "My Pharaoh. It is the same sign. As on the papyrus from the lower storehouse. Third from the left in the first line. I remember it. I looked at it all night."

* * *

I did not sleep. Until dawn I sat by the window, holding three things in my hands: Iti's scroll, Djehuty's fragment of stone and the papyrus from the lower storehouse. Three pieces of one riddle. The script on the papyrus, the script on the rock at the cataracts, the script on the forehead of the blind southern old woman.

And I thought over the riddle she had sent me.

"Two went out of one house. One went to the sunrise, the other to the sunset. They walked all their lives without looking back. And when they came to the edge of the world - they met. Face to face. Where did they meet?"

If one goes toward the sunrise, turning nowhere, and goes one's whole life... And if the other goes toward the sunset...

They will meet only if the world is not flat. If it is a circle. If the edge of the world in the east and the edge of the world in the west are one and the same place.

They met where they had set out from. At home.

I remembered a riddle I had never heard. About stones - day and night. One keeps, the other leads. He who holds both stands in the doorway. Where he looks - there is home. I did not know how I knew it. But it came by itself and stood beside the old woman's riddle, like two halves of one potsherd.

The sky in the east was beginning to turn grey. I rose, threw on a dark cloak - this time I did not forget the sandals either: plain, worn-down ones, borrowed from Bata - and went out into the garden.

At the gate Senusret was waiting for me. Dressed as a donkey-driver, with a staff instead of a sword - but the sword, I knew, was under his cloak.

"You are going to the river, my Pharaoh," he said. Not a question.

"She said - alone."

"I shall not go to her," said Senusret. "I shall stand behind the palms, fifty paces off. She will not see me." He paused. "And if she does - I shall say I am herding donkeys."

"You have no donkeys, Senusret."

"Then I am looking for them," said the commander of the army imperturbably. "Donkeys are like that. Always getting lost."

I laughed - quietly, for the first time in that endless night. And we went to the river together, in the grey pre-dawn gloom, past the sleeping tents of the embassy, past the giraffe, dozing on its feet by the stripped fig tree - to where on the bank of the shallowed Nile, on a flat stone at the very water's edge, motionless as a statue, sat a small bent figure with a staff crowned by a lioness's head.

She did not turn when I approached. She sat facing south, toward the cataracts, toward the desert, and her white, sightless eyes were open.

"Where did they meet?" she asked. In our language. In a creaking, ancient voice like the rustle of sand on stone.

I sat down on the stone beside her.

"At home," I said. "Where they set out from. Because the world is a circle."

The old woman was silent for a long time. The river lapped quietly at our feet - shallow, weary, almost still.

"Right," she said at last. "But that is only half the answer. The second you will tell me when you return."

"Return from where?"

Nanakhat turned her face to me - carved, ancient, in patterns of scars - and her white eyes rested on me as if they saw through me. And not only me. The one who was before me. And the one who would come after.

"From Luxor, son of Ra," she said. "If you return."

* * *

The sun rose over the eastern hills. On the far bank, in the fields, the first birds began to sing. Somewhere far off, in the palace, the baboons cried out - all three at once, greeting the sunrise, as they have greeted it, they say, since the beginning of time.

Nanakhat spoke for a long time. I listened.

She spoke of the lioness who had gone south - and of how her people believe that the Eye leaves not out of anger but because there is no one to hold it. Of the rock at the cataracts, which her people call not the Rock of the First Ones but the Rock of Waiting - because it is written upon it that one day someone will come for it. Of the two Eyes, which must be together but always turn out to be in different hands. Of how the drought will end when one hand takes both. Of how in the Temple, in the chest of black wood, not everything lies. That half of what is needed is by the river. And half is with the one who came without memory.

"I do not understand," I said.

"I know," said Nanakhat. "And you need not. Yet. Understanding comes like the flood: at first the water rises quietly, and no one notices. And then - in the morning - everything around is already green."

She rose, leaning on the lioness staff. Small, bent double, ancient.

"The ambassador will leave in seven days," she said. "I shall leave with him. But before that you will come to me once more. When you find what is sought on the Rock." She paused. "And beware of the man with the scar through his eyebrow. He is not ours. He was put into our caravan like a snake into a basket of dates. Irikhet does not know. I know. But I am a blind old woman. Who will listen to a blind old woman?"

"I will listen," I said.

Nanakhat smiled - toothlessly, anciently, as a stone smiles over which water has run for a thousand years.

"You have always listened," she said. "Even last time."

And she went off along the bank, tapping her staff - to where behind the palms, clumsily playing a drover who had lost his donkeys, stood Senusret. Passing him, she stopped, turned her sightless face to him and said something - briefly, creakingly. Senusret flinched so that his staff fell from his hands.

When she had gone, I went up to him.

"What did she say to you?"

The commander of the army was silent for a long while, picking up his staff from the sand.

"She said," he spoke at last, dully, not looking at me, "'The donkeys will be found, warrior. But the one you guard you cannot keep safe in Luxor. If you go after him there - stay at the gates. Otherwise you will both not return.'"

Over the river the sun was rising - enormous, red, merciless. The Nile lay at our feet as a thin, shallow ribbon amid cracked mud. Less than two moons remained until the festival of Opet.

And we both - Pharaoh and his old warrior - stood for a long time on the bank in silence, daring neither to say to each other what we were thinking, nor to turn back.
