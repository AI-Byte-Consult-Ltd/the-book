Chapter X
The Council of Commanders

Even before the sun had risen above the eastern hills, I went out into the inner courtyard - alone, without a retinue, as I went out more and more often in the last days, when sleep refused to come and my bed began to seem a cage.

The courtyard was empty. Almost. At its far end, by the colonnade, where in the shade lay the rolled-up mats of the guard, two men were practising with spears.

More precisely, one was practising - the boy Paney, the very one who had survived the burned Little Nekhen. His arm, still bandaged at the elbow with clean linen, held a short training spear without a point - simply a smooth stick of acacia - and opposite him, crouching, stood Bata, the young guardsman who had accompanied me unrecognised to the prayer service. Bata made a thrust - slowly, deliberately slowly - and Paney parried it, biting his lip, with such a concentrated, furious face as though before him were not a cheerful fellow with dimples in his cheeks but that very southerner who had set fire to his home.

"Not like that," said Bata, stopping. "You look at the spear. Do not look at the spear. Look here." He tapped himself on the chest with a finger. "The spear lies. The shoulder does not lie. Where the shoulder goes - there the blow goes."

"And if he is left-handed?" Paney asked hoarsely.

"Then look at the other shoulder," Bata laughed. "Come, again."

They did not see me. I stood in the shade of a column and watched the boy parry slow, ungrudging blows time after time, and how with each time his movements became a little surer, a little more exact - and his face, at first stone, thawed little by little, until, on the seventh or eighth thrust, Paney suddenly parried Bata's spear so deftly that it flew out of the guardsman's hand and rolled clattering across the flagstones.

Bata stared at his empty palm. Paney stared at Bata. And then the boy - for the first time since the day I had seen him in the throne room - laughed. Briefly, uncertainly, as though he had unlearned it in these days and was now recalling how it is done.

"Did you see?" said Bata, picking up the spear and not in the least offended. "Senusret himself will come to learn from you in a year."

"He will not come," objected a low voice behind me, and I started. Inu, the senior guardsman, stood two paces behind my back - so quietly that I had not heard him come. "Senusret learns from no one. But he will come to watch."

He bowed to me - briefly, in the soldier's way - and, after a pause, added in an undertone:

"The boy comes here every morning, my Pharaoh. Before dawn. Bata volunteered himself to teach him. Says he had a younger brother just like him. Died in the last famine."

I looked at them for a long time - at the young guardsman and at the boy with the bandaged arm - and it occurred to me that of everything I had done in these weeks, of all the decrees, distributions and counsels, perhaps precisely this - a boy laughing in a courtyard at dawn - was the one thing that had truly succeeded.

The council assembled at dawn, in the Small Hall, the very one where my predecessors - if one believed the Most Ancient Epic and the few scrolls that survived in the Library - had for centuries decided the fates of campaigns, made peace with neighbours and declared wars about which songs were afterward composed. The walls of the hall were painted with scenes of ancient victories: a Pharaoh in a chariot trampling vanquished enemies; files of prisoners with hands bound behind their backs; barques laden with tribute from distant borders. The colours had faded with time, but in the slanting rays of the morning sun that pierced the narrow windows beneath the ceiling these figures seemed almost alive - and almost mocking, as if asking me whether I would manage to add to them even one page worthy of the wall.

The commanders entered one after another, and each bowed before me as men bow who have long been accustomed to the gesture and have long ceased to put into it anything but form.

First was Djehuty - commander of the southern outposts, tall, dry, as if carved of the same dark wood that went into the shafts of his spearmen. It was said of him that he had never once retreated a step before the enemy and never once forgiven a subordinate for retreating. A scar across the whole of his left cheek, received, by rumour, in a skirmish with brigands at the cataracts, gave his face the expression of an eternal, frozen sneer.

After him came Paser - the oldest of all, grey, heavy, leaning on a staff less from necessity than from habit. He had once commanded the chariot army of my predecessor, and by rumour still remembered by name every horse that had fallen under him in battle. Nowadays he advised more than he commanded - but his counsels were valued even by those who did not like to hear them.

Third was Khaemuas - young, dapper, in bracelets that jingled at every movement, a scion of a noble family of the Delta, who had received his rank, as they whispered at court, rather thanks to his father's connections than his own merits. He smiled too readily and too often - with the smile that serves equally for flattery and for betrayal.

Senusret entered last and took the place at my right hand - not because custom demanded it, but because I myself had indicated that place to him, and all present noticed it.

Besides the commanders, at my word there had assembled in the hall those without whom no decision about the army would be worth the papyrus on which it is recorded.

The Vizier Ptahhotep - stooped, careworn, with the eternal basket of scrolls at his feet, from which the edges of reports stuck out like feathers from a pillow. The keeper of the treasury Nebamun - spare, motionless, with a counting tablet on his knees, like a heron watching for fish. By the wall, on a low bench, perched the old scribe of the council Ani - deaf in one ear, for which reason he sat with his head constantly cocked to one side and kept asking in a loud whisper: "Repeat for the record?" - to the quiet fury of all present except me.

Merira, commander of the river barques, lumbered into the hall as onto a deck in a storm - broad, squat, tanned to blackness, with arms covered from wrist to shoulder with blue tattoos: fish, lotuses, eyes, crocodiles - a chronicle of all the rivers on which he had sailed. His voice was such as if he had spent his whole life shouting down the wind - and, apparently, so it was.

"My Pharaoh!" he thundered, bowing. "Forgive me for smelling of silt. My barques stand in the Nile up to their bellies, like ducks on ice, and for the third day I have myself been pushing them with the oarsmen."

And last - soundlessly, so that I noticed him only when he was already standing at the door - entered Panehsy, commander of the Medjay, the desert guard. Tall, thin, with skin the colour of dark wood and with ritual scars on his cheekbones - three thin stripes on each side, the mark of his southern people, who had long served the Pharaohs as guides and guardians of the desert roads. He wore neither bracelets nor necklaces - only a leather baldric with throwing sticks and at his belt a knife in a gazelle-skin sheath. He bowed silently, touching his palm to his brow, and stood by the wall, arms folded. In all the time I had known him I did not remember Panehsy ever speaking first.

And then, when the servants were already about to shut the doors, one more man entered the hall, whom I had not summoned.

"Peace be upon you, my Pharaoh," he said, bowing lower than all the commanders put together - with that emphasised, almost mocking courtesy which in itself sounded like a challenge. "The High Priest, grieving that urgent temple affairs do not allow him to be present in person, begs that you accept me as his voice at this council."

Bakenren. The second man of the Temple after Nefer-Ka himself - small, rotund, with a smoothly shaven head gleaming in the morning light, and with eyes in which one could never read anything but impeccably calibrated respect.

"The council of commanders has no need of the voice of the Temple," said Senusret before I could answer, and steel rang in his voice.

"The council of commanders," Bakenren objected gently, "decides how to feed the army in an hour of famine. And the grain on which the army feeds, as the esteemed Senusret knows, lies half in the temple granaries. Is it not wise to hear him who has the bread?"

I raised a hand, stopping Senusret, who was already drawing breath to answer.

"Let him stay," I said. "But let him remember that he sits here by my grace and not by right."

Bakenren bowed still lower, so that his smooth head almost touched his knees - and took his place by the far wall, under a faded painting depicting a file of bound prisoners. I could not help thinking that he had chosen that place by no means by chance.

* * *

"Let us begin with the chief matter," I said when all were seated. "Senusret has reported to me a murmur in the detachments. I wish to hear from each of you - how deep it is and what causes it."

Djehuty spoke first, not waiting for anyone else to take the word.

"The murmur is caused by the warriors sitting without work and without bread, my Pharaoh," he said sharply, with that frankness that bordered on insolence. "And a warrior without work and without bread begins to think. And a thinking warrior is the worst warrior in the world."

A short laugh ran through the hall; however, only Khaemuas laughed.

"And what do you propose?" I asked.

"To give them work," answered Djehuty, leaning forward, and the scar on his cheek seemed to tighten, making the sneer still more distinct. "The southern country has for the third year troubled our outposts. They burn villages, drive off cattle, and then flee beyond the cataracts, where we cannot reach them. But they have what we have not: their lands are not scorched by drought as ours are. They have grain. They have cattle. A campaign south will feed the army better than all the temple granaries put together - and at the same time teach our neighbours to respect our borders."

"War," said Paser slowly, leaning with both hands on his staff, "in an hour of drought is like putting out a fire with oil. I have seen such campaigns, Djehuty. I have seen how a hungry army goes beyond the cataracts - and how it returns from there half the size, without booty, with sand instead of bread in its sacks. The southerners will not wait for us at their granaries. They will drive their cattle into the hills and burn their fields themselves before we reach them."

"You are old, Paser," Djehuty threw out, without turning. "The old always see only defeats."

"I am old," agreed Paser, not in the least offended, "because in my time I have seen enough defeats to learn to avoid them. You are young because so far you have been lucky."

"Enough," I said before Djehuty could answer. "Khaemuas. You are silent."

Khaemuas smiled - with that same smile that served for everything - and made his bracelets jingle, adjusting the folds of his costly linen garment.

"I listen to the wise, my Pharaoh," he said. "And I think that both are right. A campaign south is dangerous, that is true. But to sit idle while the warriors murmur is no less dangerous. Perhaps we should seek a third way?"

"Which?" I asked.

Khaemuas slid a quick glance to the far wall - to where, under the painting with the prisoners, Bakenren sat - and that glance, fleeting, almost unnoticeable, told me more than all his subsequent words.

"To come to terms with the Temple, my Pharaoh," he said softly. "The Temple has grain. The army has strength. If these two hands acted in concert..."

"They do act in concert," Senusret interrupted, and his voice now sounded such that even Djehuty turned his head. "Under the hand of the Pharaoh. Or do you propose otherwise, Khaemuas?"

The hall grew very quiet. One could hear water-carriers calling to one another somewhere beyond the walls, in the inner courtyard - the last who still had anything to carry.

Khaemuas held Senusret's gaze for no longer than an instant, then laughed - lightly, carelessly, as if all that had been said were merely an unsuccessful joke.

"I propose only what pleases the Pharaoh, esteemed Senusret. As do all of us."

* * *

"Nebamun," I said, to break this silence. "What does the army cost us now?"

The keeper of the treasury did not look at his tablet - he seemed to remember every figure by heart.

"In the capital and its environs stand four thousand spears, my Pharaoh. To each, two loaves and a jug of beer a day. That is little for a warrior but much for the granaries. If we add the southern outposts and Ankhtifi's men, to whom you gave five hundred sacks - the royal stores will suffice to feed the army to the end of the season. And then," he shrugged his dry shoulders, "the army will have to be fed with promises."

"With promises an army is never fed," Djehuty growled.

"Repeat for the record?" Ani the scribe whispered loudly, his head on one side.

"He said that one cannot be fed on promises, esteemed Ani," Ptahhotep answered patiently, not raising his eyes from his basket.

"Ah!" said Ani and diligently scratched with his reed brush. "Wise."

Merira guffawed so that the cups on the little table shook.

"Merira," I said. "And what of the river?"

The commander of the barques stopped laughing.

"The river, my Pharaoh, is dying." He spread his tattooed hands. "I can take a light boat from the capital to the cataracts if the oarsmen every hundred cubits leap into the water and push it. But I will not carry the army by river. War barques sit deep. They will stick on the first shoal. And the cargo ones with grain also. If bread must be carried south - carry it on asses. If warriors must be led - lead them on foot."

"And the festival?" Khaemuas suddenly asked. "The Opet festival is in two moons. The god's barques must go south. Will they pass?"

All turned to him. Khaemuas smiled, jingling his bracelets.

"I only think of piety," he said. "If the god's barque runs aground, the people will take it for an ill omen."

Merira scratched his shaven head.

"The god's barque is light, they will tow it on ropes," he said reluctantly. "It will pass. It will keep grounding, but it will pass. And who guards it - that is no affair of mine, that is the affair of the esteemed Senusret."

"Of course," said Khaemuas gently. "I only asked."

Senusret gave him a long, heavy look. Khaemuas held it - this time longer than the first.

I remembered that question. Not even the question itself - but the ease, the feigned casualness with which it was asked. As one throws a bone to a dog to check whether it is hungry.

* * *

The doors flew open without a knock.

The guards at the entrance seized their spears, but the man who burst into the hall did not even glance at them. He was covered with dust from head to foot - dust baked by sweat into a grey crust - so thickly that it was impossible to make out either the colour of his clothes or the features of his face. He took three steps, staggered and fell to his knees, almost striking his forehead on the flagstones.

"A messenger... from the nomarch Ankhtifi..." he rasped. "My Pharaoh..."

"Water for him!" I cried.

Panehsy was first beside him - in one noiseless movement, as if he had flowed across the hall - and held his gourd flask to the messenger's lips. The latter drank for a long time, choking, spilling water on his chest. Then he straightened - as far as he could.

"Five nights ago," he began, "the southerners came again. To the wells at Dry Acacia, a day's journey from Little Nekhen. Forty spears, perhaps more. But this time..." He drew breath, and in the dusty face flickered something like triumph. "This time they were awaited. The three hundred spears you gave Ankhtifi, my Pharaoh. We met them at the wells. We fought until dawn. The southerners fled. Twelve remained lying. One was taken alive."

A murmur ran through the hall. Djehuty struck his knee with his fist.

"There!" he exclaimed. "That is what steel on the border means!"

"It means," Paser corrected quietly, "that you were wrong, and the Pharaoh was right. Three hundred spears on the border did what a thousand beyond the cataracts would not have done."

Djehuty opened his mouth - and shut it. The scar on his cheek twitched.

"What of the prisoner?" I asked the messenger.

"He is silent, my Pharaoh. Ankhtifi ordered him not to be touched until your word. But on him..." the messenger reached into his bosom, into the folds of his dirty, sweat-soaked shirt, and drew out something small, clenched in his fist. "Ankhtifi ordered me to give this into your hands. He said - you would understand."

He opened his fist.

On his palm, on a cord of twisted papyrus, lay a scarab. Small, blue, of shining faience.

The hall grew so quiet that I heard somewhere in the courtyard Bata's and Paney's training spears still knocking against one another.

All eyes - one by one, slowly, as though drawn by an invisible thread - turned to the far wall. To the painting of the bound prisoners. To the small rotund man with the smoothly shaven head who had all this time sat silently under it.

Bakenren did not stir. Not a muscle moved in his face.

"Such amulets are sold in every market of the Two Lands, my Pharaoh," he said evenly, with impeccable respect. "Two copper rings apiece. Half the Delta wears them. Any merchant could have sold it to any wanderer, and he could have lost it in the desert, where some southerner picked it up."

"Southerners do not buy in our markets," Panehsy said suddenly.

These were the first words I had heard from him at the council at all. His voice was low, quiet, with a soft southern accent - but in the silence of the hall it sounded like the snap of a bowstring.

Bakenren slowly turned his head toward him.

"The esteemed commander of the Medjay perhaps knows all southerners by name?"

"I know the desert," answered Panehsy, not raising his voice. "My people have guarded its roads for the Pharaohs for thirty generations. The southerners who burn our villages do not go to our markets. They have nothing to pay with. They have no copper." He paused. "And one thing more. The cord." He nodded at the messenger's palm. "Southerners braid cords of goat wool. Of papyrus they braid only in the Delta. This scarab hung on the neck of a man of the Delta before it came to the neck of a southerner."

The silence became so dense that it seemed one could cut it with a knife.

"Repeat for the record?" Ani the scribe whispered loudly.

No one answered him.

"I do not know how this amulet came into the desert," Bakenren said at last, and his voice was as even as before, but I noticed that the plump fingers folded on his belly clenched a little tighter. "Nor does the Temple. But the Temple is ready to help the esteemed Panehsy in his search - if it pleases the Pharaoh."

"It will please the Pharaoh that Panehsy search himself," I said. "Without the help of the Temple." I turned to the commander of the Medjay. "Panehsy. Bring the prisoner to the capital. Alive. Quietly. Keep the cord and the scarab with you. Find out from whose neck it was taken. And from whose market it came."

Panehsy touched his palm to his brow.

"It shall be done, my Pharaoh."

The messenger was led away - to be fed and washed. The scarab vanished into the folds of Panehsy's leather baldric as soundlessly as it had appeared.

* * *

Bakenren waited until the silence became quite unbearable, and only then rose from his place by the far wall.

"Will the Pharaoh permit a humble servant of the Temple to say a word?"

I nodded, without uttering a sound.

"The High Priest," Bakenren began, folding his plump hands on his belly, "deeply grieves at the sufferings of the army. And he is ready to open the temple granaries to the Pharaoh's warriors - generously, without reckoning, as befits the Temple in an hour of calamity."

"The generosity of the Temple is known," I said. "As is the fact that this generosity is never free. What does Nefer-Ka want?"

Bakenren spread his hands with the air of a man offended to the depths of his soul by the very suspicion.

"Only what will lighten the burden of the Pharaoh himself. The distribution of grain to the army is a complicated matter, requiring supervision. The Temple has experienced scribes who know how to keep accounts. If supervision of the distribution of bread in the detachments were entrusted to the people of the Temple - all would go smoothly, without thefts, without grievances. And the warriors would know by whose mercy they are fed."

There it was. I almost physically felt the trap close, laid with such patience: not gold, not lands, not a place on the council - merely the right to distribute bread. A right which in a year would have made every warrior in every detachment a debtor of the Temple and not of the throne.

"The warriors even now know by whose mercy they are fed," I said evenly. "From today the detachments receive bread and beer from the royal granaries. Under the supervision of Senusret's men. The Temple may keep its grain for the starving in the villages - there, I am sure, it will find no lesser use and bring the High Priest no lesser glory."

Bakenren bowed - just as low, with the same calibrated respect - but for a fraction of an instant, before he lowered his head, I saw in his eyes something I had not seen there before: not anger, no - rather a calm, cold note for memory. Thus a scribe marks in the margin of a scroll a line to which he will certainly return later.

"The wisdom of the Pharaoh knows no bounds," he said. "I shall relay to the High Priest every word."

"Relay it," I said. "And relay one thing more. If I hear that in the detachments standing by the temple lands anyone again says that the gods have turned from the throne - I shall ask the Temple whence such speeches come."

Bakenren withdrew, and the heavy doors closed behind him with the same hollow, detached sound with which they had once closed behind Nefer-Ka himself.

* * *

"And the southern border?" asked Djehuty as soon as the doors closed, as though the conversation with the Temple were for him merely a vexing interruption. "My Pharaoh, if we do not go south, the southerners will come to us themselves. They see our weakness as well as we see it."

"The southerners will get an answer," I said. "But not by a campaign beyond the cataracts, but by strong outposts. Djehuty, you will take three detachments from those standing by the temple lands - precisely those where the murmuring is loudest - and lead them south, to the outposts. There they will have work. There they will have bread - I shall see to it. And there they will be far from sermons about the wrath of the gods."

Djehuty was silent, weighing the order, and I saw disappointment - there will be no glorious campaign - struggle in him with the realisation that he had just been handed three detachments and a border.

"It shall be done, my Pharaoh," he said at last, and in his voice for the first time in the whole council there was neither challenge nor mockery.

Paser nodded slowly, approving, and it seemed to me that the old man smiled faintly into his grey beard.

Khaemuas nodded too, jingling his bracelets - hastily, too hastily, as people nod to whom it is all one what is decided, so long as it is not decided against them.

"Three detachments south - that is another thousand mouths from the royal granaries," remarked Nebamun, looking at no one.

"Better a thousand mouths in the south than a thousand tongues at the temple walls," Senusret cut in.

"Repeat for the record?"

"Write it down, esteemed Ani," I said, and for the first time that morning everyone in the hall laughed, even Panehsy permitted himself a barely noticeable smile: "'The Pharaoh ordered three detachments sent south. Bread for them - from the royal granaries. Barques - not needed, they will go on foot. And all the rest - not for the record.'"

Ani diligently scratched with his reed brush, moving his lips - and I am still not sure whether he did not enter even the last words in the scroll.

* * *

When the council dispersed, Senusret lingered at the doors.

"Did you see?" I asked quietly.

"I saw, my Pharaoh," he answered. "Khaemuas looked at the priest before he spoke of a 'third way.' Such glances are not cast by chance. And one thing more - he asked about Opet. Who guards the procession. Khaemuas has never in his life thought of piety longer than a prayer lasts."

"And the scarab?"

Senusret was silent.

"The scarab, my Pharaoh, is no longer a murmur in the detachments. If Panehsy is right - then someone in the Delta is hobnobbing with those who burn our villages." He looked at me heavily. "I do not know whether it is the Temple. But I know who profits from the southerners burning villages while the throne cannot protect them."

"Do you think he is already in league with the Temple?"

Senusret was silent.

"I think that Khaemuas has costly bracelets and his pay is not so large as he would wish. And that his father, by rumour, is indebted to the temple of Ptah in Memphis for more than he can repay. More I do not know - yet. But I shall find out."

"Find out," I said. "Quietly. If he is a traitor, I want to know with whom else he speaks. If not - I do not wish to offend a loyal man with suspicion."

"Loyal men at court, my Pharaoh," Senusret remarked with that bitter smile that so rarely appeared on his face, "are now so few that one can offend with suspicion hardly anyone but me. And even then I shall not take offence."

He went away, and I remained alone in the Small Hall, under the faded paintings of ancient victories. The Pharaoh in his chariot still trampled enemies on the opposite wall, and it suddenly came to me that the artist who painted that scene certainly did not know what victories really look like: they are never so clear. Today I had, it seemed, won - led the murmuring detachments away from the temple lands, not let the Temple take the army's bread into its hands, pointed Nefer-Ka to the border. And yet I did not feel a victor. I felt a man who has stepped one pace back from the edge of a precipice - knowing that the precipice has not gone anywhere.

* * *

I did not at once notice that I was not alone in the hall. Paser, who had gone, as I thought, together with all the rest, stood by the far wall, under that same painting of the bound prisoners where Bakenren had lately sat, and studied the faded figures so intently as though seeing them for the first time.

"Did you want to tell me something, Paser?" I asked.

The old man turned not at once, slowly, as people turn whose bodies have long ceased to keep up with their thoughts.

"I did, my Pharaoh. But not before all." He tapped his staff on the flagstones, as if testing their strength. "I served your predecessor. And the one before him - in my very youth, still a boy at the stables. I remember Nefer-Ka as a young priest. Even then he knew how to wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For everything," Paser answered simply. "He waited until the old High Priest died. Waited until a place fell vacant on the council of the Temple. Waited until the drought made people compliant. He never takes by force what can be obtained by patience. Today you refused him bread for the army, and he will not argue. He will simply wait until you yourself bring him that bread - from powerlessness."

I was silent, and Paser, as though taking my silence for permission to continue, came nearer, leaning heavily on his staff.

"There is one thing more, my Pharaoh. Old men like me forget much, but some things they remember too well. When I was young, a story was told at court - in a whisper, as one tells what is better not told at all. That once, long ago, in the time of the Most Ancient, one of the Pharaohs believed the priests too much. He went with them to a sanctuary far in the south, where he was summoned to see an omen. And came back from there... different. Not as he had left. They said that something in him broke - or, on the contrary, opened. That afterward all his life he looked through people, as though he saw something else behind them."

A chill ran down my spine - that same familiar one, from beneath the ribs on the left.

"What sanctuary, Paser?"

The old man shrugged.

"Who will remember now, my Pharaoh. Just a story. Old men like to frighten the young." He paused, looking at me with faded but still keen eyes. "But if the High Priest ever summons you south to see an omen - do an old man a favour. Take Senusret with you. And as many spearmen as possible."

He bowed - heavily, slowly, but with a dignity that none of those who had bowed to me that day had - and went out, tapping his staff on the stone flags. That sound echoed in the empty hall for a long time until it died away completely.

* * *

I was about to leave the hall when in the doorway appeared a youth whom I did not recognise at once - only when he raised his head, bowed in respectful obeisance.

Iti. Junior keeper of the Library, yesterday's scribe at the foot of the throne. In his hands he held a scroll - not a library one, by its look, but a very small one, rolled tight, tied with a plain linen thread.

"My Pharaoh," he said, and his voice, unexpectedly low for so young a face, trembled slightly. "Forgive me for troubling you. I was charged to deliver this to you personally into your hands. And to no one else."

"Who charged you?" I asked, taking the scroll.

Iti hesitated, cast a quick glance over his shoulder into the empty corridor, and only then answered - so quietly that I had to bend toward him to make out the words:

"The priestess Khemu, my Pharaoh. She said that you would understand."

I untied the thread. On a tiny scrap of papyrus, in a hurried, uneven hand, only a few signs were traced:

"The temple pond. When the moon rises. Alone."

I raised my eyes to Iti. He stood before me, daring neither to go nor to speak first - slender, dark-eyed, with that boyishly grave expression that I had noticed yesterday in the audience hall. A strange thing: of all the people surrounding me in these days - counsellors, commanders, priests, servants - this youth was, it seemed, the only one in whose face I saw neither calculation, nor fear, nor hidden intent. Only anxiety - for me, as it seemed to me, though to believe it was almost impossible.

"Do you know what is written here?" I asked.

"No, my Pharaoh," he answered. "I did not read it. I was told not to read."

"And you did not read?"

He blushed - so suddenly and deeply that I barely restrained a smile - and lowered his eyes.

"I did not read, my Pharaoh. Though, I confess, I badly wanted to."

This time I did smile - for the first time, it seemed, in all that long, heavy day.

"Honesty is a rare treasure at this court, Iti," I said. "Guard it. And tell no one that you brought me this scroll."

"No one, my Pharaoh," he answered, bowing, and, straightening, lingered a moment longer, as though wishing to add something. But he said nothing in the end - only bowed again and vanished into the corridor as quietly as he had appeared.

I remained standing in the middle of the Small Hall, clutching in my palm the scrap of papyrus, and looked at the narrow windows under the ceiling, in which the daylight was already beginning little by little to yellow, inclining toward evening.

When the moon rises. Alone.

Until moonrise there remained several hours yet. And for the first time in a long while I awaited the night not with dread of another sleepless vigil, but with the anxious, almost feverish impatience of a man who has at last been promised if not an answer then at least the beginning of an answer.
