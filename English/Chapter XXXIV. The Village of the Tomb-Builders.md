Chapter XXXIV
The Village of the Tomb-Builders

"We are hungry!"

The cry reached us before we saw the village, from the ridge of the last hill, as Senusret's chariot, raising yellow dust, climbed the stony road from the valley into the western desert.

"We are hungry! Eighteen days!"

There were many voices. Dozens. A hundred. They did not shout raggedly, but in unison, steadily, as oarsmen cry on a barque to the beat of a drum. And in that steady cry there was something that made Senusret, who was driving, rein in and lay his hand on his sword.

"My Pharaoh," he said through his teeth. "This is a mutiny."

"It is not a mutiny," said Nebamun. The keeper of the treasury rode in the second chariot beside Iti, gripping the side with both hands and trying to preserve his dignity, which on a mountain road came hard. "A mutiny is when they cry 'death.' They cry 'we are hungry.'" He paused. "That is a reckoning. They are presenting a bill."

The chariot crested the ridge.

* * *

The village lay below, in a narrow stony hollow between two yellow hills, like a nest in a palm. One long straight street, houses along it. Small, cramped, built wall to wall, of mud-brick and broken stone, with flat roofs on which washing dried, mats lay, jars stood. Around it all, a wall. High, whitewashed, enclosing the village on every side, with a single gate at the northern end.

And beyond the village, higher up the slope, in the cliffs, were entrances. Dark rectangular openings in the yellow rock. Dozens. Tombs. Some long finished, sealed, with names carved above the entrance. Others still under construction, with wooden scaffolding, with heaps of rubble at the entrance. And at the very top, just below the ridge, a large entrance, deep, wide, framed by pillars not yet finished.

My house of eternity. The tomb that had been built for me from the first day I ascended the throne. As it is built for every Pharaoh.

And at the gate of the village, on the open space before a small temple with sandstone columns, people were sitting.

Not standing: sitting. On the ground. Right in the dust. A hundred men, stonecutters, carvers, painters, plasterers, porters, sunburnt, sinewy, in loincloths so covered with white stone dust that they seemed moulded from chalk. They sat close, shoulder to shoulder, cross-legged, facing the temple. And cried, steadily, in unison, again and again:

"We are hungry! Eighteen days!"

And between them and the temple, on the steps, stood a small, fat, frightened scribe in temple linen, waving his arms and trying to say something. No one was listening.

* * *

We drove right up to the gate before we were noticed.

The first to turn was one of the seated men, a stocky, broad-shouldered, grey-haired man, with a small beard braided into a plait, and with hands so calloused they seemed carved from the same stone as the cliffs around. He saw the chariot. Saw Senusret. Saw me, in plain travelling dress but with the uraeus on my brow.

He stood. Slowly. Dusted off his knees. And bowed, low, but not prostrating himself, as peasants do, but as masters bow: with the dignity of a man who knows his worth.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "I am Neferhotep. Foreman of the right gang." His voice was hoarse but firm. "We did not expect you."

A hundred heads behind him turned. The cry fell silent. A hundred men, white with dust, looked at me.

"You are hungry, Neferhotep," I said. "Eighteen days."

"Eighteen days, my Pharaoh," the foreman confirmed. "Our ration, grain, fish, oil, beer, firewood, comes from the temple granaries. Every ten days. So it has always been. So it is written." He nodded toward the fat scribe on the steps, who at the sight of me had fallen to his knees and pressed his forehead to the stone. "Eighteen days ago he said: 'A delay.' Ten days ago he said: 'Soon.' Three days ago he said: 'The Temple is in difficulty, work, and then we shall see.'" Neferhotep paused. "We worked. Fourteen days on empty gruel. Then we stopped."

"Why did you stop?"

Neferhotep looked at me calmly, without fear.

"Because a hungry carver, my Pharaoh, carves crooked," he said. "And a hungry painter mixes his colours. And a hungry stonecutter drops his mallet. Yesterday Hui, our best painter, fainted on the scaffolding in your tomb. Nearly fell to his death." He paused. "We are building you a house of eternity, my Pharaoh. You will live in it for millions of years. Would you want it built crooked?"

Behind him someone grunted. Someone laughed. A hundred men waited.

* * *

Nebamun was already at the steps of the temple. He bent over the fat scribe, asked him something quietly; the scribe, without lifting his head, babbled something; and the keeper of the treasury straightened with the face of a man who has found in a column of figures an error long suspected.

"My Pharaoh," he said, coming up. Not loudly. "The village ration was withdrawn three weeks ago from the temple granary on the west bank. By order." He paused. "The order bears Bakenren's seal."

"Bakenren has been gone two weeks."

"The order is dated the day before his disappearance, my Pharaoh," said Nebamun. "And no one has revoked it since." He looked at me over his tablets. "The scribe says he was told not to ask. He was told to say 'a delay' to the workmen. And to wait."

"To wait for what?"

"For the workmen to abandon their work," said Nebamun evenly. "For Pharaoh's tomb to stand still. For a rumour to go round the capital that even the builders of Pharaoh's tomb are starving. That even Pharaoh's eternal house is unfinished." He paused. "Before the festival of Opet. Before the road to Luxor. That is a very bad omen, my Pharaoh. The people love omens."

Behind me Senusret made a low sound.

"Nebamun," I said. "How much do they need?"

The keeper of the treasury did not even glance at his tablets.

"For a hundred and twenty men with their families, for twenty days of missed ration and ten ahead: seven hundred hekats of barley, two hundred of spelt, forty jars of oil, three hundred fish, a hundred and twenty jars of beer," he said. "And firewood." He paused. "And my Pharaoh has forgotten the fine on the Temple for the disruption of work. That is by law. I shall reckon it separately."

"Today, before sunset," I said.

"From the royal granaries, my Pharaoh?"

"From the royal. And the bill, to the Temple."

Nebamun allowed himself a barely perceptible smile. And bowed.

* * *

When I told this to those seated, loudly, from the steps of the temple, a hundred men at first did not believe it. Then someone shouted. Then all shouted, not steadily, not in unison, but all at once, each as he pleased, and leapt up, and embraced, and slapped one another's dusty backs so that white clouds rose over the square.

But Neferhotep did not shout. He came right up to me and said quietly:

"My Pharaoh. Thank you." He paused. "And now, may I show you something? Not here. Up there. In your tomb."

"What?"

The foreman was silent for a long time. Behind him the workmen were celebrating.

"Five days ago," he said at last, "when we were still working, my stonecutters were cutting the lower corridor. The deepest. Where the chamber for the sarcophagus will be." He lowered his voice. "And they broke through a wall."

"What wall?"

"One that should not have been there, my Pharaoh," said Neferhotep. "Behind it is a room. Old. Very old. Older than all the tombs in these hills. Older, perhaps, than the Pharaohs themselves." He swallowed. "My men do not want to go down there. Not one. Not even for double ration." He looked at me. "The Wise Woman says: do not touch. She says it is not a house of the dead. It is a house of those who wait."

* * *

The Wise Woman was named Tausert. She lived in the last house on the street, by the northern wall, a small, low one, with a door painted blue and an eye drawn over the threshold in red ochre.

Neferhotep took me to her before leading me up. "It is the custom, my Pharaoh," he said, "to go to the Wise Woman before the dead. She knows what they need." Iti followed us with his tablet under his arm. Senusret came last, with his sword.

Tausert sat on the threshold of her house, on a low stool, a small, dry, very old woman with a face so thickly lined that it seemed hewn from the same yellow stone as the hills around. She wore a simple linen dress, and around her neck a necklace of blue faience beads and small amulets: an Eye, a scarab, a tiny figure of the dwarf Bes, who drives off evil spirits. Her hair was grey, braided into dozens of thin plaits. Her eyes were black, bright, alive.

Before her on a mat lay potsherds. Dozens of potsherds, fragments of jars, flat flakes of limestone, and on each something was drawn. I bent down. On one, a cat standing on its hind legs, with a shepherd's crook, was herding a flock of geese. On another, a mouse in the crown of Pharaoh rode in a chariot drawn by dogs and shot a bow at a fortress on whose walls cats were defending themselves. On a third, a donkey in priest's vestments, with a censer, was solemnly proclaiming something before an altar on which lay a bundle of hay.

I could not help it; I laughed.

Tausert raised her black eyes to me. And smiled, toothless, sly.

"You like it, son of Ra?" she said. Her voice was creaking, like an unoiled door. "The boys draw these. The painters' apprentices. When they are learning. They are not yet allowed on the tomb walls; they would spoil them. So they draw on potsherds." She took up the sherd with the priest-donkey. "This one Hui drew. Twenty years ago. When he was an apprentice. They nearly threw him out of the gang for it; someone showed the sherd to a priest from the temple." She laughed. "The priest did not understand. Thought it was about some other priest."

"And the mouse-Pharaoh?" I asked.

"The mouse-Pharaoh," said Tausert, "is about all Pharaohs, son of Ra. Do not take offence. Boys always draw kings as mice. And then they grow up and paint them as gods on tomb walls." She shrugged. "One does not hinder the other. A god too is sometimes a mouse, when he must squeeze through a narrow crack."

* * *

"Sit, son of Ra," she said, nodding at the ground before her. "You have not come to me. You have come to that room below. But before you go there, tell me your dream."

"What dream?"

"The one that comes to you every night," said Tausert. "These last two moons. I see it in your eyes. You have the eyes of a man who wakes in a cold sweat and cannot for a long time make out where he is."

I sat on the ground. Iti sat beside me. Senusret remained standing.

"I dream," I said slowly, "that I stand between columns. Enormous ones. In darkness. And someone raises a hand. And I do not see the face."

Tausert nodded. Without surprise. She reached to a shelf by the door, took a bundle, old papyrus, worn, tied with twine. Unrolled it on her knees.

"The Book of Dreams," she said. "A priest of Hermopolis wrote it. Long ago. Two hundred years ago. In it are all the dreams a man can see. And what they mean." She ran her finger along the lines. "Listen."

She read slowly, in a chant, as prayers are read:

"'If a man sees himself in a dream gazing at a great cat, good: it means he will have a great harvest. If a man sees himself in a dream drinking warm beer, bad: it means suffering will come to him. If a man sees himself in a dream gazing into a deep well, bad: it means he will be cast into prison. If a man sees himself in a dream gazing at the moon when it shines, good: it means the god will forgive him.'" Tausert turned the sheet. "'If a man sees himself in a dream between two columns in darkness...'"

She fell silent.

"What?" I asked.

Tausert looked at the line for a long time. Then raised her eyes to me.

"Here it is erased," she said. "The line is there. The beginning is there. And the end is erased. As though someone scraped it off." She ran a fingernail across the papyrus. "'Good' or 'bad' cannot be seen. What it means cannot be seen."

"So no one knows?"

"So," said Tausert, "the priest who wrote the book knew. And whoever erased it knew too. And did not want others to know." She rolled up the papyrus. "For forty years I have read this book to people. Only this line has always been erased. I thought, by time. Now I think not."

* * *

"Then I shall tell you myself," said Tausert. "Not by the book. From myself."

She took my hand. Turned it palm up. Looked for a long time. Then ran her finger across the palm, dry, rough, like a piece of papyrus.

"Smooth," she said quietly. "Like a child's. Like a stone the water has rolled." She raised her eyes. "For a man with a smooth palm to see himself between columns in darkness is neither good nor bad, son of Ra. It is a door. You stand in a doorway. The columns are its jambs. The darkness is what lies beyond."

"And the hand? That rises?"

Tausert was silent for a long time.

"A hand," she said at last, "can open a door. Can close it. Can push through it. Can hold one on the threshold." She released my palm. "You see only the hand. You do not see whose it is. And you do not see what it is doing." She paused. "When you see, you will know whether it is good or bad."

"And if I do not see?"

"Then, son of Ra," said the Wise Woman, "you will see this dream again. And again. Until you see." She smiled. "All dreams repeat until they are dreamed to the end."

Beside me Iti was writing quietly. I saw his brush trembling.

"And one thing more," said Tausert. "Below, in that room which my boys broke into. Take nothing. Touch nothing. Only look." She paused. "And if you find an empty place there, do not try to fill it. It waits not for you. It waits for what you will have."

* * *

The tomb, my house of eternity, began at the top, at the very ridge, with a wide rectangular entrance framed by two pillars not yet finished, with a red grid of guide-lines marked in ochre on the limestone. From the entrance a corridor went down into the depth of the rock, straight, level, slightly sloping, so long that its far end was lost in darkness.

Neferhotep went ahead with a lamp. After him, I. Then Iti. Then Senusret. Then two masters whom Neferhotep had called with him: the painter Hui, thin, nervous, pale-faced, not yet recovered from his faint on the scaffolding, his fingers smeared with red and black paint to the wrists, and the carver Ipui, huge, slow, silent, with a chisel stuck in his belt and so calm a face that nothing in the world seemed able to surprise him.

The walls of the corridor were already dressed, smooth, even, white. On some, a red grid: squares ruled in ochre, and in the squares sketches of future paintings. Gods. Stars. The barque of the sun. The serpent Apep. The twelve gates of the Duat. I recognised them from Amenemope's tales, from the scrolls of the Epic, from the night by the pool.

"Here," said Hui, stopping by one wall and raising his lamp higher. "Here you will be, my Pharaoh. Before Osiris. At the judgment." He pointed to the red sketch: a man in the double crown stood before a throne on which sat a god with a green face, and between them, scales. "And here, your heart on the scales. And here, the feather." He paused. "I have not yet decided what colour to make the feather. White or gold. As my Pharaoh wishes?"

"What should it be?"

Hui hesitated.

"By the rules, white, my Pharaoh. But..." He blushed. "I dreamed it was gold. Three nights running. The feather, gold, and the heart lighter than it. And the scales swing, and swing... and do not stop."

"Make it gold, Hui," I said.

The painter beamed.

And Ipui the carver, who all this time had not spoken a word, suddenly ran his enormous palm over the white wall, slowly, tenderly, as one strokes a sleeping child, and said in a low, resonant voice:

"The stone here is good, my Pharaoh. Quiet. It will not crack." He paused. "You will live here long. Millions of years." He paused again. "If you wish."

"If I wish?"

Ipui looked at me with calm, dark eyes.

"My grandfather cut stone for the former Pharaoh," he said. "The one whose name was later erased. Grandfather said that Pharaoh came to his tomb once. Looked. Stroked the wall. And said: 'Good work. A pity I shall not live here.'" The carver paused. "Grandfather thought he was joking. And then he really was not buried here. The tomb stands empty. Over there, in the neighbouring hollow." Ipui stroked the wall again. "A good tomb. Quiet stone. Empty."

* * *

The corridor ended. Beyond it, a staircase hewn in the rock, steep, narrow, leading down. Then another corridor, unfinished, rough, with undressed walls, with heaps of rubble on the floor. Then a large chamber, square, with four pillars left standing in the rock, the future chamber of the sarcophagus, still unworked, rough, smelling of dust and freshly split stone.

And in its far wall, a hole.

Uneven, ragged, with sharp edges, as though someone had struck with a mallet and the stone had simply fallen inward. Man-high. Beyond it, darkness.

And from there came air. Dry. Warm. Smelling not of dust, not of stone, but of something else. Something sweet and bitter at once. Like honey and wormwood.

Neferhotep halted ten paces from the hole. Hui behind him, pale as flax. Ipui motionless, as always.

"I shall go no farther, my Pharaoh," said the foreman quietly. "Forgive me." He held out the lamp to me. "The Wise Woman said: only look. Touch nothing."

I took the lamp. Stepped toward the hole.

"My Pharaoh," said Senusret. His voice was strained. "Let me go first."

"No, Senusret."

"My Pharaoh..."

"Stand at the entrance," I said. "As there, at Luxor. Be where you can do something."

Senusret looked at me for a long time. Then nodded, curtly, heavily, and took his place by the hole, with his back to the wall, his sword drawn.

Iti stepped after me. I turned.

"My Pharaoh," he said. Quietly. "If there is script, you need someone who reads it." He paused. "Two words. But still."

I nodded.

And we went in.

* * *

The room was round.

That was the first thing I understood when I raised the lamp. Not square, not rectangular like all rooms in all tombs, in all temples, in all palaces of the Two Lands, but round. Perfectly. Like a bowl. Like a well. Like a circle.

Its walls were smooth. Not dressed with a chisel, but smooth, even, as though they had not been hewn from the rock but cast. The stone was not yellow limestone as everywhere in these hills, but dark, almost black, with a barely visible sheen, like water at night. And on those walls, from floor to ceiling, in a ring, without beginning and without end, ran the script.

Long, flowing, curving lines. Running into one another without a break. Not carved, no. I brought the lamp closer. They were neither carved nor painted. They were inside the stone. Beneath the surface. Like veins in marble. Like lines on the skin of an old scroll that I had never seen but somehow remembered, ingrained so deep that they had become part of it.

And they glowed.

Faintly. Dimly, palely, on the very verge of the visible. When I lowered the lamp, the glow became more noticeable. Weak, bluish, like the light of stars reflected in water.

Beside me Iti stopped breathing.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered. "This... these are not our signs. Nor the temple's. They are hers. The script. The whole room. All of it."

I raised the lamp to the ceiling.

The ceiling was a dome. Low, smooth, dark. And on it, stars. Not painted, also inside the stone, like the script, tiny, glowing points, hundreds, thousands. I recognised Sah, Osiris striding across the sky, with his belt of three stars. Recognised Sopdet, white, bright, at the very edge of the dome. Recognised the Imperishable, circling an invisible point in the north.

But there were others. Which I did not know. At the bottom of the dome, at its edge, to the south, four stars. Bright. In a cross.

"Four stars. In a cross. The sailor of Amenemope had seen them in the south, when a storm carried his ship beyond Punt."

"Those stars cannot be seen from here, my Pharaoh," Iti whispered. "Never. Seneb said the sky is the same everywhere. And here..."

"Here is the sky that is seen from everywhere at once," I said. And did not myself understand where those words came from.

* * *

In the very middle of the round room stood a bench. Stone. Low. Dark as the walls. And on it two hollows. Small, round, smooth. Each the size of a nut. Side by side. A palm's breadth apart.

Empty.

I looked at them and felt the cold rise from beneath my ribs, on the left. Slowly, inexorably, like water in the well of the nilometer in a good year.

Two hollows. The size of a nut. Exactly for two stones.

The stones that now lay in a casket of black wood, bound at the corners, in the treasury of the Temple. That Khemu had seen in her vision, in my hands. That Nefer-Ka had promised to give me "as the First Ones gave them," at Luxor, in the inner court of the god.

"If you find an empty place there, do not try to fill it. It waits not for you. It waits for what you will have."

"My Pharaoh," Iti said quietly. "Look. Above the bench."

I raised the lamp.

Above the bench, on the wall, the script broke off. There was a window, a small square, outlined by a thin line, and in it, not script. An image. Also inside the stone, also glowing with a dim blue light.

Two doors. Side by side. Wall to wall. One open, the other closed. And between them, on the threshold, with its back to the viewer, a figure. Tall. With a raised hand. And in each hand, a disc. One dark. The other light.

And beneath the image, a line. Not script. Signs. Ours. Hieroglyphs. But so ancient, so angular, so unlike the present ones that I barely recognised familiar outlines in them.

Iti was already on his knees before the bench, face raised to the line, lips moving.

"'Here...'" he whispered. "'Here waits...'" He faltered. Leaned closer. "My Pharaoh, this is very old writing. Such as was written before the First Pharaohs. In the Library there are only three scrolls in it. Amenemope taught me." He traced his finger in the air, not touching the wall. "'Here waits... the house... of him who holds both.'"

"House," I repeated.

Iti nodded. Then shifted his gaze, from the hieroglyphs down to the line of script that ran straight beneath them. Short. Of the same length.

"My Pharaoh," he said. His voice trembled. "This is again... this is again as in the seventh scroll. Our signs, and beside them, theirs. The same thing." He swallowed. "This sign. In the middle. With the two loops. It stands where ours has 'house.'"

He turned to me. His eyes were shining, huge, dark, wet.

"My Pharaoh. We know three words. 'Keep.' 'Appointed time.' And 'house.'"

* * *

I do not remember how long we stayed there. Iti copied, all he could, by the light of a single lamp, onto his tablet, small, quick, without lifting his head. I stood by the bench and looked at the two empty hollows. I did not touch them. The Wise Woman had said not to touch.

But I stretched out a hand over them, not touching, a palm's breadth above. And felt.

Warmth.

Faint. Even. As if from stones that were not there. As if they had lain there quite recently and gone, leaving warmth behind, as a man leaves it who has just risen from his bed.

Or as if they lie there now. Only not here. In another place. In another time. And I feel their warmth through... through what?

I snatched my hand back.

"Iti," I said. "It is time."

He raised his head. Looked at me. Then at the two empty hollows.

"My Pharaoh," he said quietly. "These are for them. For the two Eyes. Are they not?"

"I do not know, Iti."

"You do," he said. Simply. "You do not remember. But you know."

I did not answer.

* * *

When we climbed out of the round room back into the unfinished sarcophagus chamber, Senusret was standing at the hole just as before, with his back to the wall, his sword drawn. His face was grey.

"My Pharaoh," he said through his teeth. "While you were in there. Here... here someone passed."

"Who?"

"I do not know." He did not lower his sword. "Past me. That way," he nodded at the hole. "I did not see. I felt. Like wind. Like cold. As though someone very tall passed close by and brushed me with a shoulder." Senusret swallowed. "I struck with my sword. Air."

"We saw no one," said Iti.

"I know," said Senusret. "He cannot be seen."

Neferhotep, waiting by the stair, seized the amulet of the dwarf Bes at his neck and began to mutter protective words. Hui stood white as flax. Ipui, motionless, as always. He alone looked not at the hole but at me.

"It is he, my Pharaoh," said the carver in his resonant, calm voice. "Grandfather used to say. When that Pharaoh, whose name was erased, came to his tomb, someone was always beside him. Invisible. The workmen felt it, like wind." Ipui paused. "Grandfather said it was his shadow. Only longer than it ought to be."

* * *

I ordered the hole walled up.

Not the room destroyed: walled up. Carefully. With stone. So that no one would know what lay behind it. And on the new masonry to be carved a simple sign. Only one. The Eye.

"My Pharaoh," said Neferhotep, when I gave the order. "We are building you a house of eternity." He paused. "But it seems someone built you a house before us."

"It seems so," I said.

"What are we to do with your tomb? Continue?"

I thought. Of the empty tomb in the neighbouring hollow. Of the Pharaoh with the erased name who said: "Good work. A pity I shall not live here."

"Continue, Neferhotep," I said. "Build well. Evenly. On full stomachs." I paused. "Even if I do not live here, someone will. A good house does not stand empty."

The foreman looked at me for a long time. Then bowed, low, lower than he had bowed at the gate.

"Ipui," he said, not straightening. "Did you hear? A good house does not stand empty. Carve it. Above the entrance. Small. So that only we can see."

"I shall carve it," said Ipui.

* * *

As we descended to the village the sun was setting behind the western hills, to where, it was said, the lands of the dead began, where Ra every evening entered the mouth of heavenly Nut.

On the square before the temple it smelled of fried fish and fresh bread. The first sacks from the royal granaries had already arrived, and the women of the village, laughing and calling to one another, were baking flatbreads in clay ovens right in the street. Children ran among the ovens snatching hot pieces. The workmen sat by the walls drinking beer from jugs, and someone was already singing, loudly, off-key, merrily.

Tausert sat on the threshold of her blue house. Seeing me, she raised her hand.

I went up.

"Did you find the empty place, son of Ra?" she asked.

"I did."

"Did you touch nothing?"

"I touched nothing."

She nodded. Contentedly. Then held out a potsherd to me. One of those that lay on the mat before her. I took it.

On it a mouse was drawn. In the crown of Pharaoh. But not in a chariot, not with a bow. The mouse stood at a door. Between two doors. One open. The other closed. And in the mouse's paws, two discs. Dark and light.

"Hui did not draw this," said Tausert. "I found it. Forty years ago. In the dust, at the entrance to that hollow where the empty tomb is." She paused. "Forty years I kept it. Wondering whom to give it to." She looked at me with black, shining eyes. "To you."

"Why a mouse?"

Tausert laughed, creakingly, toothlessly.

"I told you, son of Ra," she said. "A god too is sometimes a mouse. When he must squeeze through a narrow crack. Between two doors."

* * *

On the way back, in the chariot, in the dusk, down the stony road, into the valley, toward the river, toward the capital, Iti rode beside me. Senusret drove, silent, not turning. Nebamun with the other chariot had fallen behind; he had stayed in the village to count sacks.

"My Pharaoh," said Iti quietly, so Senusret would not hear. "Three words."

"Three words."

"'Keep.' 'Appointed time.' 'House.'" He paused. "If put together... 'Keep the house until the appointed time.' Or 'The house keeps until the appointed time.' Or..." He faltered.

"Or?"

Iti was looking at the dark valley below, where the lights of the capital were already kindling.

"Or 'The appointed time keeps the house,'" he said. "'It is time to return home.'" He paused. "I do not know, my Pharaoh. I do not know in what order their words go. Perhaps in the reverse. Perhaps in a circle."

"In a circle," I said.

Iti looked at me.

"Like an inscription that reads in both directions?" he asked. "I have read of such. In the Greek scrolls. There is one in the Library." He paused. "If in a circle, the beginning is where you begin to read."

"And where do you begin?"

Iti was silent for a long time. The chariot bounced on the stones. Below, in the valley, the lights of the capital grew brighter and brighter.

"Where you stand, my Pharaoh," he said at last. "Always where you stand."

Four days remained until the new moon.
