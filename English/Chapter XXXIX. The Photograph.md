Chapter XXXIX
The Photograph

"To the quay!" cried Ferhat.

He was already standing at the gate, in his trousers alone, barefoot, hair dishevelled, straining toward the street, toward where below, at Galata, the white steamer with the red stripe was coming up to the berth, slapping her paddles on the golden morning water.

"Misafir, quick! He is there! The one with your face! We will make it if we run!"

I stood on the balcony with the stones in my hand, cooled, heavy, as if filled with lead, and could not move.

And Kemal was tugging at my sleeve. The other way. South. To Sultanahmet.

"Sandık," he said. The chest. Once. Quietly. Stubbornly.

Two. One pulls to the quay. The other to the chest. And I stand in the middle.

As always.

"Enough."

Suleyman had come out into the courtyard. In his night robe, with the prayer beads he had picked up from the ground where he had dropped them at dawn. His face calm. His voice quiet. But the courtyard fell silent at once.

He said something. Briefly. Ferhat lowered his hand from the gate.

"Grandfather says," Ferhat translated reluctantly, "first, the house. The guest who has sailed in will find us himself." He paused. "Grandfather says: the one who has your face cannot be lost in a city where you are. As a reflection cannot be lost from its mirror."

* * *

We walked through the waking city, Ferhat, Kemal, Orhan with his knife, Akmal, who refused to stay home and ran ahead, and I. Suleyman stayed behind, "my knees," he said, but I saw it was not the knees. He stood at the gate and watched us go. And his lips moved. He was praying.

The city woke as though nothing had happened. The water-seller cried "Su! Buz gibi su!" Simit-sellers' boys carried trays of ring-breads on their heads. Shops opened. It smelled of coffee, bread and the sea.

Only the cats behaved strangely.

They were everywhere. On the steps of houses. On windowsills. On roofs. On the rims of fountains. Hundreds of cats, ginger, white, striped, black, and all sat motionless. And all looked in one direction. South. Toward Sultanahmet.

"Misafir," Ferhat whispered, looking at the cats. "Do you see?"

"I see."

"Grandfather said the cats in this city see what people do not." Ferhat swallowed. "The Prophet loved cats. They say one slept on his sleeve, and he cut off the sleeve so as not to wake her." He paused. "They say that since then the cats of Istanbul guard what is dear to Allah."

A mangy ginger cat on the steps of a mosque turned her head. Looked at me. Long. Then stood and walked ahead of us. South.

After her, a second. A third. A tenth.

We walked through the streets of Istanbul, and behind us, and before us, cats walked.

* * *

At the door in the blind wall, beside the embedded fragment of an ancient column, we were awaited.

I had not expected this. I had thought it would be empty. There would be Hatice Hanim, terrified, in her shift, and her eleven cats.

But at the door stood people.

The first I saw was Hasan Amca, impossible not to see: huge, red-bearded, in a robe over his nightshirt, with a basket of figs in his arms which he had evidently snatched on the way "just in case." He saw me and waved with his free hand.

"Misafir!" he boomed down the whole lane. "At last! We've been here since dawn!"

"We?" I asked.

Hasan stepped aside.

Behind him, leaning against the wall, stood Hodja Nureddin, small, dry, in his enormous white turban slipping to one side, running his prayer beads with the air of one to whom all that was happening was personally disagreeable. And beside the hodja, tall, thin, straight, in the honey-coloured felt hat of a dervish, Ahmet Baba. With blue eyes. With the three-coloured cat Mismis on his shoulder.

"He was right," said Hodja Nureddin on seeing me. Neither good-day nor good-morning. "He is always right. It is unbearable."

"Who was right?" I asked.

"Ahmet," said the hodja, nodding at the dervish. "For forty years he said this would happen. For forty years I said he was out of his mind." The hodja spat in the dust. "And this morning he knocked at my door and said: 'Nureddin, get up. The chest has awakened. Let us go and see.' And I went." He straightened his turban. "Because if he is right, I want to see it. And if he is not, I want to see his face."

Ahmet Baba smiled. Mismis on his shoulder yawned.

* * *

And a little further along the lane, by the wall, I could not believe my eyes, stood a litter. Old, wooden, with carved handles, with faded silk curtains. Such as pashas and sultanas were once carried in.

The litter was held by two, one of them Sumbul Agha. Huge, in green silk, in his white sugar-loaf cap, with his bunch of keys at his belt. The other, a young sturdy porter, evidently hired at the gate.

The curtain of the litter drew back. And from within, through two pairs of spectacles, huge, black, century-old eyes looked at me.

"A hundred and two years, guest," said Niyaz Efendi in his rustling voice. "A hundred and two years I have not gone out of the old palace farther than the third gate." He paused. "And today Sumbul carried me in his arms all the way to the first gate because I could not wait for the litter." The old man smiled crookedly. "I hope it is worth it."

"Niyaz Efendi..."

"Hush, guest." The old man raised a tiny hand, black with ink. "Do not waste words. Today many will be needed."

* * *

And also, right at the door, on the step, hunched, sat an old man in an Armenian cap, with a loupe on a cord, and beside him a grandson of about ten.

Master Karapet. The jeweller of the Grand Bazaar. Fifty years in a shop by the Nuruosmaniye.

"Barev," he said, seeing me. In Armenian. Then, collecting himself, in Turkish. And added something.

"He says," Ferhat translated, "that his great-grandfather awaited this. His grandfather awaited. His father awaited." Ferhat listened on. "He says: 'I shall come once more. Perhaps not I, but I shall come.' So the nameless stranger with two stones said to his great-grandfather." Ferhat paused. "Karapet says: 'I have come to see who has come.'"

Aram, the grandson, the very one who had held the candle in the shop, looked at me with huge eyes and pressed to his chest something wrapped in velvet.

"What have you got there?" I asked him.

The boy blushed and showed. A candle. That very one. Over which my stones had been heated.

"Grandfather said to bring it," he whispered in Turkish. "Said, perhaps light will be needed."

* * *

And last, I did not recognise her at once, by the wall stood an old woman in black, with a rosary with a cross, with a white starched collar.

Despina. Kyra Theodora's maid.

She came up to me. Held out a letter. Folded. Sealed with wax. With a crest, a double-headed eagle with one head.

"From the Kyra," she said. In Greek, but I understood. And added in Turkish, with difficulty: "Kyra cannot come. Her legs. Ninety-one years." She paused. "Kyra said: 'If the chest opens, let the guest read this there. Not before.'"

I took the letter. It was warm.

* * *

Hatice Hanim opened the door herself.

Small, dry, in a white headscarf, with a face like a baked apple, she stood on the threshold, and her face was white. Not from fear. From something else. From what comes over people who for forty years have waited for something without knowing what they awaited, and have been rewarded.

"The cats," she said. One word.

And stepped back to let us pass.

We entered, all of us. Hasan with his figs. The hodja with his beads. Ahmet Baba with Mismis on his shoulder. Sumbul Agha, who carried Niyaz Efendi in his arms, the litter would not pass through the door. Karapet with his grandson. Despina. Ferhat. Kemal. Orhan. Akmal. I.

A narrow corridor. A smell of dust, old wood, cats. A stair going down.

On the top step of the stair, where for forty years Hatice Hanim's cats had sat without descending, no one sat.

All eleven were below.

* * *

The cellar was flooded with light.

Not a lamp; no one had lit a lamp. The light came from the chest. From the metal plate on its lid, the one I had seen the first time, on the walk with Suleyman: without keyhole, without seam, entirely covered with script. The plate glowed. With an even, blue, cold light, like the water at the sides of a boat in southern seas. Like the glow of plankton. Like the script on a glowing screen in a far-off land.

Around the chest, in a ring, sat the cats.

Eleven. Ginger, white, striped, black. The fattest, the ginger one, the very one that had lain on my knees the first time, sat directly before the chest, by the plate itself, like a sentry. They did not mew. They did not howl; Mustafa had been wrong, or they had howled earlier and now fell silent. They sat motionless, heads raised, and looked at the light.

When I came down the stair, all eleven turned their heads at once.

And looked at me.

And then the ginger one, slowly, unhurriedly, rose. Stepped aside. Making room.

Room by the chest.

* * *

"Wait," said Niyaz Efendi.

Sumbul Agha set him down on an old stool by the wall. The old man could not for a long time catch his breath. Then he adjusted his spectacles. Both pairs. And looked at the chest.

"Two hundred years," he said. "Two hundred years ago my predecessor set this chest here. According to an entry in the Book." He paused. "The entry is made in a hand..." He faltered. "In a hand you know, guest. With a blot."

"What does the entry say?"

Niyaz Efendi closed his eyes. Quoted from memory, in his voice rustling like a turning page:

"'Set the chest in a house by the sea, where cats will live. Do not open it. It cannot be opened. It will open by itself when two meet. Inside is what the kept one must see before returning home.'" The old man opened his eyes. "Two hundred years we thought that 'the kept' meant the stones. That inside is a place for the stones."

"And now?" asked Ahmet Baba.

Niyaz Efendi looked at me.

"And now," he said, "I think that 'the kept' is he."

* * *

I went up to the chest.

The cats did not stir. They sat in a ring. They watched.

The plate glowed. The script on it, long, flowing, curved, burned blue. And I suddenly saw, not read, saw, as one sees a face in a crowd, that it was the same line. The very one that had been read in all worlds at night.

"When two meet, what is kept will return home. The time has come."

How did I know it?

I do not know. Kemal had said in the night: "They all write the one thing." Perhaps he told me this too. Perhaps I myself knew. Always.

The lid of the chest was ajar.

A hair's breadth. The thickness of a fingernail. But ajar. Between the lid and the wall a thin black crack. Where for two hundred years there had been neither gap nor crack nor keyhole.

The click we had heard at dawn.

I stretched out my hand.

"Guest," said Hodja Nureddin. Quietly. For the first time without mockery.

I turned.

"Ahmet offered you to stay," said the hodja. "You ate the bread and threw the salt. You left." He paused. "I told you then: Ahmet will tell you where the key is, but will not tell you what is behind the door." He looked at the chest. "This is behind the door, guest. Are you sure you want it?"

I was silent a long time.

"No," I said honestly. "I am not sure."

Hodja Nureddin nodded. As though that were just the answer he awaited.

"Good," he said. "Then open it. Those who are sure are always mistaken."

* * *

I raised the lid.

It yielded easily. As though waiting. As though for two hundred years it had awaited a single movement of a hand.

The hinges did not creak. Dust did not rise. Within, darkness. It smelled, I sensed it at once, of honey and wormwood. Like the scroll from Khiva that I had never seen. Like the rose of Tarnovo that I had never smelled. Like the round room beneath the tomb that I had never entered.

Aram, Karapet's grandson, stepped forward. With trembling hands lit the candle. Held it out.

And I saw.

* * *

The chest was almost empty.

On the bottom, dark velvet. Faded, but whole. And on the velvet, three things.

The first was a hollow. Not a thing; a place. Two small round nests in the velvet, side by side, a palm's breadth apart. Empty. The size of a nut. Exactly for two stones.

I did not lay the stones in them. I do not know why. My hand reached out and stopped. As though someone, very far away, very long ago, had said to me in the voice of an old woman with plaits: "If you find an empty place, do not try to fill it. It waits not for you. It waits for what you will have."

I had never heard that woman.

The second thing was a watch. A pocket one. Silver. Old. Worn. On a chain. The lid closed.

I took it. Opened it.

The hands stood still. Three o'clock. Exactly.

Three o'clock in the night. The hour at which the stones had awakened that night.

On the inner side of the lid, an engraving. Fine. In Cyrillic. I read:

"L. A. Odessa."

And below, very small, in another hand, as if scratched with a needle:

"Do not hurry. It will catch up with you."

I knew no L. A. I had never been to Odessa.

But my hand holding the watch trembled.

* * *

And the third thing...

The third lay in the very centre. Between the empty nests for the stones and the watch. Flat. Rectangular. Wrapped in thin tissue paper, yellowed with time.

I unwrapped the paper.

Within was cardboard. Dense, hard, with a gilt edge. And on the cardboard, an image. Not a drawing. Not an engraving. Not a watercolour.

I had never seen anything like it. The image was grey, in shades of grey, from white to black, without a single colour, but so exact, so alive, so real, it was as though someone had cut a piece out of the world and laid it on the card. Every fold of clothing. Every shadow. Every wrinkle on a face.

"A photograph," Niyaz Efendi whispered behind me. "This is a photograph. The Franks have been making them for almost forty years. Writing with light." He paused. "But I have never seen one so sharp."

I held the card to the candle.

And stopped breathing.

* * *

In the photograph was a bridge.

Long, wide, on floating pontoons, a bridge across the Golden Horn. I had seen it. No, not this one. Another. The one I had seen was lower, narrower, with different spans. But this was wide, iron, with lamps on tall posts, with shops below, under the roadway, at the water's edge.

People were crossing the bridge. Many people. And, I looked and did not believe, not one of them wore a fez.

Not one. Not a single turban. Not a single headcloth. Hats. Men's hats, felt, brimmed, like the Franks'. Caps. Bowlers. Women, unveiled, with open faces, in short, knee-length! dresses, with cropped hair. Down the middle of the bridge drove a cart without horses, iron, on wheels, with round lamps in front. And behind it, a carriage on rails. Long. With wires above.

"Allahu akbar," whispered Hodja Nureddin. "Not a single fez. Not one." He clutched at his enormous turban as if afraid it would be taken off. "Are these Franks? Is this Paris?"

"This is Istanbul," said Ahmet Baba quietly. "Look at the background."

Beyond the bridge, on the hill, minarets rose. A dome. Huge. Familiar. Hagia Sophia. And farther right, a tower. Round. Stone. With a pointed roof like an astrologer's cap.

The Galata Tower.

"This is Istanbul," Ahmet Baba repeated. "But not ours."

* * *

And in the foreground of the photograph, at the bridge rail, stood three.

On the left, an old man. Very old. Bent. With a white beard. In a European jacket, in a cap. With a cane. He looked straight into the lens with serious, un-childlike, dark eyes. And behind his ear, I bent closer, to the very flame of the candle, behind his ear stuck a small dark object. A stub. Charcoal.

"Kemal," Ferhat whispered behind me. His voice cracked. "Misafir. That is... that is Kemal. Old."

Kemal beside me did not stir. He looked at the photograph. At the old man with charcoal behind his ear. At himself. Fifty years on. His face was calm. Quite calm. As one looks at what one has long known.

And to the right of the old man stood two.

Young. Of one height. One build. In identical dark overcoats. In hats. They stood side by side, shoulder to shoulder, at the bridge rail. And looked, not into the lens. At each other.

They had one face.

Mine.

* * *

I do not remember how long I held that photograph. A minute? An hour? The candle in Aram's hand burned half down. No one said a word.

Two. One face. Standing side by side. Looking at each other.

One, I. I saw my face in the mirror every morning. I knew it.

And the second...

The second was also I. But another. I cannot explain how I saw it. The features were the same. But the expression different. As if this second had grown up in another house. Spoken another language. Seen other people. Loved others. As if he were I, having lived another life. In another country. With another name.

And behind their backs, by the rail, a little to one side, something blurred. Grey. Tall. A head taller than a man. As though someone stood there but could not stand still while the lens was working. As though someone were, and were not.

"Turn it over," said Niyaz Efendi. In a whisper.

I turned it.

* * *

On the back, an inscription. Two lines.

The upper in the script. The very one. That had been read in all worlds at night.

The lower in letters. Not Arabic. Not Greek. Not Armenian. Latin. As the Franks write. But the words, I saw it, the words were not Frankish. Turkish. Written in Frankish letters.

No one in the cellar could read it. Niyaz Efendi, who knew forty languages, looked at the line and shook his head. Ahmet Baba, who had lived among the Franks in Paris, squinted and shrugged. Karapet, Hasan, the hodja, no one.

And Kemal, a twelve-year-old boy who could barely write Turkish in Arabic script, bent over the card. Looked at the line. And read. Aloud. Syllable by syllable. Slowly. As children read.

"'I-ki-si bu-lus-tu. Do-nus bas-la-di.'"

Silence.

"'The two met,'" Ferhat whispered. "'The return has begun.'"

And below, a date. In figures. Frankish. Which Kemal read as well, without stumbling:

"'Twenty-ninth of October. One thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine.'"

* * *

"Nineteen twenty-nine," Niyaz Efendi repeated slowly. "By the Frankish reckoning." He closed his eyes. "In fifty-four years."

"How did he read it?" whispered Hodja Nureddin, looking at Kemal as though the boy were a djinn. "How? These are Frankish letters! The boy does not know Frankish letters!"

Kemal raised his head. Looked at the hodja. Calmly.

"I was shown," he said. In Turkish. Simply.

"By whom?"

Kemal looked at the photograph. At the blurred grey figure behind the backs of the two.

"By him," said Kemal. "At night. When I sat on the threshold." A pause. "He said: 'One day you will write with these letters. Everyone will. Remember how they look.'"

* * *

Ahmet Baba slowly lowered himself to the floor. Right onto the stones of the cellar. Among the cats. Mismis jumped from his shoulder and climbed into his lap.

"Hasan," said the dervish. "Do you remember the riddle? The one you asked the guest in the first days? About two brothers?"

Hasan, still clutching the basket of figs to his chest, nodded. His face under the red beard was white.

"'Two brothers live in one house,'" he said hoarsely. "'One always looks forward, the other back. They have never seen each other. But one without the other cannot take a step.'" He swallowed. "I forgot the answer. Father asked it. Forty years ago. I forgot."

"He did not forget," said Kemal.

All turned to him.

Kemal pointed at the photograph. At the two with one face, standing at the bridge rail.

"Here is the answer," he said. "One looks forward. The other back. They live in one house." He looked at me. "In him."

* * *

The silence in the cellar was such that I could hear the heart beating in the ginger cat who sat at my feet.

Then Despina, silent, severe, in black, stepped forward.

"The letter," she said in Greek. And in Turkish: "The Kyra's letter. She said, there. When it opens."

I broke the seal with the one-headed eagle. Unfolded it.

Kyra Theodora's hand, old-fashioned, elegant, a little shaky. In French. I read aloud, I do not know why, no one but Ahmet Baba and Niyaz understood, but I needed to hear those words.

"Young man.

If you are reading this, the chest has opened. So I was right, and my great-grandfather was no madman. It is pleasant to be right at ninety-one. At that age it happens ever more rarely.

I wish to tell you one thing which I did not say at our meeting. I did not dare.

My ancestor, Manuel Melissenos, saw how that guest departed. On the night of the twenty-ninth of May fourteen hundred and fifty-three. He wrote in the chronicle: 'I saw how he went. It cannot be described in words. Let whoever comes see for himself.'

The chronicle burned. But my great-grandfather knew one more phrase from it. He never spoke it before strangers. Only to me. Before his death. When I was twelve.

Here it is:

'He did not go alone. There were two. They stood by the wall and looked at each other. They had one face. And then came a third, tall, grey, without a face, and laid his hands on their shoulders. And all three went into the light.'

Young man. The twenty-ninth of May. The twenty-ninth of October on your photograph, I do not know what is on it, but I know there will be a twenty-ninth in it. My great-grandfather used to say: 'In this city everything important happens on the twenty-ninth.'

The city fell on the twenty-ninth. Perhaps on the twenty-ninth he will return. Not to the Greeks. Not to the Turks. To himself.

Go to the quay. He is waiting for you.

F. P.-M.

P.S. My peacock this morning spread his tail. For the first time in ten years. He knows too."

* * *

"To the quay," I said.

I laid the photograph back in its paper. The watch in my pocket. The stones in their pouch. The Kyra's letter with the other papers.

The lid of the chest I did not close. Let it stand open. The empty nests for the stones, let them wait.

The cats, all eleven, rose at once. And went before me, up the stair. The ginger one first.

But at the stair Niyaz Efendi stopped me. With a tiny black hand. By the sleeve.

"Guest," he said. "One thing. Before you go."

"Speak."

"The Venetian," said the old man. "The man in grey. He came on the same steamer. Sumbul saw him at Galata an hour ago, when carrying me over the bridge." He paused. "The Venetian knows of the chest. Knows that it has opened. He will come here. Soon." Niyaz Efendi looked at the open chest. "When he comes, let him find emptiness. Let him find the empty nests. That will..." the old man searched for the word, "that will anger him. And when they are angry, they err."

"And you?"

"And I," said Niyaz Efendi, "have waited a hundred and two years for this morning. Now I shall sit here. With the cats." He smiled. "Sumbul will stand at the door. He knows how to stand at doors. Sixty years he has stood."

Sumbul Agha by the wall, huge, in green silk, slowly laid a hand on his dagger. And nodded to me.

"Go, guest," he said in his high boyish voice. "I will hold the keys."

* * *

We ran to the quay.

All of us, Ferhat, Kemal, Orhan, Akmal, I. Hasan puffed behind with his basket of figs, falling back at every turn. Hodja Nureddin and Ahmet Baba walked more slowly, together, arguing as they went, waving their arms. Karapet with his grandson behind them. Despina last, running her rosary with its cross.

And the cats. Hundreds of cats. They ran through the streets beside us, over the roofs above us, along the walls. Passers-by stopped, crossed themselves, murmured prayers, scattered. Traders snatched up their trays. The water-seller dropped his jug.

We ran to the sea.

On the way Hasan, breathless, purple, suddenly seized my sleeve and blurted, gasping:

"Guest! Guest! I remembered! I remembered another! Father asked it! He gulped air. 'What meets twice in the same place, but never at the same time?'"

"I don't know, Hasan!"

"Nor do I!" Hasan roared, laughing and weeping at once. "Forty years I don't know! You'll tell me when you find out!"

* * *

And on the way, by the New Mosque, at the very descent to the bridge, someone overtook us.

Tall. In a grey overcoat. In a top hat. With a cane.

He walked quickly, not running, no, but so that he could not be caught. He walked against us, from the quay into the city, toward Sultanahmet. To where we had run from. To the house with the cats.

Drawing level with me, he did not stop. Only lifted his top hat, politely, in the French manner.

"Bonjour, monsieur," he said. "My congratulations. You have got ahead of me by half an hour." He smiled. "That will not last."

Bergamot. Cologne. Expensive. Parisian.

"The chest is empty," I said to his back.

The Venetian stopped. Turned. Looked at me, and for the first time since I had seen him, in his face, which cannot be remembered, something real flickered. Surprise.

"Empty?" he repeated.

"Empty nests," I said. "For the stones. And nothing more."

He looked at me for a long time. Then at the pouch at my belt. Then at me again. And smiled. Slowly. Coldly.

"So the stones are with you," he said. "And you did not lay them in." A pause. "Clever. Very clever." He raised his hat again. "Then I shall wait. We know how to wait, monsieur. Three hundred years we have waited." He turned. "We shall wait more."

And went off toward Sultanahmet, tapping his cane on the stones. And the cats, hundreds of cats, parted before him, arching their backs, hissing.

* * *

The Galata quay.

A crowd. Porters. Boatmen. Traders. Ladies under parasols. Officers. Sailors. Noise, shouting, clanging, hooting. The white steamer with the red stripe at the berth. Gangways. Passengers descending the gangways. Greeks. Armenians. Russians. Pilgrims.

I stood at the edge of the quay. Kemal beside me, holding my hand. Ferhat behind. Orhan with his knife, glancing round. Akmal on a bollard, from where he could see better.

And I searched. With my eyes. For a face. My own face. In the crowd.

"There!" cried Akmal from the bollard. "There! The General! With epaulettes! Grey-haired!"

I saw.

At the gangway, below, on the landing stage, a grey old man in a general's uniform. Huge, upright, with orders. He stood looking at the city, and in his hand was an open silver medallion.

Beside him, a young officer. In uniform. Bareheaded. With tousled fair hair. With a white handkerchief sticking out of his breast pocket. He looked round, quickly, anxiously, as though seeking someone.

And between them, a third.

Young. In a grey frock coat. With a pot in his hands. In the pot, a rose. Dark crimson. Almost black. With two open flowers and one bud.

He stood looking at the city. At the hills. At the roofs.

And then turned his head. And looked at me.

* * *

I do not know how to describe what followed.

The noise of the quay vanished. Not subsided: vanished. As though someone had switched off the whole world, leaving only the two of us. The crowd froze. Porters with bundles on their backs. Ladies under parasols. Gulls above the water. Smoke from the steamer's funnels. Everything stopped.

Like the clocks in the clockmaker's cellar.

Like the hands on the pocket watch in my pocket. Three o'clock exactly.

Only we two moved.

He walked toward me. Slowly. With the rose in his arms. Through the frozen crowd. I walked toward him. Slowly. With the stones at my belt. With the photograph in my pocket.

Ten paces. Five. Three.

We stopped facing each other.

And I saw his face.

* * *

My face.

The same features. The same cut of the eyes. The same line of the cheekbones. The same curve of the lips. All the same.

But other. As in the photograph. As though this man had grown up in another house. In an old castle with portraits on the walls. With an aunt in lilac, with pearls. With a lieutenant at the door. With roses in the garden. With a grille at the end of the left wing.

He looked at me as I at him. With the same expression. Astonishment. Recognition. Fear. And something more, what comes when after a long road you at last see home.

We were silent. A long time.

Then he said, in Russian, quietly, and I understood every word though I did not know that language:

"Are you I?"

And I answered in Turkish, and he understood though he did not know that language:

"I do not know. And you?"

"I do not know either."

We looked at each other.

He held out a hand. Palm up. I looked at it.

Smooth. Without a life line.

I held out mine. Palm up. He looked.

Smooth. Without a life line.

* * *

Kemal beside me, the only one besides us who moved in that frozen world, stepped forward. Stood between us. Looked at one. At the other. With his serious, un-childlike eyes.

And said one word. In Turkish. To both at once.

"Misafir."

Guest.

And then did what he had never done. Took my hand with his right. And the other's hand with his left. And joined them. Palm to palm.

Two smooth palms. Without a life line.

The pouch at my belt flared. The stones, both, grew hot. The scroll in the pocket of his frock coat flared. The rose in the pot, the third bud opened. All at once. Entirely.

And behind him, and behind me, simultaneously, like a shadow cast not by the sun but by something else, a figure rose.

Tall. Grey. Without a face.

One. For the two.

It laid its hands on our shoulders. One on mine. The other on his.

And I heard a voice. From within. From beneath the ribs, on the left. Older than mine. Calmer. The very one that in the night had said in all worlds: "Soon."

Now it said another thing.

One word.

Not in the language that does not exist. In all languages at once. In Turkish and Russian. In Egyptian and Greek. In the language of the people at the red rock and in the language of the glowing window in a room I had never seen.

It said:

"Now."

* * *

And the world moved.

The noise of the quay crashed over me like a wave. Shouts. Hoots. Gulls. The crowd. All moved again.

I stood at the edge of the Galata quay. Kemal held my hand. Ferhat my shoulder. Orhan stood beside me with his knife.

And before me, no one.

An empty place. The stones of the pavement. A scrap of rope.

"Where is he?" I turned. "Where?!"

The General at the gangway, grey, huge, with the medallion in his hand, stood looking at where the youth with the rose had just stood. His face was white. The lieutenant beside him, fair-haired, with the handkerchief in his pocket, was pushing forward, shoving through the crowd, and shouting. In Russian. The same thing. Over and over.

I did not understand the words. But I understood.

He was calling a name.

Not mine. Not the one in my passport. Another. The one an old housekeeper had whispered to the youth at the step of the carriage.

The lieutenant shouted that name, and the crowd parted before him, and he ran, looking round, along the quay, along the landing, to the water, and found no one.

* * *

At my feet, on the stones of the pavement, lay a pot.

Clay. Cracked. Bound in wet sacking. And in it a rose. Dark crimson. Almost black. Three flowers. All open.

I knelt. Lifted the pot.

It was warm.

The lieutenant, fair, with tousled hair, with a white handkerchief in his pocket, ran up to me. Stopped. Looked at me. At my face.

And froze.

"Mon cher?" he whispered.

I did not know what these words meant. But I knew that voice.

From somewhere. From beneath the ribs. On the left.

The lieutenant looked at me. Long. At my face, his face. At the rose in my arms. At the boy with charcoal behind his ear beside me.

"You are not he," he said in Russian. Slowly. "But you have his face."

Ferhat, who stood beside me, suddenly stepped forward. And said, in broken Russian which, I was certain, he had never learned:

"He is here. He is everywhere. He will return."

The lieutenant looked at Ferhat. Then at me.

Then slowly, very slowly, reached into the pocket of his tunic. And took out a silver cross on a thin chain. Darkened with time.

"My mother's," he said. To me. As though I understood. "I gave it to him. Yesterday. On the steamer. Told him, if you go where I cannot, leave it on the threshold." He opened his palm. "He left it. Here. On the stones. Beside the rose."

He laid the cross in my hand.

"Give it to him," said the lieutenant. His voice broke. "When you see him. Tell him I found."

* * *

The General came last.

Slowly. Heavily. As old men walk who suddenly understand that they are old. He stopped before me. Looked at my face. Long. As he had looked for half a century, at faces. On all continents.

And closed the medallion.

"Fifty years," he said in Russian. Quietly. To himself. "Fifty years I sought. Found." A pause. "Two."

He turned to the lieutenant.

"Kostya," he said. "He did not drown. He did not vanish." A pause. "He went away. These are different things. I was told so. Half a century ago. In a grotto."

"Where did he go?" cried the lieutenant. "Where?!"

The General looked south. At the Sea of Marmara. At the sun rising above the Asian shore.

"To where he is awaited," he said. "To other places. Other times." He paused. "To the river where a barque carries an empty shrine. To the city of two towers. To the red desert." The General clenched the medallion in his fist. "The man in grey told me in the night. I did not believe."

And then he looked at me. And said, in Turkish. Clean. Without accent. As he had, probably, spoken with viziers and pashas for thirty years.

"You are the one to whom he came. Keep the rose. It has bloomed for the third time. That means he will return."

* * *

I stood on the Galata quay. With the rose in my arms. With the lieutenant's cross in my palm. With the stones at my belt. With the photograph in my pocket.

In the photograph, a bridge. Hats instead of fezzes. Carts without horses. Old Kemal with charcoal behind his ear. And two with one face standing side by side at the rail.

The twenty-ninth of October. Nineteen twenty-nine.

In fifty-four years.

The two met. The return began.

But the meeting I had just seen lasted a moment. And in the photograph they stood side by side. Calmly. For long. As people stand who have nowhere to hurry. As though they had met, and stayed.

Then this is not yet that meeting.

Then there will be another.

In fifty-four years. On this same bridge. Only iron. With lamps. And old Kemal will stand beside them. And a photographer, someone I do not yet know, will say: "Do not move." And the grey shadow behind our backs will not be able to stand still while the lens works.

And until then...

* * *

Kemal tugged my sleeve.

I looked at him. A twelve-year-old boy. Barefoot. With charcoal behind his ear. With serious, un-childlike eyes that saw the underside.

He was pointing upward.

Above the Galata quay, above the bridge, above the Golden Horn, in the morning sky, a white dove was flying. With feathered feet. With a fan tail. Akmal's dove.

She circled above us. A second time. A third.

And then flew. South. Over the Sea of Marmara. To where, many days' journey away, beyond sea and desert, a shallowed river flowed, and along it slowly, by Merira's grace, sailed the flotilla of the god, to Luxor.

Akmal on the bollard shouted after her. Waved his arms.

"She will come back," I said to him. "She always comes back on her own."

Ferhat looked at me.

"If someone is waiting very much," he said quietly.

* * *

And in the evening, when the sun was setting behind the hills of Istanbul and from the minarets the evening call floated, I sat on the balcony of Suleyman's house. With the rose in its pot at my feet. With the photograph on my knees.

And Kemal sat beside me. Drawing with charcoal on the flagstone of the balcony. Not two doors. Not two circles. A bridge. Long. With lamps. And on it, three. An old man with charcoal behind his ear. And two, side by side.

"Kemal," I said. "Do you know who the second is?"

Kemal did not raise his head. Finished drawing a lamp.

"I know," he said.

"Who?"

Kemal looked at me. For a long time. With his serious eyes. Then stretched out a hand and laid it on my chest. On the left. Where beneath the ribs lived a cold, long-standing, familiar.

"The one who looks back," he said. "While you look forward."

He took away his hand. Took the charcoal. And wrote beneath the drawing, not in Arabic letters, which he barely knew. In Latin. Frankish. Which no one in this city wrote. Which all would write fifty-four years on.

One word.

"*Dönüş.*"

Return.

* * *

Over Istanbul the moon rose.

Thin. New. The first after the moonless night. A crescent like an eyelash, like the curve of an Arabic letter, like the beginning of a line that no one has yet written.

In all worlds, I knew it without understanding how, at that moment she was rising too.

Over the river, where the flotilla of the god lay aground by Abydos, and Pharaoh looked south holding in his hands a child's tablet.

Over the Black Sea, across which an empty steamer was returning to Odessa.

Over the red desert, where by a roadside station an old Afghan prayed, and on the laptop screen two systems were silent, for the first time all night, as if waiting.

Over the cellar in Sultanahmet, where a hundred-and-two-year-old keeper sat by the open chest, and eleven cats by the door, and a eunuch in green silk held the keys, and beyond the door, in the lane, stood a man in grey, and did not enter.

Over the round room in the old palace, where on an empty shelf, just beneath the dome, stands a blue notebook with blots. Which does not yet exist. But already stands.

And over my balcony.

I held the photograph up to the moonlight. Two at the rail. One face.

And it seemed to me, for a moment, shorter than a breath, that the second, in the photograph, turned his head.

And looked at me.

And smiled.
