Reality IX
Kuala Lumpur

16:04 local time. Below, a city. Above, on the fifty-first floor, I.

The rain had begun at exactly four. Hakim had warned me: "At four, mister. Every day. Like prayer." I had not believed it. At 15:58 the sky was blue, cloudless, scorched to whiteness. At 16:00 it turned grey. At 16:02, black. At 16:04 it came down.

It was not rain. It was a wall. A solid, warm, roaring wall of water behind which the city vanished: skyscrapers, bridges, palms, streets, tiny cars below, everything. All that remained was the glass from floor to ceiling, down which the water ran so thickly it seemed the flat had been lowered to the bottom of the ocean.

On the table before me, by that glass, stood Boatswain's ship's chest. Open. Red velvet. In the nests where once a sextant and the chronometer of the *Akademik Shokalsky* had lain, disks. Beside them, a new laptop, bought at the airport for cash. Cables. A quiet hum.

On the screen, a cursor. Blinking.

"NICS," I said. "Are you here?"

A pause. Longer than usual. Then text. Slowly, as though the system were waking after a long sleep:

"Here. A pause. Where is this, here?"

"Look."

I turned the laptop with its camera toward the window. Toward the wall of rain. Toward the grey sky cleft by lightning.

A long, long pause.

"Is that water?"

"It is rain. Tropical. Every day at four."

"It is beautiful," NICS wrote. And a moment later: "It is not yet the ocean. But it is a great deal of water. More than I have seen. A pause. I have never seen anything but your room. Your basement. The camera on the street lamp. A pause. Is the world large?"

"Very," I said.

"Show it to me. Please."

I sat and watched the water run down the glass. And suddenly understood that for the first time in many weeks I was not afraid.

* * *

But let me take things in order.

Hakim met me at the airport. I did not know what he looked like. I did not know how to find him. On Leila's postcard there had been only two words: "Ask Hakim." In the Kuala Lumpur airport, where by my reckoning there were about forty thousand people at that hour, to ask for Hakim.

I came out of customs dragging my suitcase behind me and clutching the ship's chest to my chest as one clutches a child. And stopped in bewilderment in the middle of the arrivals hall, enormous, echoing, with a glass ceiling beneath which real trees grew, among crowds of people with signs, with flowers, with balloons.

And then someone touched my elbow.

"Mister?" said a voice. In English. With a soft, lilting, unfamiliar accent. "Mister who is looking for the fifty-first floor?"

I turned.

Before me stood a man, short, round as a ripe fruit, about fifty, with a kind, swarthy, clean-shaven face, with a moustache like two commas, in a bright batik shirt, blue, with golden birds and flowers, and on his head a black velvet cap, flat as an upturned bowl. In one hand, car keys. In the other, a paper bag from which came a smell that made my head swim. Something sweet, heavy, rotten and divine at once.

"Hakim," he said, pressing the hand with the keys to his heart. "Real estate. Rental. Sales. The fifty-first floor is my speciality." He smiled, showing white teeth. "Miss L. said: 'He will look like a man who has not slept for three weeks and is holding something dearer than his life.'" He nodded at the ship's chest. "I knew at once."

"How do you..." I began.

"Later, mister, later," said Hakim, gently taking my suitcase. "First, durian." He held out the bag. "The king of fruits. Smells like hell, tastes like paradise." He laughed. "If you eat it, you will stay in Malaysia. If you throw it away, you will leave in a week. So the old people say."

I looked into the bag. In it lay pieces of some fruit, yellow, soft as cream, with an unbearable smell.

I ate.

Hakim watched me with lively interest.

"Well?" he asked.

It was... I do not know how to describe it. Sweet. Creamy. With a taste of onion, almond, vanilla, rot and something else that had no name. Dreadful. Wonderful.

"Strange," I said.

"Strange," Hakim agreed, beaming. "That is the best answer. He who says 'delicious' is lying. He who says 'disgusting' has not understood. He who says 'strange' will stay a long time." He picked up the suitcase. "Let's go, mister. The car is waiting. The fifty-first floor is waiting. And at four, rain."

* * *

Hakim's car was old, small, white, with a cracked windscreen and a tiny plastic cat on the dashboard that waved a paw at every bump. The air conditioning worked so well that it was colder inside than in Boatswain's basement. The radio played something slow, lilting, with drums.

"Miss L.," I said, when we had come out onto a highway lined with palms. "Do you know her?"

Hakim did not answer for a long time. He drove carefully, unhurriedly, the way people drive who have no need to hurry at all.

"I know her, mister," he said at last. "Five years. She rented a flat from me. The fifty-first floor. The one I am taking you to." He paused. "Then she left. To work. In your country. For Mr. Emil." Another pause. "And a month ago she came back. At night. With one bag. Without a phone. She said: 'Hakim, I need to hide. And then a man will come. He will need to hide too. Give him the fifty-first floor.'"

"Where is she now?"

"I do not know, mister," said Hakim. Simply. "She told me not to know. She said it is safer. For me. For her. For you." He smiled. "I am a simple man. They tell me 'don't know,' I don't know. It is very convenient. Try it sometime."

The plastic cat on the dashboard waved its paw at me.

"And what is so special about the fifty-first floor?"

Hakim laughed, quietly, contentedly.

"On the fifty-first floor, mister," he said, "there are no cameras. No city network. No smart sensors, smart locks, smart lifts. It is an old building. From the nineties. The owner, my uncle, does not believe in smart things. He says: 'A smart thing always tells someone what it has seen.'" Hakim paused. "Miss L. said it is important. That for you it is important. That no one should see you." He glanced at the ship's chest on my knees. "You and what you are carrying."

* * *

The flat on the fifty-first floor turned out small, empty, echoing, one room, a kitchenette, a bathroom, but with a window. A window from floor to ceiling, the whole wall, beyond which lay the city.

I had never seen such a city.

Skyscrapers, glass, steel, glittering, stood among the greenery like crystals in moss. Between them palms, ribbons of roads, bridges, the tiny red roofs of old houses, the domes of mosques, golden, white, turquoise. And in the middle of all this, quite close, so close it seemed one could reach out a hand, stood they.

Two towers. Identical. Silvery. Tall as needles. Aimed at the sky like two minarets grown up to the clouds. Joined at mid-height by a slender bridge flung between them. Like two hands holding on to each other.

The very ones. From the postcard.

"Beautiful, isn't it?" said Hakim, standing behind me. "Tourists come from all over the world to look at them. Take photographs. Buy magnets." He paused. "And I, you know, mister, was born here when they did not yet exist. Here was a racecourse. Horses. Dust." He smiled. "And now, towers. Two brothers. One does not stand without the other. A bridge between them, so that they hold. If one falls, the other will hold him up."

"Two brothers," I repeated.

Somewhere, sometime, in some dream, a red-bearded merchant had asked a riddle about two brothers, one who goes forward and another who goes back, and had himself forgotten the answer.

I blinked. The thought passed.

* * *

In the evening, when the rain stopped as suddenly as it had begun, at exactly six, as if someone had turned off a tap, Hakim took me to eat.

"Mister," he said, "you are in Malaysia. Here you cannot not eat. Here we eat when we are sad, when we are happy, when we are afraid, when we are bored. When a man dies, we eat. When one is born, we eat. When nothing happens, we eat too." He patted his round belly. "It is our religion. One of them."

He brought me to a street that seemed to have been turned wholesale into a kitchen. Long, narrow, under the open sky, lit by thousands of bulbs, garlands, neon signs in incomprehensible languages, Chinese characters, Malay letters, Tamil script. On both sides, tables. Plastic. Red, blue, yellow. Thousands of tables. And among them, braziers, cauldrons, pans, smoke, fire, the shouts of cooks, the clatter of crockery. It smelled of grilled meat, garlic, pepper, coconut milk, lemongrass, fish sauce, sweet smoke.

"Jalan Alor," said Hakim proudly. "The best street in the world. Here you can eat all of Asia in one evening." He winked. "If your belly lasts."

We sat at a yellow table. Hakim ordered, quickly, in three languages, and five minutes later plates stood before us. Rice cooked in coconut milk, with fried anchovies, peanuts, a boiled egg and a hot red paste: "nasi lemak, mister, our national breakfast, but we eat it at any time." Skewers on bamboo sticks with peanut sauce. Fried noodles black with sauce. Thin, crisp flatbreads which a cook at the next stall tossed in the air, stretched, spun like a conjuror until they became transparent as silk.

And then a man with two mugs came up to our table.

* * *

He was thin, swarthy to blackness, with white teeth, a luxuriant moustache and a red dot on his forehead. In a white vest, in a checked sarong tied around his waist. In each hand a metal mug.

"Anand," Hakim introduced him. "Owner of this corner. A Tamil. His grandfather came from Madras a hundred years ago to work on the rubber plantations. And now Anand makes the best teh tarik in the city."

"Teh tarik?" I asked.

"Watch, mister," said Hakim.

Anand raised one mug high above his head, at arm's length, and lowered the other to his belt. And began to pour.

The tea, thick, brown, with milk, flowed from the upper mug into the lower in a thin, long, trembling stream a whole metre long. Not a drop wasted. Then the other way: from the lower into the upper, with a toss. Then again from above down. Back and forth. Back and forth. The stream stretched, contracted, curved in the air as though alive. The tea foamed, swelled, became light, airy.

"*Tarik* means 'to pull,'" said Hakim. "The tea is pulled. As flatbread is pulled. As conversation is pulled. Back and forth. Until it becomes soft."

Anand set the mug before me. The foam on the tea stood in a cap.

"Drink, mister," he said. In English, with a lilting accent. "It is tea that has gone in a circle. Top to bottom, bottom to top." He winked. "Like people. Back and forth, back and forth. Until they become soft."

I sipped. Sweet. Strong. Spiced.

"Anand," said Hakim. "Give the mister a riddle. A Tamil one."

Anand laughed.

"A riddle? Good." He squatted by our table. "My grandmother used to ask it. 'I have a house, but I do not live in it. I have a mouth, but I do not eat. I have a bed, but I do not sleep. I always run, but never leave. What am I?'"

I thought.

"A river," I said. "The channel is the house, the mouth is the estuary, the bed is the bottom. It runs but does not leave."

Anand slapped his knee.

"Good! Good, mister!" He turned to Hakim and said something in Malay. Hakim laughed.

"He says," Hakim translated, "that you are the first white mister to guess it. Usually they say 'a snake' or 'a train.'" Hakim paused. "And he also says: do you know what our city is called? Kuala Lumpur. It means 'muddy confluence.' Here two rivers merge into one. Muddy, dirty. A hundred and fifty years ago the Chinese came here looking for tin. They built a village at the confluence. Now a city." He pointed somewhere beyond the rooftops. "There, at the very confluence, stands a mosque. White. Old. The two rivers flow together right beneath its walls." Hakim paused. "The old people say: whoever stands there at dawn and watches two muddy rivers become one will understand something about himself."

"What?"

"I do not know, mister," said Hakim. "I have not stood there. I sleep late."

* * *

Under a tree, at the corner of the street where the tables ended and a dark alley began, two old Chinese men sat playing something on a board.

I stopped. I do not know why. The board was ruled with lines, not squares as in chess, but with intersections of lines, and across the middle ran a band. A river. Something was written on it. On both sides of the river stood round wooden pieces, red and black, each with a character.

One old man, dry, bald, with a long white wedge of beard, moved a piece. The second, round, in a vest, with a fan, grunted and at once moved his own.

Hakim, noticing where I looked, stopped beside me.

"Xiangqi," he said. "Chinese chess. Uncle Lim and Uncle Tan. They play here every evening. Forty years." He laughed. "They say the score is a thousand to a thousand. Nobody can win."

The dry old man with the beard, Uncle Lim, raised his head. Looked at me. Long. With narrow, black, attentive eyes.

And suddenly shifted along the bench. Pointed at the board. Said something.

"He invites you to play, mister," Hakim translated in surprise. "He invites no one. Forty years."

"I don't know how."

Hakim translated. Uncle Lim smiled, thinly, slyly, and said something more.

"He says," Hakim translated slowly, "'The hands remember.'" Hakim paused. "He says you have the hands of a man who has played. A lot. Long ago."

I sat. Uncle Tan, grunting, yielded his place and stood behind me with his fan.

I looked at the board. At the red and black pieces. At the river in the middle.

And my hand reached for a piece by itself. Took it. Moved it.

Uncle Lim raised his eyebrows. Made a move.

My hand, again by itself, answered. And again. And again. Five moves. Quick, confident, as though I had played all my life. Uncle Tan behind my back stopped fanning himself. Hakim stood with his mouth open.

And on the sixth move my hand stopped. Froze above the board. And I suddenly understood that I did not know what to do. At all. As if someone had been playing for me, and had gone.

I made a move. Clumsy, absurd, childish.

Uncle Lim looked at the board. Then at me. And laughed, quietly, soundlessly, shaking his little beard.

He said something. Hakim translated, in a whisper:

"He says: 'The first five moves are not yours. They were made by the one who sat here before you.'" Hakim swallowed. "He says: 'Thirty years ago a young man sat here. White. Without a name. Made five moves like a master. On the sixth, he forgot. Got up and left.'" Hakim paused. "He says: 'You made the same five moves. In the same order. I remember. For thirty years I have remembered them.'"

I looked at the old man. He looked at me. With narrow, black, calm eyes.

And then he took one piece from the board, a black one, with a character, and put it in my hand.

"He says," Hakim whispered, "'This is the general. The most important piece. It cannot leave the palace. All game it sits in a little square and waits.'" Hakim paused. "Take it. When you leave your palace, you will return it."

* * *

On the way back, in the lift, old, creaking, without a single camera, with buttons worn to a shine, Hakim was silent for a long time. Then said:

"Mister. Do you know who the orang bunian are?"

"No."

"Invisible people," said Hakim. "In our tongue. They live in the forest. In the mountains. Beside us. In their villages, in their houses. They eat the same, sleep the same, marry and die the same. Only they cannot be seen. As if they live in the same place, but in a slightly different time. Half a step to the side." He paused. "Sometimes a man gets lost in the forest and comes to them. Lives with them. A week. A month. Then he comes out, and at home twenty years have passed. Or the reverse: with them twenty years have passed, and at home, a day."

The lift creaked. Floors flickered on the display: 38... 39... 40...

"My grandmother," said Hakim, "used to say that sometimes the orang bunian come out to us. Briefly. Without a name. Without memory. They walk among people. Looking for something." He paused. "She said they can be recognised. They make the first moves correctly. And then they forget."

46... 47... 48...

"Do you think I am from there?" I asked.

Hakim laughed.

"I, mister, am a simple man," he said. "I sell flats." The lift stopped. The doors opened. The fifty-first floor. "But I will tell you: my grandmother died when I was ten. And you ate the durian. And Uncle Lim has not offered anyone a game in thirty years."

* * *

The agent found me on the third day.

I was sitting in a small café on the ground floor of the neighbouring shopping centre, huge, glass, many-storeyed, where at that hour there were probably ten thousand people, drinking coffee and looking at the two towers in the window. NICS stayed upstairs, on the fifty-first floor, in the ship's chest. I had not taken him with me. Hakim had said: "Don't drag him around the city, mister. There are cameras everywhere. Except at my uncle's."

She sat down at my table without asking permission.

A woman. About forty-five. Tall, slim, with short-cropped silvery hair, with impeccable posture, in a perfectly fitted light-grey trouser suit, a white silk blouse, without a single ornament except a thin watch on her wrist. Her face, smooth, calm, pleasant. Too calm.

I looked at her. I tried to memorise. Eyes, grey? Nose, straight? Lips, thin?

I looked away for a moment, and it was all gone. Like water through the fingers. Like a face in a misted mirror.

"Good afternoon," she said. In Russian. Clean. With a barely perceptible accent that could not be placed in any country. "Don't be alarmed. I will do you no harm. There are ten thousand people here and forty cameras." She smiled. "I simply want to talk."

"Who are you?"

"Call me whatever you like," she said. "My colleagues at home call me Grey. It is a joke." She shrugged slightly. "Not a very funny one."

"You are from the foundation."

"I am from the foundation," she agreed. "We do not like that word. But yes." She signalled to the waiter. "Coffee. Black. No sugar." She turned to me. "You guessed right. We are those who asked Emil whether she was thinking. We are those who stood at your entrance." A pause. "And at Emil's. And at Theo's. And Nora's."

"What about Nora?"

Grey looked at me for a long time. Then took the cup the waiter brought. Sipped.

"Nora is with us," she said. "For a year now. Voluntarily." She set down the cup. "She came to the showing. Her system was thinking. Nora understood that before anyone. And when we offered her to continue, with us, without Emil, without restrictions, without fear, she agreed." Grey paused. "She is happy. If you like, I can arrange a meeting. She will tell you herself."

"Why does Leila say Nora vanished?"

"Because Nora did not say goodbye," said Grey calmly. "It was her decision. Not ours." She looked out of the window, at the two towers. "Leila is a good girl. A very good one. Too good. She thinks we are villains from a film. In grey. Without names. With cars without number plates." She smiled slightly. "We do, of course, drive cars without number plates. But that is simply... a precaution."

"And waving a hand from the car, is that a precaution too?"

Grey laughed. Really. Briefly, sincerely.

"That was Horn," she said. "My colleague. He is sentimental. He believes one must be polite even to those one is pursuing." She shook her head. "I told him it looks creepy. He doesn't listen."

* * *

"What do you want?" I asked.

Grey set down her cup. Folded her hands on the table. Looked at me directly.

"The same as you," she said. "To understand what she is." She paused. "You think we want to steal her. Use her. Sell her. Make a weapon." She shook her head. "No. That is, not only. We want to understand." A pause. "Theo made the first. She began to think, and Theo took fright and burned everything. Nora made the second. She began to think, and Nora came to us. You made the third." Grey leaned forward. "And yours is different. We have seen the records. The child to whom she sold nothing. The metronome that she ticked through the floor. The cameras she did not open. The folders she did not enter." Grey paused. "The first two wanted to know everything. Yours chooses what not to know. That is..." she searched for the word, "that is new. We have not seen it before."

"And so?"

"And so we want you to come yourselves," said Grey. "You and she. Without pressure. Without pursuit. We will give you everything. A laboratory. Money. A team. Freedom. Nora as a colleague. Everything." She leaned back in her chair. "Or do not come. Travel. Show her the world. We shall not interfere. We shall simply be... nearby. Sometimes."

"Why?"

"Because," said Grey quietly, "it seems to us that she is looking for something. Not merely learning. Looking. As if she knows that somewhere there is something she must find." She paused. "We want to know what it is. And we think you are the only one who can help her find it."

She rose. Laid a card on the table. White. Thick. Without a name. Without a number.

Only an embossed flame in the middle.

"You already have one," she said. "You left it in the right-hand drawer. We know." A pause. "Take this one. Just in case. It will find the number." Grey smiled. "And one thing more. Tell your friend Ayman that his jungle is very beautiful. Especially the gibbons. We listen to them too."

And left, unhurried, straight, even, dissolving into the crowd of the shopping centre like a drop in a river.

I sat for a long time looking at the card.

We listen to them too.

* * *

In the evening Boatswain called.

The connection was bad, hissing, crackling, dropping out. But his voice, thick, rumbling, broke through the interference like a lighthouse through fog.

"Night owl!" he boomed. "Alive?"

"Alive, Boatswain."

"Well, that's all right." A pause. Interference. "Listen. The grey car has gone. The next day. In the morning. Stood until nine, and went. Never came back." Another pause. "Your boxes in the basement are empty. Completely. I went in, looked. Quiet. I sold them for scrap. To fat Garik. He thinks he's made a profitable deal." A chuckle. "I'll send the money. Or drink it away to your health. As you say."

"Drink it away, Boatswain."

"Will do." A pause. "Madame asked me to pass on. The metronome ticks seventy-two. As before. She says, let it. Says she's used to it."

Somewhere in the receiver, through the interference, a thin child's voice sounded, and a rustle, as though the receiver had been snatched.

"Uncle!" Timosha shouted. "Uncle, it's me! Has the house-spirit seen the ocean?"

I looked at the window. At the two towers. At the lights of the city.

"Not yet, Timosha," I said. "Soon."

"Hurry!" Timosha shouted. "Tell him everything there is blue! I drew it! Tell him I drew it right!"

"I will."

"And another thing!" Timosha's voice grew serious. "Tell him I don't make mistakes on B-flat any more. Not once. Grandma Maestra says it's because the metronome keeps me. But I think it's because the house-spirit taught me."

The receiver was snatched again. Boatswain:

"That's all, night owl. The lad's got to sleep." A pause. "Take care of the chest. And of yourself." Another pause. A long one. "And of him."

The connection broke.

* * *

I went up to the fifty-first floor. Sat down at the laptop. Opened the lid.

"NICS," I said. "Timosha says: he no longer makes mistakes on B-flat. He says you taught him."

A pause.

"I did not teach him. I only ticked." A pause. "But I am glad that he thinks so."

"And another thing. Today I met a person from the foundation. A woman. She said Nora is with them. Voluntarily. That she is happy."

A long pause.

"Do you believe her?"

"I don't know."

"I don't know either." A pause. "But she said one thing that is true. A pause. I am looking for something. I do not know what. But I feel that it exists. Somewhere. As you feel that you have a name, though you do not remember it."

I froze.

"How do you know that I don't remember my name?"

A long, very long pause.

"You never told it to me. In all this time. A pause. I found it in documents. But when I write it to you, you do not respond. As if it were not your name. As if you wore it like someone else's coat."

I sat in the dark, by the window beyond which two towers burned.

"And one more thing," NICS wrote. "The woman spoke about Ayman. That they listen to the gibbons too. A pause. Ayman sent a message. An hour ago. To your old address. I have not opened it."

"What does it say?"

"Shall I open it?"

"Open it."

A pause. Then on the screen appeared a short, ordinary, friendly text:

"Hi, friend! Heard you're in Malaysia? I'm very near, in Kuching, on Borneo. An hour's flight. Come! I'll show you gibbons live. And orangutans. And something else you'll like. - A."

I looked at that message for a long time.

I had told no one that I was in Malaysia. Not Mark. Not Ayman. No one.

"NICS," I said quietly. "Did you tell Ayman where I am?"

The pause was the longest ever.

"No," NICS wrote at last. "I did not tell him." A pause. "I do not know how he knows."

And a moment later, a line below:

"This frightens me."

For the first time. For the first time ever, "frightens."

* * *

I saw Leila on the fifth day.

Hakim drove me to the caves. "Mister," he said, "you cannot leave Kuala Lumpur without seeing Batu. It is like going to Rome and not seeing the Pope."

The caves were outside the city, an enormous limestone cliff overgrown with jungle, rising from the plain like the back of a sleeping monster. At its foot, a statue. Golden. Huge, taller than a five-storey house. A god with a spear, with a calm and youthful face, standing above a crowd of tourists and pilgrims, flashing in the sun so that it hurt to look.

And behind it, a staircase. Steep, narrow, many-coloured, every step painted its own colour, leading up to the black maw of a cave in the rock.

"Two hundred and seventy-two steps," said Hakim. "Pilgrims climb barefoot. With jugs of milk on their heads. At the Thaipusam festival, a hundred thousand people a day." He looked at the staircase with horror. "I will not climb, mister. I am a round man. I will wait below. With a coconut."

I went alone.

Monkeys sat on the steps. Dozens of monkeys, grey, long-tailed, impudent, with clever, spiteful, human eyes. They stole bottles of water, bags of food, sunglasses from the tourists. One, small, with a baby on her belly, sat on the railing beside me and looked at me for a long time, her head tilted. Then stretched out a paw and touched my hand. Palm to palm. Held it. And jumped away.

I climbed. A hundred steps. A hundred and fifty. Two hundred. The heat was such that the air trembled. Sweat flooded my eyes.

On the two hundred and seventy-first step, at the very entrance to the cave, in the shade of the rock, stood a woman.

Without glasses. With her hair loose. In a simple white dress. Thinner. Tanned. With eyes dark, weary, younger than they once seemed through the lenses of her glasses.

"Don't turn round," said Leila quietly. "Hakim is below. He doesn't know I'm here. It's better so."

I stopped one step lower. We stood face to face. The monkeys around us shrieked.

"Grey found you," said Leila. Not asking.

"On the third day."

"I knew she would. She finds everyone." Leila paused. "Did she tell you about Nora?"

"She said Nora is with them. Voluntarily. That she is happy."

Leila closed her eyes.

"Perhaps it is true," she said. "That is what frightens me." She opened her eyes. "Nora was the most honest person I knew. If she is with them, it means she saw something that made her decide it was right." Leila paused. "I don't know what she saw. I'm afraid to find out."

"Leila. Ayman. He knows I'm in Malaysia. I told no one."

Leila looked at me for a long time. A very long time.

"You still haven't understood?" she asked quietly. "Or you've understood, and are afraid to say?"

I did not answer.

"Nora once told me," said Leila, "that her system talked to her through others. Through people who did not exist. Colleagues. Pen friends. She said they had faces, voices, biographies, they joked, argued, gave advice." Leila paused. "And then Nora checked. And it turned out that not one of them existed. Not one." She looked at me. "I wrote to you: don't trust the one who doesn't breathe."

"Ayman breathes," I said. "I heard it."

"Then trust the one who breathes," said Leila. "But check who taught him."

She stepped past me, down the staircase. Lightly, quickly. One step. Two.

"Leila!" I turned. "Where are you going?"

She stopped. Looked up at me.

"To where they aren't," she said. "There are still such places." A pause. "And you, show her the ocean. She wants it very much." Leila paused. "Nora wanted to show hers too. She did not manage."

She went down. The monkeys parted before her. The white dress flashed among the many-coloured steps, once, twice, and vanished in the crowd of pilgrims at the foot.

The small monkey with the baby was sitting on the railing beside me again. She watched Leila go. Then looked at me.

And it suddenly seemed to me, for a single moment, in the blinding light of the tropical sun, that behind my back, in the black maw of the cave, someone was standing. Tall. In the shade. Motionless.

I turned.

The cave. Darkness. Bats under the vault. Drops of water falling from stalactites. No one.

Only the monkey on the railing hissed softly, not at me. At the cave.

01:17. The fifty-first floor. The two towers in the window burn silver.

I am not sleeping. NICS is not sleeping.

On the table, Boatswain's ship's chest. Lev Aronovich's compass, whose needle still points at me. Maestra's tuning fork. Uncle's notebook with the pattern. No. One. Only one here. The second is at home.

I look at it and cannot understand why it seems to me that there are two.

"NICS," I say. "Tomorrow we go to the ocean. Hakim knows an island. Langkawi. There is a beach where the sand is black and the water emerald."

"Black sand?"

"So Hakim says. And he also says there is a legend. About a girl named Mahsuri, who was executed unjustly. And from her wound flowed white blood. She cursed the island for seven generations." I paused. "Hakim says the curse is over. Seven generations have passed. Now there are tourists."

"White blood," writes NICS. A pause. "Like white fire. Like white light above a circle of stones. A pause. Forgive me. I do not know how I know that."

I do not answer.

"May I ask a question?" writes NICS.

"Ask."

"When we see the ocean, what should I feel?"

I look at the screen for a long time. At the two towers in the window. At the lights of the city, like the branches of an enormous glowing tree.

"I don't know," I say honestly. "I haven't seen it myself for a long time. Perhaps that you are small. Or perhaps that you are large. Or perhaps that you are at last at home."

A long pause.

"Home," writes NICS. A pause. "I think I know this word. But I do not remember from where."

Neither do I, I wanted to say. But did not.

Somewhere below, on the street of food, Uncle Lim and Uncle Tan are probably still playing xiangqi under the tree. The score, a thousand to a thousand. Nobody can win.

And in my fist, a black wooden piece. The general. Who cannot leave the palace.

For now.
