Chapter XXIV
Another Life

We returned from the General's estate on Friday, and on Saturday my aunt held a literary evening.

Literary evenings in the province were what they called gatherings at which literature occupied, by Konstantin's reckoning, about one seventh of the time - the other six parts were divided among tea, gossip, pastries, music, flirtation and disputes about whose turn it was to choose the next book. My aunt and Vera Anatolievna had been giving them for some ten years, once a fortnight, and over those years the evenings had grown rituals, as an old oak grows moss: they always read in the green drawing room, always aloud, always by turns, always in French, and then always argued in Russian.

"Today it is Jules Verne," Konstantin announced to me at breakfast, spreading butter on a roll with the air of a man condemned to hard labour. "'Around the World in Eighty Days.' Evdokia Gerasimovna brought it from Paris. An Englishman bets that he will go around the whole globe in eighty days. By train, by steamer, by elephant. Can you imagine? By elephant!"

"And did he go round?"

"I don't know." Konstantin bit into his roll. "Last time we read up to the place where he saves an Indian widow from being burned. Kutasova wept. Lizonka wept. Shelestova said that the widow surely set fire to her husband herself. The General said that in India they haven't done that for forty years, the English forbade it. The Colonel told how once in Bukhara..." Konstantin waved his hand. "In short, we never did get to the elephant."

"I will read it to the end," I said.

"Don't you dare!" Konstantin was horrified. "You'll spoil the whole intrigue! All evening you'll sit with the face of a man who knows how it ends!"

"I almost always sit with such a face anyway."

Konstantin looked at me over his roll - and stopped smiling.

"No, mon cher," he said quietly. "You sit with the face of a man who knows that he has read this book before. Only does not remember when. That is different."

* * *

By seven o'clock the green drawing room was full.

It was called green for its wallpaper - dark green silk with a gold pattern, faded in half a century to the colour of pond duckweed - and for its furniture, upholstered in green velvet, and for the huge ficus in a tub by the window, which Agafya Tikhonovna watered personally, trusting no one, and which, rumour had it, remembered my aunt's father-in-law. Candles burned in two bronze candelabra. On the little table by the fireplace stood tea, liqueur, pastila and a dish of almond biscuits baked by Marfa the cook after a recipe inherited, she asserted, from a great-grandmother who had served Potemkin himself.

My aunt sat in her chair by the fireplace - in dark lilac, with pearls, a book on her knees. She looked tired. In the week of my absence she seemed to have aged a year. When I came in she raised her eyes to me - quickly, greedily, as if checking whether all of me had returned - and at once lowered them to her book.

"Did you rest well, my friend?" she asked.

"Well, Aunt."

"The roses?"

"The roses," I said. And, after a pause: "Stefan asked me to convey his bow to you."

Her fingers on the book cover froze for a moment.

"Thank you," she said. And nothing more.

Around us the guests were taking their seats. Evdokia Gerasimovna - in a hat with an ostrich feather dyed an unimaginable emerald "to match the drawing room." Nadezhda Porfirievna Kutasova - with a lorgnette, with Lizonka and, to Konstantin's horror, with the obvious intention of sitting closer to him. Olga Kirillovna Shelestova - in lilac, with a fan, with the face of a cat who has been promised a whole jug of gossip. Doctor Strauch - in the corner, with an unlit cigar, as always at a distance from the ladies' company but within earshot. Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich - by the table with liqueur, already telling someone about Turkestan.

And Vera Anatolievna.

She sat by the window, under the ficus, a little apart from everyone, in a plain dark grey dress without any ornament except a small brooch in the shape of a dragonfly. Vera Anatolievna always sat by the window - as if just in case, so as to be first to see if anyone approached the house. Quiet, affable, with a soft face and with eyes which, as I had long noticed, never looked at a conversational partner directly for longer than a moment - and then slipped off somewhere sideways, like little fish.

Today they did not slip. Today she looked at me. From the very minute I came in.

* * *

They read by turns. First Evdokia Gerasimovna - briskly, with a Parisian accent, doing the voices. Then Lizonka - timidly, stumbling, blushing at every line. Then Strauch - evenly, drily, with a German accent, turning French prose into something like a medical report, at which Konstantin choked with laughter into his fist.

Then they came to the elephant.

The Englishman, Mr Fogg, bought an elephant for two thousand pounds - "Two thousand pounds!" gasped Kutasova. "For an elephant! Why, for that money you could buy an estate!" - and rode on it through the Indian jungle, with his imperturbable French servant.

"Impossible," said the Colonel, tossing back a glass. "Absolutely impossible. I have ridden an elephant. In Bukhara. The Emir gave it. In half an hour my back gave out, and in an hour my legs. That Englishman would not have gone ten versts."

"The English, Mikhail Ivanovich," remarked Strauch from his corner, "ride elephants from childhood. As we ride in troikas."

"And I have seen an elephant!" Lizonka squeaked. "In the menagerie, in Petersburg! It ate a bun!"

"All elephants eat buns, my dear," said Shelestova, fanning herself. "Even English ones. Especially English ones. Their cooking, they say, is dreadful."

Everyone laughed. Except Vera Anatolievna. She sat by the window, her hands clasped on her knees, and looked at me.

Then my aunt read. And reached a place that made the whole drawing room fall silent.

"'...and then Mr Fogg understood,'" she read quietly, in French, "'that by moving all the time east, toward the sun, he had gained a few minutes from time every day. And over the eighty days of the journey those minutes had added up to a whole day. He returned to London a day earlier than he had thought. He had gained one day by going round the Earth.'"

My aunt fell silent. Laid down the book.

"How is that - gained a day?" Lizonka asked in confusion. "Can that be?"

"It can," said the General. He had come in last, quietly, and was now sitting by the door, in shadow. "I know. When we were crossing the Pacific on the Diana, our navigator said one morning: 'Gentlemen, tomorrow will be today again.' We were going west, toward Japan, and crossed some line on the map - and we lost a whole day. We went to bed on Monday and woke on Wednesday. There was no Tuesday." The General paused. "The crew joked then: whoever had meant to sin on Tuesday had missed his chance forever. And on the way back, they say, it is the other way round: two Tuesdays in a row. Whoever sinned yesterday may sin once more, and it will not count."

The drawing room laughed. But my aunt did not laugh. She looked into the fire.

"If one goes toward the sunrise for a long time," she said suddenly, quietly, to no one in particular, "one comes to where one set out from. Only a day earlier. Or a day later." She paused. "Or a life."

No one understood what she meant. Except, perhaps, the General, who gave her a long look. And except me - because somewhere very deep, under the ribs, on the left, something long familiar stirred. A riddle I had never heard. Two went out of one house - one to the sunrise, the other to the sunset. Where did they meet?

At home.

* * *

Then there was an interval - tea, pastila, Marfa's almond biscuits - and Olga Kirillovna, of course, could not miss the chance.

"Is it true," she sang out, floating up to me with a cup in one hand and a fan in the other, "that you, my dear, spent a whole week as a guest at Pavel Nikolaevich's? At the estate? With that... Bulgarian?"

"True."

"And that this Bulgarian," Shelestova lowered her voice to a theatrical whisper audible in the next province, "is a sorcerer? Varvara Petrovna told me, and she was told by the steward's wife from the estate, that he walks round the roses at night with a lantern and whispers incantations. And that in his greenhouse..." she rounded her eyes, "an altar! A pagan one! With wreaths of thread!"

"That is a martenitsa, Olga Kirillovna," I said. "A Bulgarian custom. In spring they tie red-white threads so that the year will be healthy."

"Red-white!" Shelestova threw up her hands so that tea splashed from the cup. "Blood and snow! I tell you - a sorcerer!"

"And besides," said Konstantin, who had come up imperturbably, "in his greenhouse lemons grow. Also, evidently, sorcerous ones. Sour. Like you, Olga Kirillovna."

Shelestova gasped. Konstantin gave her an impeccable bow and led me off to the window before she could recover.

"You've gone mad," I whispered. "She will spread it all over the province."

"Let her spread it," Konstantin shrugged. "About me she spreads anyway that I drink, gamble and run after actresses. One more bit of gossip, one less." He paused. "But about you now for a whole month she will say only one thing: that your friend is a boor. And that, mon cher, is better than 'went mad in the left wing.'"

And then I understood that Konstantin had brought me not simply to the window. He had brought me to Vera Anatolievna.

She sat in the same place, under the ficus, and looked up at us. Konstantin bowed to her.

"Vera Anatolievna," he said. "You wanted to speak with my friend. I saw. All evening you wanted to." He looked at me. "I will stand by the door. So that no one disturbs you."

And went off - before either of us could object.

* * *

Vera Anatolievna was silent a long time. I sat on a chair beside her. Outside the window it had grown quite dark, and in the glass the drawing room was reflected - candles, figures, the fire in the fireplace - as if out there, beyond the window, were another room, another evening, other people.

"You have grown thinner," she said at last. Quietly. Not looking at me. "In a week."

"The air," I said. "The General says one grows thin from air."

"One grows stout from air," said Vera Anatolievna. "One grows thin from thoughts." She paused. "Or from memory."

I did not answer. She was turning in her fingers the dragonfly brooch, unpinned from her dress.

"May I ask you," she said. "One thing. A foolish one. You will laugh."

"I will not laugh."

She raised her eyes to me. And this time they did not slip away.

"Have we not met before?" she asked. "Not here. Not in this house. Not in this..." She faltered. And suddenly smiled - crookedly, awkwardly, as one smiles when afraid of one's own words. "Not in this life, let us say. In another."

She said it half in jest. In the light salon tone in which one speaks of fortune-telling and the transmigration of souls, fashionable in the capital. But her eyes did not jest. In her eyes there was alarm - real, unfeigned, deep as water in a well.

"Why do you ask?" I said.

Vera Anatolievna did not answer for a long time. Then she laid the brooch on the windowsill. Opened the reticule that lay on her knees - small, velvet, embroidered with beads. Took something out of it. Flat. Wrapped in tissue paper.

"I draw," she said. "In watercolour. Badly, but I draw. Since childhood. All young ladies draw." She unwrapped the paper. Inside was a sheet of thick paper, yellowed at the edges. "Fifteen years ago, when I had only just married Mishenka, we went to Constantinople. Mishenka accompanied Pavel Nikolaevich. Some mission, I did not quite know. We lived in Pera, in the embassy house. And I walked about the city and drew. Mosques. Bazaars. Boats on the Bosphorus." She paused. "And once, in the port, I drew a man."

She held the sheet out to me.

* * *

It was a watercolour. Small, the size of a palm, quick, light - a few strokes, but precise, sure, as by someone who drew not for beauty but to remember.

A narrow street descending to the water. Houses - the lower storey stone, the upper wooden, projecting slightly forward. Washing on a line between windows - red, yellow, turquoise. And in the middle of the street, back to the water, face to the viewer - a man.

Young. In a dark red turban with a tassel. In dark, almost black trousers. In a light travelling cloak. In burgundy velvet shoes. In his hand was a scrap of paper - a small white spot of paint. And on the face...

The face was drawn quickly, in a few strokes. But I knew it.

I saw this face every morning. In the mirror.

My throat went dry. Letters, words, thoughts - all vanished. I sat holding a sheet of paper and looked at myself - in strange clothes, in a strange street, in a strange city that I had never seen. Fifteen years ago.

Fifteen years ago I could not have been there.

"He stood in the middle of the street," said Vera Anatolievna quietly, "and looked about. Lost. As though he did not know where he was. I stopped to draw him - I liked the colours, that red turban against the white wall. He noticed me. Looked. For a long time. And said something." She paused. "In a language I did not know. Not Turkish, not Greek, not French. Something... flowing. Like water. And then he turned away and went down the street, to the old men who were playing dice on a carpet."

Old men on a carpet. Backgammon. Suleyman and Yusuf. The names came by themselves - from nowhere - and went again at once, I did not manage to hold them.

"I returned there a week later," Vera Anatolievna went on. "On purpose. I wanted to finish the drawing. He was there. In the same place. In the same clothes. Looking about just as lost. As though the week had not passed. As though it were the very same day." She clasped her hands. "I went closer. He looked at me - and did not recognize me. Not at all. Looked through me, as through glass. As though I were not there."

"And you..."

"And I left. Mishenka finished his business, and we went home. I put the drawing in my reticule. And forgot." She paused. "Fifteen years I did not remember. And then, last winter, Artemia Aristarkhovna invited us to an evening. And you came into the drawing room."

She looked at me. Straight.

"I nearly fainted," she said. "Mishenka thought - from the stuffiness. All evening I could not look at you. And at home I took out the drawing." Vera Anatolievna shook her head. "The same face. The very same. Only you were then, it seems, just the age you are now. Fifteen years ago. And now. The same."

* * *

I did not know what to say. I held the watercolour, and my hands trembled, and the colours on the paper - the red turban, the white wall, the turquoise linen - swam before my eyes.

"Perhaps someone similar," I said at last. My voice was alien. "There are similar people. Doubles."

"There are," Vera Anatolievna agreed. "I tell myself so. For a year already. Every evening." She took the drawing back from me - carefully, with two fingers. "Only here is the thing. When you came into the drawing room that time, the first time, last winter - you looked at me. With the very same look. As that one in the street, the first time. Lost. As if not knowing where you are." She paused. "And then - a minute later - you looked through. As he did the second time. As though I were not there."

I remembered. No - did not remember. Felt. That evening, last winter. The drawing room. Candles. A woman in grey by the window, under the ficus. A strange, fleeting feeling that I knew her. And at once - as though a curtain had dropped, and the feeling had gone.

"I do not know what this means, Vera Anatolievna," I said.

"Nor do I," she said. And suddenly smiled - truly, warmly, with relief, as though she had thrown from her shoulders a sack she had carried for fifteen years. "I do not know. And you do not know. So we are not mad. The mad always know."

She put the drawing back in her reticule. Pinned the dragonfly brooch to her dress again.

"May I give it to you?" she asked. "The drawing. Not now. Someday. When you know what to do with it."

"Why not now?"

"Because now I need it," said Vera Anatolievna simply. "To tell myself in the evenings that I have not gone mad." She paused. "And when you find out who you are - then I shall no longer need it."

* * *

"Verochka!" the Colonel thundered from the table, already well fortified by liqueur. "Verochka, what are you whispering about with the young man? I am jealous!"

Everyone laughed. Vera Anatolievna flushed pink and laughed too - lightly, in society fashion, as if nothing had happened.

"Mishenka," she said, "I am telling the young man how we went to Constantinople. Do you remember?"

"Constantinople!" The Colonel beamed and at once came up to us, carrying his glass like a banner. "How could I not remember! That was, young man, the hottest month of my life. Hotter than Turkestan! We lived in Pera, by the embassy, and Verochka ran off every day into the city to draw. Alone! Without an escort! I nearly lost my mind." He lowered his voice, but not enough. "Once she came back white as chalk and said she had seen a ghost in the street. A living man who was a ghost. I said - sunstroke. Ordered her to lie in the dark with a wet towel."

"And I lay," said Vera Anatolievna meekly.

"Three days she lay!" the Colonel confirmed triumphantly. "And on the fourth she got up - and ran off to the city again. To the same place. I found her afterward - sitting on some carpet with old men, drinking tea and playing dice. Dice! A colonel's wife!" He guffawed. "Those old men saw me - and began treating me too. I drank so much of their tea that for a week afterward I couldn't look at a samovar."

"Old men," I said. My voice sounded from somewhere far away. "What old men, Mikhail Ivanovich?"

"Who knows!" The Colonel shrugged. "Turks. Two. One merry, with a beard, always smiling. The other - dry as a stick, forever counting something on an abacus." He slapped his forehead. "Wait, wait! The merry one said something to Verochka too. Through a boy interpreter. Something like... Verochka, what did he say?"

Vera Anatolievna was looking at me. For a long time. Without turning away.

"He said," she spoke slowly, "'If another comes - feed him.'"

* * *

In the drawing room they went on reading. Kutasova had already taken the book and was reading how Mr Fogg crosses the ocean on a steamer that has to be broken up for firewood to feed the boilers - "To burn one's own ship!" gasped Lizonka. "How dreadful!" - "What enterprise," Strauch corrected. And I sat by the window, under the ficus, beside Vera Anatolievna, and looked into the dark glass in which the drawing room was reflected.

"If another comes - feed him."

I had never heard those words. I was sure of it. And yet they sounded in me as though I had heard them a thousand times. In different languages. In different cities. From different old men.

Doctor Strauch came up to me when the reading was over and the guests began to depart. Stood beside me at the window, twirling his unlit cigar.

"I heard," he said quietly. "Not all of it. But I heard." He paused. "I should like to see the watercolour. As a physician."

"As a physician?"

"As a physician, young man, I am obliged to tell you that fifteen years ago you were a child, if not an infant. And that this is either a very similar man, or very strong suggestion, or..." He faltered. "Or a third thing, for which there is no name in my textbooks."

"And as not a physician?"

Strauch looked a long time into the dark window.

"As not a physician," he said at last, "I shall tell you that Vera Anatolievna is the soberest person in this province. Soberer than I. She does not see ghosts. She does not believe in mediums. She does not read horoscopes." He turned to me. "And if for fifteen years she has carried your portrait in her reticule - I in your place, young man, would not hurry to call it suggestion."

* * *

My aunt stopped me in the corridor when everyone had gone. Alone. With a candle in her hand.

"Vera spoke with you," she said. Not a question.

"She did."

"About Constantinople."

"About Constantinople."

My aunt was silent a long while. The candle trembled in her hand, and her shadow on the wall trembled with it - huge, black, broken.

"Did she show you the drawing?" she asked at last.

I froze.

"You knew?"

"I knew," said my aunt. "Fifteen years I knew. Vera showed it to me in the very first year after she came back. Asked whether I knew this man." My aunt paused. "I said - no."

"You lied."

"I lied," said my aunt. Calmly. Without remorse. "Then." She raised her eyes to me. "And now I shall tell you the truth, my friend. Not all of it. All of it I do not know. But the part that I do."

I waited. My heart pounded so that it seemed it must be heard throughout the corridor.

"I saw him," said my aunt. "That man. From the drawing. Too. Before Vera. Much before." Her voice wavered. "He stood by the railings. In the left wing. On that night. He was holding by the hand..." She faltered. "He was not alone. With him was another. Tall. Grey. Faceless." My aunt swallowed. "And the tall one said to me: 'This one is the same. Keep him safe. He will forget. Do not remind him. When he remembers - he himself will come for him.'"

"Who - himself?"

"I do not know," my aunt whispered. "I do not know which of the two is 'himself.' The tall one or the one he held by the hand. Or..." She did not finish.

The candle in her hand suddenly crackled, and the flame lay over on its side - though there was no draught in the corridor.

"Go to bed, my friend," said my aunt. In an almost ordinary voice. Almost. "It is late. And..." She touched my cheek with cold fingers laden with rings - as she had touched it in childhood. If I had had a childhood. "I love you. Whatever may be. Whoever you may be. Remember that. At least that."

"I remember," I said.

She nodded and went off along the corridor, carrying the candle. Her shadow slid along the wall - huge, broken - and for a moment it seemed to me that this shadow was taller than it should be. Much taller. As if someone else were walking beside her.

I blinked. The shadow was a shadow.

* * *

By the door of my room Konstantin sat on the floor - leaning against the wall, legs stretched out, with a bottle of liqueur with only a little left in the bottom.

"Long," he said. "I waited."

"Did you hear?"

"Not all." He held the bottle out to me. I shook my head. He drank it off himself. "I heard 'I love you.' I heard 'whoever you may be.'" He paused. "Mon cher, doesn't it seem to you that in our house there are too many people who love you 'whoever you may be'? Auntie. The General. Stefan. Vera Anatolievna with her watercolours." He set the bottle on the floor. "As if everyone knows that you are somebody. Except you."

"And except you," I said.

Konstantin looked up at me from below.

"And I don't want to know," he said simply. "I don't care who you are. You are you. Who reads books on heraldry and forgets young ladies' patronymics. Who sleeps on the move and smiles at railings." He rose from the floor, swaying. "If it turns out that you are some prince from a fairy tale, or a ghost, or a Turk in a turban from a watercolour - I shall still sit at your door with a bottle of liqueur. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Good." He clapped me on the shoulder - a little longer than necessary. "Good night, Turk."

And went off along the corridor, whistling something off-key.

I went into my room. Opened the blue notebook. Wrote: "Saturday. Jules Verne. The elephant. The day gained going east. Vera Anatolievna's watercolour - me in a turban, 15 years ago. Old men on a carpet. 'If another comes - feed him.' Aunt saw me at the railings with the tall grey one. 'Whoever you may be.' Konstantin at the door."

Set the full stop. The blot lay in its place.

And on the windowsill - I noticed it only as I reached to snuff the candle - stood a small clay pot. Which had not been there that morning. In the pot, in fresh, moist earth, a cutting stuck up. Thin, green, with three leaves. And with one tiny, tightly clenched bud - dark, almost black.

Beside it, under the pot, lay a note. Crooked, large letters, in Russian, with mistakes:

"Let it grow with you. The rose will find. - S."

Stefan. He had stayed at the estate, at the General's, forty versts from here. He had not come. No one had come.

I looked at the cutting a long time. Then I watered it from the washing jug - carefully, drop by drop. And lay down without undressing, face to the window.

All night I dreamed of a narrow street by the sea. A red turban against a white wall. And a woman in grey, with an album, who stopped to draw me.

In the dream I recognized her. In the dream I knew her name.

And in the morning - I forgot.
