Chapter XVI
A Fragment in the Archive

I had done the right thing. I knew I had done the right thing. Nebamun had said: not through the main corridor, the walls have ears, and some of those ears wear bracelets. And I had followed his advice.

But I had not expected it to be so unpleasant.

It happened at the morning audience, two days after the night in the temple granary. The hall was full - petitioners, scribes, dignitaries, guards - and at the very foot of the throne, as always, sat Bakenren, who had come to "humbly remind" me of the Temple's petition for three thousand hekats for the festival of Opet. He smiled. He bowed. He was so courteous that my jaw ached.

And in the very midst of his courteous speech the doors of the hall opened a crack, and Iti slipped through. With an ink stain on his cheek, with some scrolls under his arm, out of breath - he had evidently run from the Library on an errand for Amenemope. He bowed at the doors and froze, waiting for a sign.

I saw Bakenren's eyes slide toward him. Swiftly, fleetingly, the way a lizard's glance slides over a fly. I saw the bracelets chime by the far column - Khaemuas, who stood there among the commanders, had turned his head.

"Who allowed you to enter during an audience?" I said. Loudly. Coldly. As I had never spoken to him before. "Scribes of the Library have no business in the throne room. If Amenemope has a matter for Pharaoh, let him send a messenger. Go."

The hall fell silent. Bakenren raised his brows slightly - with interest.

Iti stood frozen in the doorway. His face - I could see it even across the whole hall - first went pale, then flared red to the very roots of his hair. He opened his mouth as if to say something. Closed it. Bowed - low, awkwardly, so that the scrolls nearly slipped from under his arm - and went out without raising his eyes.

The doors closed behind him.

"Young people these days have no notion of their place, my Pharaoh," Bakenren remarked sweetly. "In my years a scribe did not dare to breathe in the presence of a living god."

"Continue, Bakenren," I said evenly. "You were speaking of piety."

He continued. I did not hear a word. I looked at the closed doors and felt as though I had just struck a dog that had been running to me, wagging its tail.

* * *

He did not come that evening, nor the next.

The note from Pentu - the first one, with a copy of the true inventory of the granaries of Heliopolis - was brought to me not by Iti but by the boy Paney. He came into my chambers late in the evening, when I had already dismissed the servants - came in through the door to the garden, barefoot, with a bandaged hand in which a tightly rolled papyrus was clutched.

"From Iti, my Pharaoh," he said, holding out the scroll. And he added, with that directness with which only children and old men speak: "He did not come himself. He said - let Pharaoh not have to see him more than necessary. Since it is unpleasant for him."

"He said that?"

"He said exactly that, my Pharaoh." Paney hesitated, shifting his bare feet. "He said a good many other things too. Only they are not for Pharaoh."

"Tell me anyway."

Paney looked at me gravely, weighing whether he should.

"He said: 'Nebamun said - not through the main corridor. And Pharaoh, it turns out, has decided - not at all.'" The boy sniffed. "And he said that a scribe of the Library is, of course, nobody and nothing. And that he, of course, knows his place. And that his place is among the dusty scrolls where nobody goes. And that he is happy there." Paney paused. "Only he said it, my Pharaoh, the way people say things when they are not happy."

I was silent for a long time. Paney waited.

"Paney," I said at last. "You are his pupil. Tell me honestly. Was he hurt?"

Paney thought. Then he nodded - slowly, thoroughly, as Senusret nods.

"Very, my Pharaoh. He spent half the night copying the same scroll. Three times. And three times he spoiled it. Amenemope said that in twenty years he had never seen Iti spoil a scroll. And today Iti deliberately scattered all my potsherds with letters, and then gathered them up himself and apologized." The boy spread his hands. "He is like my father, my Pharaoh, when Mama was angry with him. He drops everything."

I could not help it - I smiled. Paney, seeing this, seemed to relax.

"Paney," I said. "Pass something on to him. Not a note - by word of mouth. Say: the walls have ears. The garden does not. And Pharaoh, too, sometimes walks in the garden."

Paney pondered, moving his lips, memorizing. Then he nodded.

"I will pass it on, my Pharaoh." And at the door he turned: "May I ask a riddle? Iti gave it to me and I cannot solve it. Perhaps you can?"

"Go on."

"'Who speaks without a mouth? Who remembers without a heart? Who lives a thousand years, yet dies from a single drop of water?'"

I thought.

"A scroll," I said. "Papyrus. It speaks without a mouth - by what is written. It remembers without a heart - everything that is recorded in it. It lives a thousand years in a dry storehouse - but a single drop of water washes the ink away."

Paney stared at me with admiration.

"Iti said you would solve it," he said. "He said: 'Pharaoh solves everything. Except what is right under his nose.'" And before I could ask what that meant, he darted out into the garden.

* * *

The gossip was brought the next morning by Nefret - the senior woman over the palace servants, a woman of enormous size, with a voice like a temple trumpet and a memory which, by common opinion, held more information about palace affairs than all the scrolls of the Library. She came herself - supposedly to check whether my linen garments had been properly laundered - and as she checked, she talked without pause.

"...and the keeper of the seal's wife has borne a girl again, the fifth, my Pharaoh, and now he goes about blacker than a storm cloud, says the gods are laughing at him... and the cook from the kitchen quarrelled with the baker over whose leaven is better, and now they are not speaking, and the bread, mind you, is suffering... and the nomarch Ankhtifi has sent a bearskin as a gift for the hall, and we have never had bears here, so everyone is wondering where he got it..."

I listened with half an ear, trying not to betray my impatience.

"...and another thing, my Pharaoh," Nefret lowered her trumpet voice to what she herself must have considered a whisper but which could in fact be heard in the neighbouring chambers, "they say that the junior keeper of the Library, that boy, Iti, goes to the temple garden at night. Not alone. He has been seen with a young priestess. The big-eyed one. Khemu. By the pond. Whispering, they say. Passing something to each other." She pursed her lips meaningfully. "Well, they are young. Priestesses, of course, are not supposed to. But who can hold them back when it is spring?"

I froze with the cup in my hand.

Something pricked me - somewhere under the ribs, but not on the left, where the familiar cold lived, but on the right, in a place entirely new and unknown. Swift, sharp, like the prick of a fish bone. And at once it let go.

Of course they meet by the pond. Of course they whisper and pass things - I myself had told Khemu to send word through Iti. He carries the notes. He does exactly what I asked. No spring. No "they are young."

I knew this. And yet for several moments, while Nefret, noticing nothing, went on about the bearskin, I sat motionless and listened to that strange, swift prick - trying to understand what it was.

Concern for them, I decided. If Nefret knows - then the whole court knows. Then Bakenren knows. Then Khemu is in danger. And Iti. That was what it was. Alarm. Simply alarm for two people who are helping me.

Of course.

"Nefret," I said. "And who else is talking about this?"

"Oh, my Pharaoh," she waved her plump hands, "everyone! The laundresses. The water-carriers. The gardeners. They even say that one of the men of the honourable Khaemuas asked the gardener whether the boy goes to the pond often." She giggled. "Perhaps he is jealous too."

I set down the cup.

"Nefret. When you speak with the laundresses - tell them this. The junior keeper of the Library is, by my order, collecting ancient inscriptions for the temple pond. From the garden walls. For copying. The priestess shows him where they are. That is all."

Nefret gave me a long, shrewd, not at all foolish look.

"Inscriptions," she said. "From the walls. At night." She nodded. "Of course, my Pharaoh. I shall say so. The laundresses will believe it." She gathered up the garments. "The water-carriers too. But the gardeners..." She shrugged her mighty shoulders. "The gardeners, my Pharaoh, know there are no inscriptions on the walls of the temple garden. But I shall tell them there are. The gardeners are afraid of me."

She left. I looked after her for a long time and thought that perhaps I had underestimated the palace servants.

* * *

He came to the garden on the third night.

I was waiting for him by the old fig tree at the far end of the garden, where the wall was overgrown with jasmine so thickly that a whole chariot could have been hidden behind it. The moon was thin, almost invisible, and the garden lay in darkness full of scents - jasmine, damp earth, the blooming lotus in the little pond - and of sounds: cicadas, the distant barking of a dog in the city, the rustle of the night wind in the palm leaves.

He appeared soundlessly, as he always did - I heard him only when he was already standing three paces away, at the very jasmine. In his hands was not a scroll but something wrapped in a piece of coarse linen. He pressed it to his chest with both hands, the way one holds a small child or a very fragile thing.

"My Pharaoh," he said, and bowed. Correctly. Precisely. Coldly. The way one bows not to a man but to a throne.

"Iti," I said.

He straightened and looked at me. Not in the eyes - slightly higher, at my forehead, as scribes are taught to look when they speak with exalted persons.

"You are angry with me," I said.

"A scribe of the Library does not dare to be angry with Pharaoh, my Pharaoh," he answered in an even voice, as if reading from a scroll. "Pharaoh was right. Scribes of the Library have no business in the throne room. I know my place."

"Iti."

"My place is among the scrolls, my Pharaoh. Where it is dusty and nobody goes. I am happy there."

"Iti, look at me."

He hesitated - and looked. And in the darkness, in the thin light of the almost invisible moon, I saw that his eyes were shining.

"Bakenren was in the hall," I said quietly. "Khaemuas stood by the column. They both looked at you when you came in. The way one looks at a new piece on the board - calculating where to move it. I could not allow them to see that you are to me..." I faltered. "That you are important to me. Nebamun said: the walls have ears. I made sure the ears heard that the boy from the Library is nobody. So that they would stop looking at you."

Iti was silent.

"I did not think," I said, "that you would hear it too. And believe it too."

He was silent a long while. Then he lowered his eyes - to the bundle he was pressing to his chest.

"Paney passed it on to me," he said softly, no longer like a scribe but in his own voice. "About the ears. And about the garden." He paused. "For two days I thought that you... that Pharaoh..."

"That what?"

"Nothing, my Pharaoh." He suddenly smiled - crookedly, awkwardly, as if he had forgotten how in these days. "Paney said that you solved the riddle about the scroll."

"I did."

"I set it on purpose," said Iti. "I knew you would solve it. I wanted..." He faltered. "I wanted you to think about something connected with me, at least once. Even if it was only a riddle."

I found nothing to answer. On his cheek, in the thin moonlight, a stain showed dark - ink, as always. I raised my hand, not knowing why, and wiped it away with my thumb. Simply wiped it away. The way one wipes a smudge from a younger brother's face.

Iti went utterly still. He seemed to have stopped breathing.

"You always have ink on your face," I said, lowering my hand. "How do you manage it?"

"I... rub my cheek when I think, my Pharaoh," he whispered. "With the brush. I forget it is in my hand."

"So you think a great deal."

"A great deal, my Pharaoh," said Iti. And after a pause he added, so quietly that I barely heard: "Especially these last two days."

Somewhere in the garden a night bird cried out. Iti started, as if waking, and hurriedly held out the bundle to me.

"That is not why I came, my Pharaoh. That is... not only why. Here. I found this. In the lower storehouse. Yesterday." His voice was businesslike again, quick, but his fingers, unwrapping the linen, trembled slightly. "I have shown it to no one. Not even Amenemope. Because..." He raised his eyes to me. "Because, my Pharaoh, I think it is what the High Priest is looking for."

* * *

Hardly anyone ever went down to the lower storehouse of the Library, Iti told me, while by the light of a small clay lamp, which he had taken from his bosom and lit from a flint, I examined his find. Into the lower storehouse they carried what was considered spoiled: scrolls gnawed by mice, washed out by water, scorched in some old fire, cracked tablets, shards of clay tablets with half-effaced signs. What is written may not be thrown away - that is sacrilege, the word of Thoth cannot be thrown away - so all of it was put into huge clay jars, sealed, and set in rows in an underground vault where it was dry, dark, and smelled of mice and eternity.

Over this subterranean kingdom ruled old Isiret - one of the few women scribes at court, dry as a stick, with a face like a crumpled papyrus, a tongue sharp as a reed brush, and a memory which, according to Iti, was more terrible than any archive.

"She drove me out," Iti told me while I looked at the papyrus. "On the first day. She said that the boys of the upper Library know only how to copy beautiful scrolls for Pharaoh, and do not want to do the real work - sorting what the mice have devoured. I said that I wanted to. She looked at me and said: 'Very well. Here is a jar for you. No one has been in it for three hundred years. Sort it out. If you find even one whole line, I will kiss you.'" Iti blushed. "I did not, of course, want her to kiss me. She is a hundred years old and has three teeth. But I sorted it out."

"And you found it?"

"I found it, my Pharaoh." He pointed to the papyrus in my hands. "This. At the very bottom of the jar. Wrapped in leather. The leather had almost rotted away, and the papyrus had not. As though someone had hidden it on purpose among the refuse. Where no one would look."

The papyrus was small - two palms long, one wide. Its edges were frayed, one corner was eaten away as if by mice, but the middle had survived remarkably well. And on it were signs.

Not ours.

Not hieroglyphs, not the cursive of the priests, not the counting marks of the scribes. Long, flowing, curving lines, running one into another without breaks, with no clear boundary between the signs - like water pouring over stone. Like the trace a retreating wave leaves on the sand.

I had never seen such writing. I was certain that I had never seen it.

And yet, looking at it, I felt something pass through the fingers that held the papyrus, from the fingertips to the wrist - neither warmth nor cold - something like recognition. The very feeling one has on hearing a melody known long ago, when one has forgotten where it was heard.

"My Pharaoh?" Iti asked quietly. "Can you... can you read it?"

"No," I said. "I cannot. But..."

"But?"

I did not answer. I looked at the signs, and it seemed to me - this was, of course, a trick of the lamp, of the thin, trembling flame - that the lines on the papyrus were faintly stirring. Not changing - no. Simply as if breathing. Like a sleeping beast.

* * *

"That is not all," said Iti. "Turn it over."

I turned it over.

On the reverse side, in the very corner, there was an inscription. Small, faded almost to invisibility. In our signs. Ancient, angular ones, such as were written under the very first Pharaohs - but ours.

Iti bent to the lamp beside me, so close that I could feel the warmth of his shoulder, and read aloud, slowly, moving his finger along the signs:

"'Words of the First Ones. Sent down from the Heavens together with the Two Eyes. Do not read. Do not show. Keep until the appointed time.'"

Keep until the appointed time.

I read it again. Then once more. The words were ordinary - the most ordinary words, such as are written on jars of grain and on chests of dowry. Keep until the appointed time.

But somewhere inside me - very far away, as if many days' journey down the river, or many years, or something else entirely that had no name - these words resounded as though spoken by someone I knew. Someone who held in his hand a similar scrap of paper - not papyrus but paper, thin, white, such as is not made among us. On a narrow, sun-flooded street. In a strange city smelling of the sea and of spices.

I blinked. The vision - if it was a vision - vanished.

"There is a drawing as well," said Iti. "Under the inscription. Very pale."

I brought the papyrus right up to the flame. Beneath the ancient signs there was indeed a drawing - thin, faded, almost wiped away. Two circles. One filled in. The other empty. And between them a wavy line.

"The Two Eyes," Iti whispered. "Black and white. And a road between them. Or a river."

We sat crouching under the jasmine, shoulder to shoulder, bent over a small scrap of papyrus that had lain at the bottom of a jar of refuse for three hundred years - and said nothing. The cicadas sang. The lamp crackled.

"Iti," I said at last. "Who else knows?"

"No one, my Pharaoh. Isiret saw that I had found something, but I said it was just a scrap of a prayer. She believed me." He paused. "I think she believed me."

"You think?"

"Isiret never shows whether she believes or not, my Pharaoh. She says that belief is for priests, and for scribes there is only verification."

* * *

We heard footsteps - light, quick - and both of us started. Iti blew out the lamp. I thrust the papyrus into my bosom.

From behind the fig tree came Khemu. Without a lamp, in a dark cloak over her white priestess's robes, breathless, her hair disordered.

"Iti!" she whispered, not at once making me out in the darkness. "Iti, trouble, Bakenren..." And then she saw me. She froze. She fell to her knees. "My... my Pharaoh!"

"Rise. What has happened?"

"Bakenren," Khemu gasped, getting up. "This evening he came to the Library. To Amenemope. He demanded the Most Ancient Epic. Again. The fourth time this moon. And then..." She caught her breath. "Then he asked about the lower storehouse. He asked who works there. Amenemope said - old Isiret. And the boy. Iti. Bakenren asked what the boy was looking for there. Amenemope said - sorting spoiled scrolls. Bakenren said: 'How interesting. I should like to see what he has already sorted.' And he ordered that tomorrow at dawn Isiret show him all the jars the boy has opened."

Iti turned so white that it was visible even in the dark.

"How do you know?" I asked.

"Merit heard," said Khemu. "She was in the Library - she had come for a scroll of hymns. She told me to run and warn you. She said: 'Little bird, run to the boy. The fox has gone to the henhouse.'"

"The jar," Iti whispered. "I left the jar open. And the leather the papyrus was wrapped in. It is there, at the bottom. If Bakenren sees it..."

"He will understand that something was there," I finished.

The three of us stood under the jasmine in the darkness, and no one said a word.

* * *

"Isiret," Iti said at last. "We must go to Isiret. Now."

"She will betray you," said Khemu.

"Perhaps," said Iti. "And perhaps not. I do not know whose side she is on. No one knows. But she is the only one who can make sure Bakenren finds nothing."

"I will go with you," I said.

They both stared at me.

"My Pharaoh," said Khemu. "If you are seen in the lower storehouse at night..."

"If I am seen with you in the temple garden at night, it will be worse," I said. "The palace is already whispering that you two meet by the pond." I paused. "That you, Iti, go to Khemu because of the spring."

Iti choked. Khemu snorted - nervously, briefly - and at once clapped her palm over her mouth.

"To me?" she repeated. "Him?" She looked at Iti, then at me, and something mischievous, quite girlish, flickered in her huge, kohl-rimmed eyes. "My Pharaoh, with all respect - Iti does not go to the garden for me. Iti goes to the garden..." She broke off, looked at Iti, who stood red as a crab, and finished in quite a different tone: "...for the inscriptions. From the walls. For copying."

"Nefret has already told the laundresses so," I nodded.

"Nefret?" Khemu raised her brows. "My Pharaoh, if Nefret is on your side, you have no need of an army."

Iti, still red, muttered something under his breath. I did not catch what. But it seemed to me - quite absurdly, at such a moment - that I felt lighter. There, on the right, under the ribs, where two days ago it had pricked.

* * *

The lower storehouse lay beneath the Library - a narrow stair hewn in the rock descended into the darkness, and with each step the air grew drier, cooler, and heavier with the smell of dust, mouse droppings, and old, very old papyrus. Below stretched a long corridor, and along it, in niches, stood rows of jars - huge, man-high, sealed with clay, with signs scratched on their sides: the year, the storehouse, "spoiled by water," "spoiled by fire," "spoiled by mice."

At the end of the corridor a lamp was burning. And beside it, on a low stool, sat an old woman.

Isiret was exactly as Iti had described her - only more terrible. Small, shrivelled, almost bodiless, in a dark robe, with a face so densely furrowed that it looked like a map of all the roads of the Valley, and with eyes - black, shining, alive - that in the lamplight seemed two drops of ink on old papyrus. Before her on a mat lay potsherds, and she, without raising her head, was fitting them together like a mosaic - piece to piece, sign to sign.

"You took your time, boy," she said without turning. "I thought you would come running as soon as Bakenren left. And you have, I see, brought guests besides." At last she turned her head. She looked at Khemu. At me. She held her gaze on me - long, appraising, without the slightest reverence. "Ah. Himself. A living god. In a cellar. With the mice." She snorted. "Well, then. The gods, they say, are everywhere. Even in spoiled jars."

Khemu gasped. Iti turned pale. I did not know whether to laugh or be angry - and Isiret, it seems, read this on my face, for her three teeth flashed in a smile.

"Sit down, living god," she said, nodding at the next jar. "I have no stools. Kings do not come here. Except once in three hundred years."

"Once in three hundred years?" I repeated.

"And you think you are the first?" Isiret bent again over her potsherds. "The jar the boy opened was sealed by a scribe named Hekanakht son of Pepi. I read his seal. He was keeper of this vault three hundred years ago. Under a Pharaoh whose name has been erased from half the walls of the Valley." She fitted one sherd to another. "And in the scroll of Merira son of Pepi, which lies over there in the third jar from the stair, it is written that one night Pharaoh himself came down into the storehouse. Alone. Without guards. And brought something wrapped in leather. And ordered it hidden where no one would look. 'Until the appointed time,' he said."

It grew so quiet in the vault that I could hear a mouse scratching somewhere in the darkness behind the jars.

"Which Pharaoh?" I asked.

"The one whose name was erased," said Isiret. "I told you." She raised her black, shining eyes to me. "Hekanakht son of Pepi writes that Pharaoh was young. That he came down here on the eve of a journey south. To the festival. In Luxor." She paused. "And that he did not return from the festival. Or rather - he returned. But different. He looked through people. As if he saw something else behind them."

Paser. The council of commanders. The old legend that is told in whispers.

The cold under the ribs - on the left, familiar, known - rose so swiftly that it took my breath away.

* * *

"Bakenren will come at dawn," said Isiret, returning to her potsherds. "He wants to see the jars the boy opened. Very well. I shall show him." She nodded to Iti. "Boy, go to the fifth jar from the stair. The one 'spoiled by water.' There is a scroll of grain accounts from the time of some long-forgotten Pharaoh. Of no use to anyone. Sodden. Bring it. And the leather from your jar, too."

Iti ran. He came back with the sodden scroll and a rotted piece of leather. Isiret, without looking, wrapped the scroll in the leather, tied it with a scrap of cord, and threw it into the jar from which Iti had taken the papyrus.

"There," she said. "Tomorrow Bakenren will see that the boy found in the jar, wrapped in leather, an account of grain. Sodden. Useless. In front of him I shall unwrap it, show it, grumble at the boy for wasting his time on refuse. Bakenren will laugh and leave. Priests like to laugh at scribes. It consoles them."

"Why are you helping us?" I asked.

Isiret did not answer for a long time. She fitted potsherds together.

"Do you know a riddle, living god?" she said at last. "An old one. It is asked only in this cellar. 'Which is stronger - the one who writes, or the one who erases?'"

I thought.

"The one who erases. He can destroy what is written."

"Wrong." Isiret set the last sherd in place - and the sherds on the mat suddenly came together into a whole sign. An Eye. "Stronger is the one who remembers. What is erased can be remembered. What is written can be forgotten. And the one who remembers is stronger than both. I remember everything that lies in this cellar, living god. Every jar. Every potsherd. Twenty years. And I remember that the High Priest has never come down here. Not once. Until today. And today he came down - and the first thing he asked about was the boy. So he knows there is something here that he did not find in the Epic. And if the High Priest is searching so hard for something, it is better that he not find it."

She paused.

"And besides," she added, unexpectedly gently, "the boy sorted my jar. The whole of it. In a single night. Which no one had touched for three hundred years." She looked at Iti, and her wrinkled face suddenly became almost tender. "I promised him a kiss for one whole line."

Iti stepped back a pace, turning pale.

"Do not be afraid, boy." Isiret giggled - dryly, with a rattle. "Three teeth are all I have left. I shall save the kiss for someone who needs it more." She moved her black eyes from Iti to me and back - once, swiftly, the way one shifts one's gaze from one line to another, checking that it has been copied correctly. "For someone who will wipe the ink from your cheek. Otherwise you go about like a spotted cat."

Iti turned crimson. Khemu, standing by the stair, turned to the wall - and her shoulders shook.

I looked at old Isiret and thought that in all the palace there seemed to be not a single person who did not see more than he showed.

* * *

We climbed the stair in silence. At the exit from the vault, in the darkness of the Library, Khemu touched my hand.

"My Pharaoh," she whispered. "That inscription. 'Keep until the appointed time.' I have seen it before."

"Where?"

"On a chest. The one of black wood. In the treasury. On the lid, from the inside." She swallowed. "I did not understand then what it was. I thought they were just words. And now..."

She did not finish. We both knew what she had wanted to say.

Iti walked with me as far as the garden. At the fig tree, under the jasmine, he stopped.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "The papyrus. What will you do with it?"

I touched my chest, where under my garment the bundle lay.

"Keep it," I said. "Until the appointed time."

Iti nodded. He stood a little longer. Then he said - very quietly, looking not at me but at the jasmine:

"My Pharaoh. When you said 'go' in the hall... For two days I thought I would never come again."

"And you came."

"And I came," he agreed. "Because I found the papyrus. And I thought: Pharaoh needs this. Even if Pharaoh does not need me."

"Iti," I said.

He raised his eyes to me.

I did not know what to say. I - Pharaoh, living god, lord of the Two Lands - stood under the jasmine in the darkness before the junior keeper of the Library with his perpetual ink stain on his cheek, and did not know what to say. Every word that came to my mind was either too large or too small.

"Come tomorrow," I said at last. "Through the garden. Bring me another riddle."

He smiled - truly, for the first time in these days, so that it could be seen even in the dark.

"Which one, my Pharaoh?"

"Any. One that I cannot solve."

"There is no such one," said Iti. "Paney says you solve everything."

"Paney says that you said: except what is right under my nose."

Iti froze. Then he blushed - so that it showed even in the light of the thin moon - muttered something unintelligible, bowed - awkwardly, hurriedly, not at all like a scribe - and vanished behind the jasmine so fast that it was as though all the crocodiles of the Nile were after him.

I stood alone under the fig tree, pressing to my chest the papyrus on which was written what I could not read, and words that I somehow knew. Keep until the appointed time.

And I thought not of Bakenren. Not of the High Priest. Not of the Pharaoh whose name had been erased from half the walls of the Valley and who three hundred years ago had descended into the vault on the eve of the festival at Luxor.

I thought of what exactly Iti had meant when he said: except what is right under his nose.

And of why I so wanted to solve it.

* * *

When I returned to my chambers, old Amenemope was waiting for me at the doors - for the first time in all the time I had known him, risen by himself in the night from the Library. He stood leaning on his black staff and looked at me with the same faded, all-seeing gaze with which all the old men of this palace had looked at me today.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "The boy found something below. I know. I do not ask what." He paused. "I have come to tell you a tale. An old one. It was told to me when I was like Iti. About the god Thoth and Pharaoh."

"Now? At night?"

"Tales, my Pharaoh, are better heard at night," said Amenemope. "By day they seem untrue."

I let him in. He sat on the bench by the window, laid his staff across his knees, and began to speak - slowly, quietly, the way one tells a story looking not at the listener but into one's own memory:

"'Once the god Thoth, lord of writing, came to the Pharaoh who ruled in Thebes and brought him his inventions. Number. The measuring of land. The stars. The game of senet. And writing. "Here," said Thoth, "is writing. It will make the Egyptians wiser and strengthen their memory. I have found a remedy for memory." But Pharaoh shook his head. "O most skilful Thoth," he said, "you, the father of writing, out of love for it ascribe to it a power opposite to the one it has. Writing will not strengthen memory - it will weaken it. People, having learned to write, will cease to exercise their memory. They will remember not from within, but from without - by the signs of others. You have found a remedy not for memory but for reminding."'"

Amenemope fell silent.

"And what did Thoth do?" I asked.

"The tale does not say, my Pharaoh," answered the old man. "But writing remained. And since then we keep scrolls instead of remembering." He rose, leaning on his staff. "I think of this tale each time I see a man who does not remember himself. Perhaps he need not look for the scroll on which his name is written. Perhaps he needs to remember from within."

He bowed and went to the door.

"Amenemope," I said after him. "Do you see a man who does not remember himself?"

The old man stopped in the doorway. Turned.

"Every day, my Pharaoh," he said. "Every day since you ascended the throne." And, after a pause: "Do not be afraid. The boy from the Library remembers a great deal for you. More than you think."

He left, tapping with his staff.

And I sat by the window until morning, holding on my knees the papyrus that had lain for three hundred years among the refuse, waiting for its appointed time. And when the first ray of the sun fell on its edge, it seemed to me - for only an instant - that the flowing, incomprehensible signs on it had come together into a word. One single word.

But the sun rose higher - and the word melted away like breath on glass.
