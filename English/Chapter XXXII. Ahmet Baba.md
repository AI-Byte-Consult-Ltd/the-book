Chapter XXXII
Ahmet Baba

I left Suleyman's house before dawn, alone, as the novice boy at the gate had instructed.

The courtyard slept. The pigeons on the roof slept. Even Orhan, who for the past few nights had slept across my threshold wrapped in my own woollen blanket, was asleep, and I stepped over him as quietly as I could. He did not wake. He only muttered something in his sleep and clenched the hilt of his knife tighter.

Kemal was waiting for me at the gate.

Barefoot, in his nightshirt, with charcoal behind his ear. He stood leaning against the post and looked at me with serious, un-childlike eyes. I was not surprised. I seemed to have stopped being surprised that Kemal always knows where he needs to stand.

"I must go alone," I said. "The sheikh said so."

Kemal nodded. He understood. Then he stepped up to me and checked my pockets, one by one, the way equipment is checked before a journey. The blank sheet: here. The note in Persian: here. The drawing with two doors: here. The coins: here, three of mine and two warm ones, the widow's and Kyra Theodora's. The pouch of stones at my belt: here. The red thread with the half nazar on my wrist: here. Hodja Nureddin's pouch of salt and bread: here. And the white spiral shell that he himself had pulled from the water at the walled-up sea gates: here.

He nodded. Everything was in place.

Then he stepped back and made a sign with his hand: go.

I went. At the bend I looked round. Kemal stood by the gate, small, in his white shirt, in the grey of the pre-dawn, and watched me go. He did not wave. He simply watched. As one watches someone who will return, or will not, and you do not know which of the two.

* * *

At that hour the city belonged to the cats, the dogs and those few people whose work begins before the sun.

I met the water-seller, the same wiry old man with a copper jug on his back and polished cups at his belt who on my first day had called out on the square: "Su! Buz gibi su!" He recognised me. He stopped. Poured water into a cup, not asking for money, and held it out.

"Misafir," he said. And something more.

I drank. The water was cold, faintly sweet. Like the fountain on the square that first morning.

I met the baker, opening the shutters of his shop. The shop smelled of warm bread, and on the wooden shelves behind him, golden, sprinkled with sesame, lay rings of simit. He nodded to me, as one nods to an acquaintance, and tossed me one. I caught it.

I met Hasan.

That was altogether astonishing; Hasan Amca never rose before noon, the whole quarter knew it. But there he stood at the door of his spice shop in a robe over his nightshirt, his red beard dishevelled, yawning so wide that all his teeth showed, and, seeing me, he waved.

"Didn't sleep," he said in his broken tongue, which I now understood almost entirely. "All night. Thinking." He scratched his beard. "Ahmet Baba. Twenty years ago. Saffron. Remember, I told you?"

"I remember."

"I didn't tell you everything," said Hasan. His voice was unusually quiet. "When I left him, then, twenty years ago, he said one more thing to me. I didn't understand then. Thought it was old man's chatter." Hasan paused. "He said: 'In twenty years a guest without a name will come to you. Feed him. And then bring him to me. Or don't bring him; he will find the way himself. But feed him without fail.'"

I looked at him. At the red beard, the sleepy face, the bags under his eyes.

"I fed you," said Hasan. "That first morning. With figs and bread. Remember? You stood at my shop and looked at me like a child at a miracle." He suddenly laughed, quietly, hoarsely. "I didn't even remember then what he had said. Remembered only yesterday. When you said at the gate, 'where my home is.'"

He thrust something small into my hand, wrapped in paper. I unfolded it. A pinch of saffron, dark red threads that smelled of honey and sun.

"Give it to him," said Hasan. "From me. Tell him: the very one. From the brother-in-law. Twenty years it lay." And he gave me a push in the back. "Go. The sun is coming."

* * *

Hagia Sophia in the pre-dawn murk was not dark red but grey, almost black, an enormous shadow against the lightening sky. The minarets at its corners stood like sentinels. The dome drowned in the mist rising from the sea.

I walked round it, past the old cemetery where, between the cypresses, leaning gravestones with stone turbans stood, and came out at a gate. Once green, now peeled to grey. With a copper plaque above it. "Patience."

The gate was open.

And on the threshold sat a cat. Three-coloured, fluffy, with green eyes. The very one. She looked at me. Then stood up, stretched, slowly, with relish, arching her back, and walked inside without looking round. Tail high.

I followed her.

* * *

Ahmet Baba's lodge turned out to be small, smaller than I had expected. A courtyard paved with old flagstones worn to a shine. A well in the middle, with a wooden windlass and a copper bucket. A fig tree, huge, ancient, gnarled, spreading its branches over the whole courtyard like a tent. Beneath the fig tree, a low bench. Along the walls, cells, small, with wooden doors. And at the back, a hall. Round. Domed. With a wooden floor polished to a mirror shine.

From the hall came music.

A flute. A single one. Reed; I recognised the sound though I had never heard it. Long-drawn, hoarse, sorrowful, like a human sigh stretched out over an eternity. It rose and fell, trembled, died away, and rose again. As though someone very far away, on the other shore, were calling someone by a name he could not remember.

"Listen to the reed, how it complains," red-bearded Hasan had once told me, reading Rumi. "Ever since I was cut from the reed-bed..."

The cat entered the hall. I followed.

* * *

In the middle of the round hall, on the wooden floor, a man was whirling.

Tall, thin, in a long white gown to the ankles with a wide skirt that, as he turned, opened around him like a bell, like the petals of an enormous flower. On his head, a tall felt hat the colour of honey, like a gravestone. His arms were outstretched: the right palm up, to the sky, the left palm down, to the earth. His head inclined slightly toward the right shoulder. His eyes closed.

He whirled. Slowly. Evenly. Unstoppably. On the spot, on the left foot, pushing off with the right, circle after circle, circle after circle, and the skirt of his gown flew round him in a white ring, never touching the floor. And his face was calm. Not concentrated, not strained, but calm, like the face of a sleeping child.

By the wall, on a cushion, sat the flute-player. An old man, very old, grey, with a white beard to his chest, with closed eyes. Blind, I realised, by the way he held his head, tilted back a little, as though listening not to his own flute but to something beyond it. He played, and his fingers, dry as reeds, ran over the holes of the flute, and the sound poured out.

Beside the flute-player, cross-legged, sat the novice boy. The same one. Shaven-headed, in a felt cap. Seeing me, he put a finger to his lips and pointed to a place on the floor by the wall.

I sat.

The cat lay down on my knees. As though she had always lain there.

* * *

He whirled for a long time. I do not know how long. The sun beyond the high windows under the dome had time to rise, first rosy, then golden, and its rays, entering the hall, fell on the whirling white figure, and it seemed that he whirled inside the light, like a mote of dust.

I watched, and it seemed to me that I was whirling with him. Not with my body, with something else. As then, in that dream of the Monolith, among the stars, when I was a point and worlds revolved around me on the branches of the tree. As then, with the old men on the carpet, when my hand of its own accord made the first five moves at backgammon. As then, by the column in Hagia Sophia, when my finger traced a full circle in the wet stone.

A circle. Everything is a circle.

And then the flute fell silent. And he stopped.

At once. Without slowing. As though someone had stopped a top with a single motion. The skirt of his gown subsided. His arms dropped and crossed on his chest, right on the left shoulder, left on the right. He bowed to someone invisible, in the centre of the hall. Then he opened his eyes.

And looked at me.

* * *

His eyes were blue.

That was the first thing that struck me. Not brown, not black like everyone's here, but blue, light, almost transparent, like water in a mountain stream. Like the Bulgarian gardener's, I thought, and did not understand where the thought came from. I knew no Bulgarian gardener.

He was not young, perhaps seventy, but he held himself straight, lightly, like a youth. His face was lean, sunburnt, with a short white beard, with a net of fine wrinkles at the eyes such as people have who have laughed much in the sun. And a smile, light, mocking, kind.

"You came from the sea," he said. In my language. Cleanly, almost without accent, a little old-fashioned. "You brought a blank sheet. You did not give your name. You asked where your home is, and knew the answer." He tilted his head, studying me. "And also you made no wish at the weeping column. The kayyim told me."

"You speak my language," I said.

"I speak many languages," said Ahmet Baba. "I studied in Cairo, in Baghdad, in Bukhara. Three years I lived among the Franks, in Paris, in Vienna. Hodja Nureddin thinks it spoiled me." He smiled. "Perhaps he is right. He is often right. That is the most unpleasant thing about him."

He came over and sat down opposite me on the floor, cross-legged. The cat on my knees raised her head, looked at him, mewed, and lay down again.

"Mismis has accepted you," said Ahmet Baba. "That is good. She accepts few. Hodja Nureddin, for instance, never. He comes to me once a week to argue about faith, and each time she sits on his turban." He paused. "He says it is a sign from Shaitan. I say it is a sign from the cat."

I laughed. I could not help it. Ahmet Baba laughed with me.

"Good," he said. "You know how to laugh. Those who cannot, I do not receive. It is dull with them." He clapped his hands. "Yunus! Tea!"

The novice boy leapt up and ran off.

* * *

The tea came in small glass cups, strong, dark red, sweet, with clove. Yunus brought it on a copper tray together with flatbread, honey and dried apricots, and sat by the wall, next to the blind flute-player, who had not once opened his eyes.

"This is Shemseddin," said Ahmet Baba, nodding at the old man. "He has played the ney, the reed flute, for sixty years. He went blind when he was twenty. He says it is the best thing that ever happened to him. Because since then he hears what the sighted do not see."

The old man, without opening his eyes, smiled faintly.

"Ask him what he hears," said Ahmet Baba.

I looked at the flute-player.

"What do you hear, honoured Shemseddin?"

The old man was silent for a long time. Then he spoke, slowly, in Turkish, and Ahmet Baba translated.

"He says: 'I hear that you have two hearts. One beats here. The other, far away. Very far. And they do not beat in time.'" Ahmet Baba paused. "He says: 'When I play, I always look for which heart to play in time with. With your two I could not. I played into the emptiness between them.'"

I did not know what to answer.

"Do not answer," said Ahmet Baba. "Shemseddin does not wait for answers. He waits for the hearts to beat in time." He sipped his tea. "I wait too. But I am not so patient."

* * *

I took Hasan's paper from my pocket. Unfolded it. Held it out to the sheikh.

"This is for you. From Hasan Amca. He said: the very saffron. From the brother-in-law. Twenty years it lay."

Ahmet Baba took the paper. Looked at the dark red threads. And laughed, long, heartily, with his head thrown back, so that tears came to his blue eyes.

"Hasan!" he said when he had finished laughing. "Old fox! Twenty years he kept it!" He sniffed the saffron. "Do you know what I told him then? 'You are looking not for saffron but for a reason to quarrel with your brother-in-law.' And for twenty years he did not quarrel. I know. The brother-in-law came to me. To thank me." Ahmet Baba wiped his eyes. "And now he has sent me the saffron. So, he has made peace for good. So, he no longer needs the reason." He folded the paper. "Good news you bring, guest without a name."

"Hasan said," I began slowly, "that twenty years ago you told him: in twenty years a guest without a name will come. Feed him."

Ahmet Baba stopped smiling. Looked at me.

"I did," he nodded.

"How did you know?"

The sheikh was silent for a long time. By the wall Yunus stopped chewing his flatbread. Shemseddin did not stir.

"Would you like me to tell you a story?" said Ahmet Baba instead of answering. "An old one. They tell it in Baghdad. Or perhaps in Cairo. It depends where it is told."

* * *

"'There lived in Baghdad a man,'" the sheikh began, and his voice grew lower, slower, like a storyteller's in the marketplace. "'He was rich, and then he was ruined. He lost everything. House, money, friends. He was left alone, in an empty house, with empty hands. And one night he had a dream. As if someone said to him: "Your fortune is in Cairo. Go there."'"

Ahmet Baba sipped his tea.

"'And the man went. On foot. Across the desert. A month he walked. Two. Starved. Froze. He came to Cairo, a beggar, in rags, without a penny. At night he fell asleep in a mosque, because there was nowhere else. And that night thieves were caught in that mosque. And the guards seized him along with them. And beat him. And brought him to the captain of the guard. And the captain asked: "Who are you? Where from?" The man said: "I am from Baghdad." "Why did you come to Cairo?" "I dreamed that here was my fortune."'"

The sheikh paused.

"'And the captain of the guard burst out laughing. So hard that he fell off his cushions. And said: "Fool! You walked two months across the desert because of a dream? I myself have dreamed three times that in Baghdad, on such-and-such a street, in such-and-such a house, under the fountain in the courtyard, a treasure is buried. So what? Do I go to Baghdad? Dreams are dreams! Go home, simpleton." And let him go.'"

Ahmet Baba looked at me.

"'And the man,'" he said quietly, "'listened to the address. The street. The house. The fountain. And recognised it. His own. His own house. In Baghdad. Which he had abandoned to go seeking fortune in Cairo.'" The sheikh paused. "'He returned home. Another two months across the desert. Dug up the earth under the fountain in his own courtyard. And found the treasure.'"

The hall was quiet. The sun lay on the floor in golden patches.

"Hodja Nureddin tells the same thing about Nasreddin," I said. "The key under the lamp-post. He lost it at home, looks for it in the street, because it is lighter there."

"Nureddin tells it from one side," said Ahmet Baba. "I, from the other." He smiled. "Nasreddin does not go home. He crawls under the lamp-post. That is why it is funny. But the man from Baghdad went to Cairo. Two months. Across the desert. He was beaten. He starved." The sheikh paused. "And only there, at the edge, from a stranger, the captain of the guard, did he learn where his home is."

"So he had to go?"

"So, otherwise he would not have known," said Ahmet Baba. "The treasure lay beneath his feet all his life. But to find it, he had to go so far that some stranger would tell him the address of his own house." He paused. "You asked how I knew you would come."

"I did."

"I too had a dream," said Ahmet Baba. "Twenty years ago. Many times. That in Istanbul, in this lodge, by this fig tree, I would meet a man without a name, with two stones at his belt." He looked at me with his blue, light eyes. "I, unlike the captain of the Cairo guard, did not laugh. I waited."

* * *

"Show them to me," said Ahmet Baba.

I untied the pouch. Poured the stones onto my palm. Black and white.

The sheikh did not take them. He looked. For a long time. Mismis the cat rose on my knees, sniffed the stones, sneezed, and went off to Shemseddin.

"You carry them," Ahmet Baba said at last, "and you think they are a key. To a door. To a chest. To your memory." He raised his eyes to me. "They are not a key."

"Then what?"

"Imagine that you walk along a rope," said Ahmet Baba. "Over a chasm. Like the rope-walkers at the fair. What does the rope-walker hold in his hands?"

"A pole."

"A pole," the sheikh nodded. "Long. Heavy. With two ends. Why?"

"So as not to fall. For balance."

"For balance," Ahmet Baba repeated. "One end pulls left, the other right. Remove one end and the rope-walker falls. Remove both and he falls. He stays up while he holds both." He pointed to my palm. "That is what you have in your hand, guest. Not a key. A pole. You walk a rope between two chasms. And you stay up while you hold both."

"Between which chasms?"

Ahmet Baba smiled.

"That you tell me," he said. "You know the answer. You always know the answer. You simply do not remember that you know."

I looked at the stones. Black. White. One keeps, the other leads. Who holds both stands in the doorway.

"Past and future?" I ventured.

The sheikh shook his head.

"Too easy. That is the answer for a fool."

"Life and death?"

"That is the answer for a clever man." Ahmet Baba leaned toward me. "And the true one?"

I was silent. A long time. The sun shifted on the floor. The flute-player Shemseddin, without opening his eyes, raised the flute to his lips and breathed out one single note, long, trembling.

"To remember and to forget," I said.

Ahmet Baba leaned back. Looked at me for a long time.

"Well, then," he said softly. "Now you have said it." He paused. "In sixty years Hasan Amca has not met a man who solved the true answer to a dervish's riddle. Tell him: he has met one. Today."

* * *

"May I ask something?" I said.

"You have already asked where your home is," said Ahmet Baba. "At the gate. Yunus passed it to me. You answered: where you look. And I had it conveyed to you: now you are looking back."

"What does that mean?"

The sheikh rose from the floor, lightly, in one movement, as youths rise. Went to the tall window under the dome. Stood looking out, to where the sun was rising above the roofs.

"You are looking for your name," he said without turning. "You are looking for where you came from. For who you were. For who wrote you the note. Who gave you the stones. You look for the past." He paused. "You look back, guest. Entirely. Completely. You walk the rope, and you look over your shoulder. At the end from which you came."

"Where should I look?"

Ahmet Baba turned.

"At the end to which you are going," he said simply. "A rope-walker who looks back falls. Always." He paused. "Your home is where you look. You said so yourself. So if you look back, your home is behind you. In the past. Which you do not remember." The sheikh smiled. "But if you look forward..."

"Then home is ahead?"

"Then home is where you have not yet arrived," said Ahmet Baba. "Like the Red Apple among the Turks. Like the City of the Caesars among the Franks in the New World." He paused, and something flickered in his blue eyes. "Like the Marble King among the Greeks. Who has not returned, but waits. Ahead. Beneath the gate."

I started.

"How do you know about the Marble King?"

"Kyra Theodora is an old acquaintance of mine," said the sheikh. "We have corresponded for forty years. She writes to me in French, I answer her in Greek. So that no one can read it." He smiled. "She wrote to me three days ago. That a guest had come in a red turban, solved the riddle of the fountain and taken the coin of the Melissenoi." Ahmet Baba paused. "She writes that for the first time in ninety years she is at peace."

* * *

"The grey man," I said. "He was in the gallery of Hagia Sophia. He told me: do not go to the dervish. He came to Kyra Theodora. He looked for me at the port. Who is he?"

Ahmet Baba was silent for a long time. By the wall Yunus shrank into himself. The cat Mismis raised her head.

"Which one?" the sheikh asked at last.

"What do you mean, which?"

"There are two of them, guest," said Ahmet Baba. Quietly, seriously, without a smile. "You confuse them. Everyone confuses them. I confused them too, until I saw both."

He sat down again on the floor. Opposite me. Close.

"One is a man," he said. "Of flesh and blood. Tall, in a grey frock coat, with a cane, a top hat. He speaks French, Greek, Turkish. He smells of cologne, expensive, Parisian, with bergamot. He drinks tea. He pays in gold. He signs his name in registers. He has a face, but no one can remember it. Because he wishes it so. That is an art. It is taught." The sheikh paused. "He came to me. Five years ago. Asked about guests without a name. Offered money for my notes. I turned him out. Mismis sat on his top hat."

"Who is he?"

"I do not know," said Ahmet Baba. "But he is not alone. There are many such as he. They seek the same thing as you. Only from the other end. Did he tell you so?" I nodded. "He told the truth. They seek. Not you, but what you have. What you carry." The sheikh looked at the pouch at my belt. "And what you may one day remember."

"And the other?"

Ahmet Baba did not answer for a long time.

"The other," he said at last, very quietly, "is seen by children. And cats. And the blind. And old women who spit over their shoulders. He smells of nothing. He does not drink tea. He has no face, not because he hides it, but because..." The sheikh fell silent. "I saw him once. Twenty years ago. The night I first dreamed of you. He stood there." Ahmet Baba nodded toward the centre of the round hall, where he had been whirling. "On that very spot. And whirled. With me. I closed my eyes and felt him whirling beside me. I opened them, and no one."

"Who is he?"

Ahmet Baba looked at me with his blue, light, infinitely weary eyes.

"I have thought of it for twenty years, guest," he said. "I have read all the books in Cairo, in Baghdad, in Bukhara. I have asked the Sufis and the Frankish professors." He paused. "I do not know. But I will tell you one thing. The first, the man in the frock coat, you should fear. He is dangerous, because he wants. And the second..." The sheikh fell silent.

"And the second?"

"The second, do not fear," said Ahmet Baba. "The second wants nothing. He simply waits." He paused. "As the angel beneath the dome of Hagia Sophia waits for the boy who was not returned."

* * *

Then it was noon, and Yunus brought lunch, lentil soup, flatbread, olives, and we ate in the courtyard under the fig tree, the four of us: the sheikh, I, the boy and the blind flute-player. Mismis lay on the bench pretending to sleep, but followed every olive.

"Yunus," said Ahmet Baba, "give the guest a riddle. Your own. The one you made up last week."

The boy blushed to his ears. Laid down his flatbread. Looked at me, gathering his courage.

"'I whirl,'" he said in Turkish, and the sheikh translated, "'but I do not tire. I sing, but I have no mouth. I am empty inside, therefore I sound. If I am filled, I fall silent. What am I?'"

I thought.

"A flute," I said. "A reed one. Empty inside, therefore it sounds."

Yunus beamed. Shemseddin, without opening his eyes, laughed, quietly, soundlessly, with his shoulders alone.

"And why 'I whirl'?" I asked.

"Because the dervish whirls as the flute sounds," said Ahmet Baba. "He is empty inside. So that it may pass through him." He looked at me. "You too are empty inside, guest. Without a name. Without memory. You think it is a misfortune." He paused. "But perhaps it is so that something may sound through you."

"What?"

"That I do not know," said the sheikh. "Perhaps you will find out yourself. When you stop trying to fill yourself." He broke off a piece of flatbread. "Everyone tries to fill the emptiness. With names. With the past. With things. With fear. And the flute sounds only while it is empty."

Shemseddin suddenly opened his eyes. White, blind, without pupils. Turned his face to me. And said something, hoarsely, not loudly.

"He says," Ahmet Baba translated slowly, "'Your two hearts. Just now. For a moment one fell into time with the other. When you said "what?" I heard it.'"

* * *

Toward evening, when the sun was already sinking toward the dome of Hagia Sophia, Ahmet Baba called me to his cell.

Small, whitewashed, with one window, a mat on the floor, a low table heaped with books in a dozen languages. Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Greek, French, German, Latin. And among them one notebook. Worn. In a leather binding.

"Forty years," said the sheikh, laying his palm on the notebook, "I have written down in here all that I hear of people without a name. Of guests. Of those who come and go." He paused. "I have forty-two entries. Constantinople. Cairo. Bukhara. Damascus. Jerusalem." He looked at me. "And I know that I am not the only one."

"Not the only one?"

"There are people," said Ahmet Baba, lowering his voice, "who write this down not for forty years. But for four hundred. Or more. From generation to generation. They pass on the notebooks as one passes on an inheritance. They call themselves..." He fell silent. "No. Let them tell you themselves what they call themselves."

"Where can I find them?"

The sheikh took from beneath the notebook a folded letter, sealed with green wax. Held it out to me.

"In the palace," he said. "The old one. On the cape. Where you passed the walls yesterday. In the archive. There serves a man. The keeper of papers. His name is Niyaz Efendi. He is old. Older than I. He receives no one." Ahmet Baba paused. "But this letter he will read. And then he will receive you."

I took the letter. On the green wax a seal was impressed, not letters, not a coat of arms. Two small circles. One filled. The other empty. And between them a wavy line.

I raised my eyes to the sheikh.

"You are one of them."

Ahmet Baba smiled.

"I am a dervish, guest," he said. "I whirl and drink tea." He paused. "But this seal was given to me by Niyaz Efendi. Forty years ago. He said: 'When he comes, seal the letter with this seal. I will understand.'"

* * *

"And one last thing," said Ahmet Baba, when we came out of the cell back into the courtyard, under the fig tree, where an oil lamp was already burning and the first stars were lighting above the dome of Hagia Sophia. "I want to offer you something."

"What?"

He looked at me with his blue, light eyes, and this time there was neither mockery in them nor weariness. Only warmth.

"Stay," he said. "Here. With me. Be my pupil." He paused. "I will teach you to whirl. I will teach you to be empty, so that something sounds through you. You will not have to go to Niyaz Efendi. You will not have to look for your name. You will not have to fear the man in the frock coat and wait for the second, the one without a face." He spread his hands. "You will live here. Under the fig tree. Drink tea. Listen to Shemseddin's flute. Feed Mismis olives. And never again walk in a circle." The sheikh paused. "For the dervish does not walk in a circle. The dervish whirls on the spot. They are different things."

I stood under the fig tree. The lamp sputtered. Somewhere in the hall Shemseddin was softly playing his flute. Yunus sat on the rim of the well, swinging his legs, and looked at me with hope.

And it was tempting. So tempting as nothing had been in all these long, tangled days. To stay. To stop searching. To stop looking back. Simply to be. Here. Under the fig tree. With a blue-eyed old man and a blind flute-player, and a boy, and a cat who opens doors.

"If Ahmet Baba offers you to stay, do not stay," Hodja Nureddin had said.

I slowly reached into my pocket. Took out the canvas pouch tied with a red thread. Untied it.

Inside were a crumb of bread and a pinch of salt.

I ate the bread. Then took the salt and threw it over my left shoulder.

And bowed.

"Thank you, Ahmet Baba," I said. "But I must go."

* * *

The sheikh looked at me. At the salt whitening on the flagstones of the courtyard behind my back. At the empty canvas pouch in my hand.

And then he laughed.

As he had not laughed all that long day, loudly, heartily, with his head thrown back, holding his belly, so that Yunus on the well burst out laughing with him, not understanding why, and Mismis leapt off the bench and ran into the hall.

"Nureddin!" Ahmet Baba gasped, wiping away tears. "Old fox! Forty years! Forty years we have argued, and he always knows what I will do!" He laughed again. "Bread and salt! He gave you bread and salt!"

"He said," I began, "to tell you. That he is right. That you will understand."

Ahmet Baba stopped laughing. Looked at me. For a long time.

"I understand," he said quietly. "Tell him he is right. As always." The sheikh paused. "Forty years ago I offered another guest to stay. Without a name. With two stones. He stayed. For a year." Ahmet Baba closed his eyes. "And then he left. At night. Without saying goodbye. And for forty years I have thought: if he had not stayed that year, perhaps he would have got there. Where he was going." He opened his eyes. "Nureddin told me then: 'You stole a year from him, Ahmet. Do not do it again.'" The sheikh smiled. "I promised. And here, I did not keep my word. Good that you had bread and salt."

"Did you want to test me?"

"I wanted to keep you," said Ahmet Baba simply. "I am old. I am lonely. Shemseddin is blind, Yunus is small, and Mismis is a cat. I wanted someone beside me with whom I could talk of Rumi and of the stars." He paused. "But Nureddin is right. You cannot stay. Not with me. Not with Suleyman. Not with anyone." The sheikh laid his hand on my shoulder. "You are a rope-walker, guest. A rope-walker cannot stop in the middle of the rope. He can only get across. Or fall."

* * *

Yunus saw me to the gate. Mismis too. She went ahead, tail high, and at the gate raised a paw and pushed the leaf, and it opened.

The sun had already set. Above the dome of Hagia Sophia stars were burning. From the minarets the evening call floated.

"Yunus," I said to the boy at the gate. "May I ask something?"

"Ask," said the boy. In my language. Brokenly, carefully, apparently learned from the sheikh.

"Your riddle. About the flute. Did you really make it up yourself?"

Yunus looked at me for a long time. Then shook his head.

"No," he said. "I dreamed it. A week ago. A tall man. Grey. He stood in the middle of the hall. Where the sheikh whirls." The boy swallowed. "He told me the riddle. And he said: 'Give it to the guest. He will solve it. And then he will ask whether you made it up yourself. Tell him the truth.'"

I stood at the gate. The stars burned. The cat looked at me with green eyes.

"What else did he say?" I asked. My voice sounded as if from far away.

Yunus thought.

"He said: 'Tell the guest: the flute sounds while it is empty. But one day it will be filled. And then it will not fall silent. It will sing its own.'"

He bowed. The cat mewed. The gate closed.

I went down toward Suleyman's, in the dark, along the narrow lanes of Sultanahmet, with a letter sealed in green wax in my pocket. With two circles and a wavy line on the seal. And with the feeling that behind my back, somewhere high, on the dome of Hagia Sophia, under the stars, someone stands. And whirls. Slowly. Evenly. With arms outstretched: one to the sky, the other to the earth.

Waiting.
