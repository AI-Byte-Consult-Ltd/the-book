Chapter XXVII
The Scroll in the Silk Kerchief

The cutting on the windowsill had put forth a second leaf.

I noticed it in the morning, drawing back the curtains: yesterday there had been three leaves and one tight, dark, clenched bud, and today there were four. The fourth was quite tiny, reddish-bronze, rolled into a tube like an infant's fist. The bud had not opened. But it seemed darker. And when I bent toward it, it seemed to me that it smelled - faintly, as a letter smells that has lain long in someone else's pocket - of honey and dust. The very smell of the General's garden.

I watered it from the jug. Drop by drop. As yesterday.

Stefan's note - "Let it grow with you. The rose will find. - S." - I had put into the blue notebook, with the other papers, of which there were more and more and which I carried about with me as one carries an amulet. The paper from the monastery near Tarnovo. The drawing of two circles which I had not drawn. The words "He is near. Wait," which I had not written.

And someone was already knocking at the door.

* * *

"Young master," said Agafya Tikhonovna as soon as I opened. "Dress. Come with me."

She stood on the threshold - small, dry, in a dark dress and white cap, with a bunch of keys at her belt that jingled with every movement like the bells of a troika. Her face was such as I had never seen it in all those years: not stern, not grumbling - bewildered.

"What has happened, Agafya Tikhonovna?"

"The crape," she said. "Again." And, after a pause, added more quietly: "Only this time, my dear sir, no one tore it down. I sat at the gallery myself in the night. On a chair. With a lamp. I did not sleep till the cocks crowed. No one passed. Not a living soul." She swallowed. "And toward morning it fell. By itself. As if someone had pushed it from within."

We walked along the corridors in silence. She ahead, I behind. The keys jingled. Portraits of ancestors looked down from the walls - ladies in powdered wigs, men in armour and uniforms, the stern great-great-grandfather whose pupils, as I had once fancied, had shifted to follow my movement.

At the entrance to the far end of the gallery stood a chair. On it - an extinguished lamp, an unfinished stocking on needles and a mug of cold tea. Agafya Tikhonovna had indeed sat here all night.

And farther on, at the very end of the gallery, on the floor, lay the crape. Black, dusty, fallen in a shapeless heap.

And the portrait looked at us.

* * *

I had already seen it - on that night, before the railings. A young man in a dark frock coat of old-fashioned cut, in a light neckcloth tied in an intricate knot - the same as I tied mine. Hands folded on his knees. In his palm - two small objects: a black one and a white one. And the face - a dark patch of cracked paint, indistinguishable, as though the canvas had been held long over a fire.

Now, in the daylight falling from the high window, I saw more.

The paint on the face had cracked into a fine net - that was true. But through the net of cracks, if one looked from the side, at an angle, features showed. Not as a whole. Separate ones. The line of a cheekbone. The curve of a brow. The corner of a mouth.

I looked, and grew colder and colder.

"Agafya Tikhonovna," I said. "Who is this?"

The old woman did not answer for a long time. She stood beside me, gripping the bunch of keys so that her knuckles whitened.

"It is forbidden, my dear sir," she said at last. "Fifty years forbidden."

"By whom?"

"By the old mistress. Not ours - the one before ours. Artemia Aristarkhovna's mother-in-law." Agafya Tikhonovna crossed herself. "I came to this house a girl, I was twelve. This portrait was already hanging under crape then. The old mistress brought me here on the very first day. Showed me. And said: 'Agafya, you will straighten this crape every morning. Until your death. And if it ever falls of itself - run to me.'" The old woman paused. "I straightened it. Fifty years. Every morning."

"And did it fall?"

"Twice," whispered Agafya Tikhonovna. "The first time - forty years ago. On that night when the key to the railings vanished. I ran to the old mistress, as I had been told. She came, looked, wept and said: 'So he has gone again.'" The old woman raised faded eyes to me. "And the second time - today."

"Who is 'he'? Who has gone?"

Agafya Tikhonovna bent, picked up the crape from the floor, shook the dust from it. And before hanging it back on the frame, for a moment she paused - looked at the portrait, then at me, then at the portrait again.

"The old mistress called him 'our guest,'" she said. "Nothing more she said. 'Our guest.'" She draped the crape over the frame, smoothing the folds with trembling hands. "And on the plaque there was a name. I remember it. Only the plaque was scraped off before my time. And the name I will not say, my dear sir. It is forbidden. The mistress took an oath of me. On the icon."

She turned and went away along the gallery, jingling her keys. At the very turning she stopped, not looking round.

"Pavel Nikolaevich will arrive today," she said. "With the professor. The mistress ordered a table laid in the study." She paused. "And ordered that you be there. For the first time in fifty years she has ordered that someone be present when they speak of this in the study."

* * *

The General arrived by dinner - in a carriage drawn by a pair of black horses, with Tikhon on the box and Sventsitsky beside him, who held on his knees his canvas bag of notebooks as though he were carrying eggs in it.

Konstantin met them on the porch - he seemed to have been on watch since morning. Seeing the professor, he bowed with exaggerated courtesy.

"Professor! So you have lingered in our parts after all? You said - till the end of the week."

"I have lingered, lieutenant," Sventsitsky said cheerfully, jumping down from the step. "Your General abducted me. Came yesterday to the inn, said he had one thing I must see - and that if I refused, he would order his coachman to take me bound." He winked at Tikhon. "Your coachman, by the way, said he would bind me tenderly. I was touched and agreed."

Tikhon on the box chuckled into his beard.

The General greeted everyone briefly, drily, as always. But I noticed that under his arm was a parcel. Small, oblong, wrapped in a faded silk kerchief - once, perhaps, blue, and now pale blue like the sky over a desert. He held it as one holds something very fragile and very heavy at once.

* * *

The study of my aunt's late husband - the very one where the General slept when he stayed, and where I had overheard a conversation not meant for my ears - was prepared as for a council. Curtains drawn. On the writing desk - six candles in two candelabra, though it was day. Armchairs arranged in a semicircle. By the fireplace, where a small fire crackled - coffee, brandy, Marfa's almond biscuits.

There were six of us. My aunt - in her chair by the fireplace, straight, pale, in dark lilac, with pearls, gripping a lace handkerchief in her hands. The General - at the desk. Sventsitsky - beside him, already spreading out his notebooks. Doctor Strauch - in the corner, with an unlit cigar, with his black little book on his knee. Konstantin - by the door, like a sentry. And I - in the armchair opposite my aunt, so that between us there was only the fire.

The General laid the parcel on the desk. Stood over it. Then turned to my aunt.

"Artemia Aristarkhovna," he said quietly. "Are you sure?"

My aunt looked long at the parcel. Then - at me.

"No, Pavel Nikolaevich," she said. "I am not sure." She paused. "But the crape fell by itself. Tonight. Agafya sat until the cocks crowed."

The General slowly nodded - as though this were precisely what he had wanted to hear. Or feared.

"Then," he said, "the time has come."

And untied the kerchief.

* * *

Inside was a scroll. Not of paper - of leather. Old, dark, almost black leather, dressed so thin that it showed through in the candlelight, rolled into a tube two palms long and tied with a faded cord of goat's wool. It smelled of dust, sand and something else - dry, bitter, steppe-like, as wormwood smells in August.

"Khiva," said the General, addressing no one, looking at the scroll. "Thirty years ago. We were going through the sands to Khiva. A detachment - a hundred and forty men. The guide fled on the second day. The water ran out on the third." He paused. "Mikhail has probably told you. How I went to the Turkmen."

"He has, Your Excellency," I said quietly. "The Colonel said you returned at dawn with a guide and with water-skins. And with a scroll."

"With a scroll." The General touched the cord with the tips of his fingers. "I did not know their language. Not a word. I came into their camp alone, unarmed, with raised hands - and expected to be cut down. I was led to an old man. Very old. Blind in one eye. He sat by a fire and told his beads. He looked at me with his one eye - for a long time. Then said something to his people. They brought water. A great deal of water. And a guide." The General paused. "And then the old man took from under his robe this. And said - I understood him, I do not know how, as one understands in dreams - 'This scroll waits for a man. For long. You are not that man. But you will meet him. Give it to him. And say: the earth remembers.'"

The earth remembers. The old aborigine in Australia: "Tell him that the earth remembers his song." The old Turkmen near Khiva: "Say: the earth remembers."

"Thirty years," said the General, "I have carried it with me. On all campaigns. On all ships. Across all oceans." He raised his eyes to me. "I thought - perhaps I shall meet him. In Samarkand. In Peking. In Cuba. In Siam. I looked at faces. All these years - I looked at faces."

"And did you meet him?" asked Sventsitsky.

The General did not answer. He was looking at me.

Then he untied the cord.

* * *

The scroll unrolled on the desk by itself - slowly, with an old, leathery rustle, as though it had grown tired in thirty years of being rolled.

It was written upon. All of it, from edge to edge. Not in letters - no, not in letters. In long, flowing, curving lines running one into another without breaks, without spaces, without beginning or end. Dark brown, almost black - not ink, I understood, but something else, something that had eaten so deep into the leather that it had become part of it.

In the study it grew so quiet that one could hear the candles gutter.

I looked at the scroll - and could not tear my eyes away. I did not know these signs. I had never seen them. And yet, looking at them, I felt what I had felt in Stefan's greenhouse when he unfolded before me the five-hundred-year-old monastery paper. As though a key were entering a lock I had never seen.

"Professor," said the General.

Sventsitsky was already leafing through his notebook - the thick one, in an oilcloth cover, in which he had written down all I had told him on that day I did not remember. His hands, usually so sure, visibly trembled. He found the page. Laid the notebook on the desk, beside the scroll.

"Here," he said dully. "Here is what he drew me then. At the inn. Himself. With my pencil. I asked: 'Show me what the signs on your note look like.' And he wrote a line. One. Without thinking. As though he had been writing all his life."

On the page of the notebook, beneath the drawing of two circles and a wavy line, in pencil, in my hand - I recognized the pressure, recognized even the habitual blot at the end, though one cannot make a blot with a pencil, but there was a thickening there, exactly the same - ran a line. Long, flowing, curving.

Sventsitsky took a candle and slowly drew it along the scroll, from line to line, checking against the notebook. We all followed the flame. A line. Another. Another.

At the seventh line from the bottom the candle stopped.

"Here," whispered Sventsitsky.

The line on the scroll. The line in the notebook.

Identical. Curve for curve. Loop for loop.

* * *

Doctor Strauch rose from his corner and came to the desk. Looked long - at the scroll, at the notebook, again at the scroll. Put on his glasses. Took them off. Put them on again. Took a magnifying glass from his pocket.

"Allow me," he said. "I assert nothing. I only observe." He bent over the scroll. "A coincidence of form. Complete. That is a fact." He straightened. "The explanations which come to my mind as a physician are the following. First: the young man once saw this scroll, memorized, forgot, and in a state of... altered consciousness reproduced it. Memory, gentlemen, keeps more than we think."

"I have never seen this scroll," I said. "The General has carried it thirty years in a locked case."

"Second," Strauch went on unperturbed, as though he had not heard. "The professor, forgive me, could have adjusted the drawing after he saw the scroll. Yesterday. At the inn. When he was 'abducted.'"

Sventsitsky turned crimson. The General raised a hand, calming.

"The professor had not seen the scroll before this minute, Karl Fyodorovich," he said. "I kept it by me. And his notebook - look at the date."

Strauch looked. Long.

"Then third," he said at last. Quietly. "For which I have no medical name."

"And do you have one, Professor?" asked my aunt. For the first time in all that while. Her voice was even, but the handkerchief in her hands was twisted into a rope.

Sventsitsky was silent a long time.

"I have, Artemia Aristarkhovna," he said. "Only it is not scientific. It was told to me by an old woman in Kargopol. An obmiranka. She said: 'There are people who write one and the same line at different ends of the earth. Not knowing each other. Not knowing the language. Because it is not they who write. It is the one who sent them who writes through them.'" He paused. "I wrote it down then and laughed. Now I do not laugh."

* * *

"Stefan's paper," I said. I do not know why I remembered it just now.

All looked at me. I took from the blue notebook the monastery paper folded in four, almost rotted at the folds - the one that Stefan had put into my hand in the greenhouse. Unfolded it. Laid it on the desk, beside the scroll and the professor's notebook.

A dark scarlet rose. Two circles, one filled, one empty. A wavy line. And a line of script beneath them.

Sventsitsky again drew the candle along. The General bent over the desk. Strauch - with his magnifying glass. My aunt rose from her chair - for the first time - and came up, leaning on the back of it.

The line on the monastery paper. Five hundred years. Tarnovo.

The line on the Turkmen scroll. Khiva.

The line in the professor's notebook. A district town. A week ago. In my hand.

One and the same.

"Three ends of the earth," said Strauch very quietly, lowering the glass. "Three centuries. One hand." He took off his glasses and polished them a long time with the skirt of his coat, though they were clean. "Gentlemen, I am a German. I believe in what can be touched." He put the glasses on. "I have touched this."

My aunt stood at the desk, looking at the three lines. Then slowly raised her hand and touched the leather scroll with the tips of her fingers - as one touches the face of a sleeping child.

"He wrote like that too," she said. Barely audible. Addressing no one. "That one. When he stood at the railings. He wrote in the dust. With a finger. This line." She closed her eyes. "For forty years I have tried to forget it."

The General turned to her. Konstantin at the door stepped forward. But my aunt had already moved away from the desk - to the fireplace, to her chair - and sat, and was again straight and pale, and the handkerchief in her hands was again just a handkerchief.

"Continue, Pavel Nikolaevich," she said evenly. "You wished to tell. Where else you have seen such signs."

* * *

The General poured himself brandy. Drank. Poured another and did not drink - simply held the glass, looking into the fire.

"In Brazil," he said. "I told you of Rio, Artemia Aristarkhovna. Of the bay, of the Sugarloaf, of the parrots. I did not tell you of another mountain."

"Which?" asked Konstantin.

"It is called Pedra da Gávea. The Rock of the Maintop - in Portuguese, from the ship's term: the maintop is a platform on a mast, and the mountain looks like one." The General paused. "An enormous granite block above the sea, south of the city. Flat on top, like a table. And on its side, on the sheer wall - signs. Huge, each the height of a man. As though someone had carved them in the granite. The locals say - Phoenicians. That the Phoenicians sailed there a thousand years before the Portuguese and left an inscription. The Brazilian learned society even sent an expedition there. Some thirty years ago." He smiled. "The scholars said - erosion. Wind and rain. No signs."

"And you?"

"And I climbed up there," said the General. "With a mulatto guide named Joaquim. Two days through jungle, over rocks, over lianas. Joaquim crossed himself the whole way and said that only madmen and Englishmen go up that mountain." The General paused. "We climbed. I looked at the signs up close. From a rope, over the abyss."

"And?"

The General glanced at the scroll on the desk.

"Erosion," he said. "Perhaps. Wind and rain. Only wind and rain, Professor," he turned to Sventsitsky, "for some reason draw the very same loop. Here." He jabbed a finger at the scroll, at the third line from the top. "The same. I remembered it. I have remembered it thirty years since."

"And Joaquim?" asked Konstantin.

"Joaquim," said the General, and in his voice for the first time a smile flickered, "when we came down, refused to take money from me. Said: 'Señor, you are the first man who looked at that mountain as though reading a letter from a relative.' And went into the jungle. I never saw him again."

* * *

"And Argentina?" asked Sventsitsky, already writing. "You surely went on south from Brazil?"

"I did," nodded the General. "On the same American steamer of Captain Hall's. To Buenos Aires." He paused. "A strange city. It stands on a river so wide it seems a sea. The water in it is the colour of coffee with milk. Ruddy. From silt. They call it the Silver River, though there is no silver in it and never was. The Spaniards named it so because they hoped. All the Spaniards in America named things for what they hoped for."

"And the city itself?"

"Low. Flat. White one-storey houses with inner courtyards, with grilles on the windows. Broad streets - straight as by a ruler. And beyond the city - the pampa." The General set down his glass. "You have seen the steppe, Professor. Near Orenburg. Near Astrakhan."

"I have."

"The pampa is a steppe stretched to the edge of the earth and forgotten to be rolled up." The General paused. "Grass above the waist. No tree. No hill. No stone. Only grass, sky and wind. You ride a day - the same. You ride a week - the same. And suddenly on the horizon - a dot. A horseman. A gaucho."

"Gaucho?" Konstantin repeated.

"Herdsmen. Mounted. Like our Cossacks - only wilder." The General, for the first time all evening, brightened genuinely. "They live in the saddle. Eat nothing but meat - roast a whole bullock on a spit over a fire. Drink maté - tea from a gourd through a silver tube, one for all, passed round the circle. In the hand - bolas: three stones on thongs. They throw - and the thongs wind round the legs of a bull or an ostrich at a gallop." He smiled. "I tried to throw. Wound them round the legs of my own horse. The gauchos laughed so that two fell out of their saddles."

Konstantin roared. Even Strauch twitched the corner of his mouth.

"I lived two weeks with them on an estancia," the General went on. "With the old owner, Don Eusebio. And one evening by the fire I saw a payada."

"A payada?"

"A duel of singers," said the General. "Two gauchos sit facing each other with guitars. And sing in turn. Compose on the spot. Set each other riddles - in verse. Whoever does not answer has lost. Sometimes the duel lasts all night." He paused. "That evening two sang. An old, grey one, named Remigio. And a young one - dark, bold, a stranger, from other parts. They sang till dawn. Neither could win. And toward morning old Remigio set a riddle that silenced the young one."

"What riddle?" I asked.

The General closed his eyes, remembering. Then slowly pronounced - first in Spanish, in a singsong, then, after a pause, in Russian, choosing the words:

"'There is a city in the pampa that is not.
All see it, but none has reached it.
It stands where you seek it,
And leaves when you approach.
Whoever lives in it does not age.
Whoever has come out of it does not remember.
What is the name of this city?'"

The study was silent.

"The young one did not answer," said the General. "Rose. Bowed to the old man. Mounted his horse and rode away - into the pampa, toward the dawn. Everyone decided he had lost, left out of shame." The General paused. "And Don Eusebio told me that all gauchos know the answer. It is the City of the Caesars. A legend. That somewhere in the south, in Patagonia, beyond the pampa, at the foot of the mountains, there is a city. Silver. Built by survivors of a wrecked Spanish ship. Or by fugitive Incas. Or by those who came before the Incas. It has been sought for three hundred years. Hundreds of expeditions. Jesuits, soldiers, gold seekers. No one has found it. Sometimes it is seen in the distance - towers, walls, lights. You ride up - it vanishes. And the people who live there, they say, do not age. And if anyone comes out of there - he forgets where he came from."

"And the young singer?" asked Sventsitsky, ceasing to write. "Did he know the answer?"

The General opened his eyes.

"Don Eusebio said," he spoke slowly, "that this young man had come to their estancia before. Forty years earlier. Just as young. Just as dark. Sang the same payada with the same Remigio, who was then young too. And left at dawn in the same way, without answering." He paused. "Old Remigio, they say, wept after that evening. And said: 'He is from that city. He cannot name it - because he does not remember. But every time I set this riddle, he rises and goes - there.'"

* * *

"Fiji," said the General suddenly.

He said it looking into the fire, as though he had remembered only now. Or had long not wished to remember.

"Fiji?" repeated my aunt. "Where is that, Pavel Nikolaevich?"

"Far, Artemia Aristarkhovna. The farthest I have been." The General paused. "Islands in the Pacific. South of the equator. Between Australia and nothing. I came there on our corvette - we were going from Kamchatka to Sydney, and the captain decided to put in at the islands for water and provisions. Some seven years ago." He smiled. "The crew did not want to. Everyone knew that on Fiji they eat people."

Lizonka Kutasova, had she been here, would have fainted. My aunt only pursed her lips.

"Eat?" asked Konstantin with lively interest.

"They did, lieutenant." The General nodded. "Then they still did. A year before our coming they had eaten an English missionary there. Together with his boots. The boots, they say, were boiled a long time - they could not chew them." He paused. "But we were received well. We were taken in on the island of Ovalau, in the town of Levuka - white traders lived there, whalers, escaped convicts from Australia, missionaries - all the scum of the Pacific. Taverns, shops, drunken brawls. And beyond the town - mountains, jungle, waterfalls. And the natives' villages."

"What are they like?" asked Sventsitsky.

"Tall. Strong. Dark skin. Hair - like this," the General spread his hands above his head, "enormous, bushy, like a cap. Their chiefs - they are called 'ratu' - wear in their hair combs of tortoiseshell and a sperm whale's tooth at the neck. They say that whoever has more whale's teeth is more important." He paused. "I was received by one such chief. Ratu Vanua. Old, huge, with scars all over his body. He invited me to a ceremony."

"What ceremony?"

"The walking on fire," said the General.

* * *

"On the small island of Beqa," he related, "lives one tribe. Only that one, among all the islands. They walk on fire." The General paused. "I saw it. With my own eyes. They dig a pit - large, several sazhens - and fill it with stones. River stones, smooth. They build a fire on the stones. Burn it all day. The stones heat white - so that one cannot approach the pit nearer than five paces, the hairs on one's arms crackle from the heat. Then the embers are raked out with long poles. And only stones remain. White. Glowing."

He paused. In the study it was quiet, only the fireplace crackled.

"And then the men come out," said the General. "Barefoot. Their priest - an old man, whom they called Tui Kai - goes in front. They walk on the stones. Slowly. Calmly. There and back." He raised his eyes to us. "I looked at their feet afterward. Not a burn. Not a blister. Not a single mark."

"Impossible," said Strauch.

"I saw it, Karl Fyodorovich," said the General. "I do not ask you to believe. I say - I saw."

"How do they explain it?" asked Sventsitsky.

"By a legend," said the General. "Long ago, they say, an ancestor of theirs, a fisherman, caught in the river a god. A little one. The god begged to be released. And in reward gave the fisherman and all his line power over fire. Fire does not burn those whom the god has released." The General paused. "But Tui Kai, the priest, told me something else. Later, at night, by the fire. Through an interpreter - a runaway Irish convict who had lived twenty years on the islands and knew all their languages."

"What did he tell?"

The General looked into the fire a long time.

"He said that fire does not burn those who themselves came from fire. That their ancestors are not fishermen. That their ancestors came down from the sky. Together with fire. And that once - long ago, in his grandfather's time - a white man came to their island. Alone. Without a ship." The General paused. "No one saw how he arrived. In the morning he was simply sitting on the shore. Young. He did not remember who he was. And when they showed him the walking on fire - he rose and walked on the stones. First. Before the priests. Himself."

"And was not burned?" whispered Konstantin.

"Tui Kai said," the General spoke slowly, "that the stones under his feet cooled. Became cold. All of them. As though he took their heat into himself." The General paused. "And then he reached the end of the pit - and went into the jungle. To the cliff at the edge of the island, from which, by their belief, the souls of the dead leap into the sea to go to the other world. And he was not seen again."

"The cliff from which souls leap," repeated Sventsitsky.

"They say," the General nodded, "that on the western edge of the largest island there is a cape. From there the soul of the dead leaps into the sea and swims to Bulu - the land of the dead, under the water. And on the way it is met by the god Degei. A huge serpent. He lives in a cave in the mountains. When he turns in his sleep, the earth shakes." The General paused. "Tui Kai said that that white man, before leaping, stood on the cape and wrote something on the rock. With a finger. On the wet stone. And that the next morning, when the water dried, the signs remained. Burned in. Like a brand."

"Did you see that cape?" I asked.

The General looked at me. For a long time.

"I did," he said. "We put in there before sailing. The captain did not want to - there are reefs - but I persuaded him." He paused. "The signs on the rock were there. Burned in. Almost effaced by the surf in half a century. But there."

He slowly set his glass on the mantelpiece. Went to the desk. Laid a finger on the Turkmen scroll - on the fifth line from the bottom.

"These," he said.

* * *

No one spoke. The candles guttered. The fire in the fireplace was dying.

Then my aunt rose. Came to the desk. Looked at the scroll, at the monastery paper, at the professor's notebook. At the three identical lines that had come from three ends of the world. And at three more places where the General had seen them - the mountain above the sea in Brazil, the cliff above the reefs on Fiji - and at one more, about which she had been silent forty years. The dust at the railings.

"Pavel," she said. Without patronymic. For the first time in all the time I had known her - before everyone. "Give it to him."

The General looked at her. Then at me.

"The old man in the sands said," he spoke slowly, "'You are not that man. But you will meet him. Give it to him.'" He paused. "For thirty years I have looked at faces. On three continents. On five seas." He carefully, tenderly rolled up the scroll, tied it with the cord of goat's wool, wrapped it in the faded blue kerchief. "I do not know whether you are that man. I know only that I have not met another."

He held the parcel out to me.

"The earth remembers," he said. "So the old man bade me say."

I took it. The parcel was light. Light as a bird. And warm - like the coin, like a stone in the sun, like a hand held too long to let go.

* * *

Then everyone dispersed. Sventsitsky - to the guest room, to write, - he said he would not sleep till he had written down everything, to the last word, and that he would leave the day after tomorrow, "if the General does not abduct me again." Strauch - to his outbuilding, where, as I learned afterward from Dunyasha, he sat till morning smoking cigar after cigar and rereading some German book. The General - to the terrace, alone. My aunt - to her rooms, saying not another word.

Konstantin walked me to my room. In silence. At the door he stopped.

"Mon cher," he said. "Brazil. Argentina. Fiji. Khiva. Tarnovo." He paused. "Everywhere there was someone. Young. Nameless. Who did not remember who he was. And everywhere left one and the same line."

"I heard."

"And everywhere went away," said Konstantin. Quietly. "Into the jungle. Into the pampa. Into the sea from a cliff." He looked at me, and in his eyes, in the uncertain candlelight, was something that made my heart contract. "Will you go too?"

I did not know what to answer.

"I don't know," I said honestly.

Konstantin nodded. As though this were exactly what he had feared.

"Then I shall sit by the door," he said. "Every night. Until you know."

"You cannot sit by the door every night."

"I can," said Konstantin. "I am a lieutenant. I know how not to sleep." He clapped me on the shoulder. "Good night, man from the City of the Caesars."

And went off without looking back.

* * *

I laid the parcel on the table. Beside the blue notebook. Beside the cutting of the black rose in its clay pot.

And saw that the bud had opened.

Not entirely. Halfway. The dark scarlet, almost black petals had parted, revealing the heart - golden, like the pollen on the legs of bees in the General's garden. From the bud came a smell. Strong. Sweet. Honey and dust. Honey and sand. Honey and something else - steppe-like, bitter, like wormwood in August.

Like the scroll.

I sat on the bed and looked a long time at the rose that had opened out of season, in a foreign land, on my windowsill.

"When this rose blooms in a foreign land - I shall return."

Somewhere far off, at the end of the corridor, in the gallery, in the moonlight, hung the portrait of a young man in a light neckcloth, with two stones in his hand. Under crape. Which Agafya Tikhonovna, I knew, had straightened again - as she had straightened it every morning for fifty years.

And I knew - not understanding how - that this crape would fall no more.

Because the one who had been awaited had already come.
