Chapter XXXI
The Pharaoh's Judgment

The man with the scar across his brow was taken on the fifth day of the festival, at dawn, at the back gate of the temple of Ptah.

He came out of it for the first time in three days, carefully, glancing about, in the cloak of a temple water-carrier, with a jar on his shoulder, as though going to the river for water. It was the jar that gave him away. Panehsy told me afterward that the water-carriers of the temple of Ptah carry their jars on the left shoulder, because the well stands to the left of the gate. So it had always been done, for a hundred years. But this man carried his on the right.

"My men followed him all the way to the river," said the commander of the Medjay, standing before me in the morning half-light, motionless as a statue of dark wood, with throwing-sticks on a leather baldric. "At the river a boat was waiting for him. A small one. A fisherman's. The rower was from the Delta, judging by his speech. They meant to go north." He paused. "We took them both. Quietly. No one saw. We broke the jar. There was no water in it."

"What was in it?"

Panehsy held out his palm. Three things lay on it.

A handful of grain: barley, large, choice, from the temple stores. A little blue scarab on a cord of twisted papyrus. And a bracelet. Gold. Narrow, thin, with a chased pattern of interlaced lotuses.

"The bracelet was hidden in the grain," said Panehsy. "At the very bottom. Payment. Or a sign by which he was to be recognized in the north." He paused. "Such bracelets, my Pharaoh, are made in only one workshop in the capital. For one house. I asked the craftsman. He knew his work at once." Panehsy raised his eyes to me. "For the house of Khaemuas."

* * *

Senusret came an hour later. With him came Paser, leaning on his staff, and the Vizier Ptahhotep, with his inseparable basket of scrolls, which the scribe Ani carried after him, puffing.

They stood before me in the small hall, the very one where the council of commanders had once met, where beneath the painting of the bound captives Bakenren had sat, where Khaemuas had first spoken before everyone of the "third way." The morning light fell through the high windows in slanting columns, and dust turned slowly in them.

"My Pharaoh," said Senusret. His voice was even, but I saw the muscles working along his jaw. "I have been gathering this for two moons. Since the evening you returned from the temple pool in another's clothes and your own sandals." He nodded to Ani, and the old scribe, grunting, set the basket on the table and began to lay out scrolls, tablets, potsherds, one after another, as one lays out pieces on a board. "Here is what we have."

He spoke at length. Slowly. In a soldier's way: briefly, exactly, without ornament, as though reporting the disposition of enemy detachments.

Blind Meru, gatekeeper of the temple garden, forty floods at the wicket, had heard "the man who jingles" come to the pool every seventh night and speak with someone from the Temple. Meru does not see. But Meru knows the jingle. Senusret had taken five men in bracelets to him, five nobles, one after another, under various pretexts. Meru recognized one.

Pentu, junior scribe of the temple of Ptah, who had copied for Bakenren the false inventories of the granaries and now was secretly handing the copies to Iti, had found among the temple receipts one dated last flood: "House of Khaemuas, son of Nebseni. Debt to the temple of Ptah paid in full. By the grace of the High Priest." The sum of the debt was such that Nebamun, on reading it, whistled for the first time since I had known him.

Djehuty, from the southern outposts, had sent a messenger: two commanders of those detachments assigned to guard the Opet procession, the very ones with scarabs on their chests, had last month received land in the Delta. Each, a field of ten arouras. The land was registered as temple land. The deeds of transfer were signed by a scribe in the service of the house of Khaemuas.

Nakht, the young bearer of the barque, had heard priests in the temple say to one another: "The brave one with the bracelets promised that at Luxor only our men would stand at the gates."

"And now this," Senusret finished, nodding at the bracelet in my palm. "The man who burned Little Nekhen hid three days in the temple of Ptah. A hand with a bracelet let him in. And he was leaving for the north with a bracelet of the house of Khaemuas in his jar."

The hall fell quiet. Dust turned in the pillars of light.

"Shall I repeat that for the record?" Ani whispered loudly, tilting his head.

No one laughed.

* * *

"My Pharaoh," said Paser. He stood leaning on his staff with both hands, and his face, carved with wrinkles, was sad. "I knew his father. Nebseni. We served together in the chariots under your..." he faltered, "under the former Pharaoh. A good soldier. Brave. Foolish, as all brave men are." The old man paused. "He lost everything he had. At dice. In beer. In women. He owed the temple of Ptah so much that the priests could have taken not only his fields but his name. And Khaemuas was still a boy. Merry. Carefree." Paser sighed. "I remember how four years ago, on the hunt in the Delta, he joked that the priests would take their fields, but he would have a bracelet for a third hand."

"I remember too," I said.

"The Temple did not take the fields, my Pharaoh," said Paser. "The Temple took the boy. Paid for the father. And presented the bill to the son."

"That is no excuse," said Senusret sharply.

"It is no excuse," Paser agreed. "It is an explanation." He looked at Senusret. "You are a soldier, Senusret. You know the difference between an enemy and a captive. The enemy comes at you because he wants to. The captive, because he is driven."

"A captive who opens the gates to the enemy," said Senusret, "is worse than the enemy."

They looked at one another, two old warriors, one with a sword, the other with a staff, and neither lowered his eyes.

"Ptahhotep," I said. "What does the law say?"

* * *

The Vizier Ptahhotep unrolled one of the scrolls, old, worn, covered in small, faded signs, and began to read. His voice was dry and even, the voice of a man accustomed to reading aloud laws that no one wishes to hear.

"'He who has plotted against the life of Pharaoh, may he live, be whole and healthy,'" Ptahhotep read, "'shall be brought to judgment. The court shall be convened from the dignitaries whom Pharaoh names. The court shall hear witnesses. The witnesses shall swear by the life of Pharaoh. If guilt is proven, the guilty shall be deprived of his name. His name shall be erased from all walls and all scrolls. His body shall not be buried. His ka shall hunger forever. His family shall be sent to the quarries. His line shall be cut off.'" Ptahhotep paused. "So it was under former Pharaohs. And under the Pharaohs before them."

"Deprived of his name," I repeated.

"The most terrible penalty, my Pharaoh," said Ptahhotep. "Worse than death. Death is only a departure into the Duat. But a man without a name cannot pass through the Duat. He cannot stand before the forty-two judges, for they do not know whom they are judging. He cannot speak his own name. He will wander between worlds. Forever. Not remembering who he is."

A man without a name, wandering between worlds. Not remembering who he is.

I felt the cold rise from somewhere beneath my ribs, on the left.

"My Pharaoh?" Ptahhotep raised his eyes to me. "Are you unwell?"

"Go on, Ptahhotep."

"There is one more mercy," said the Vizier. "An ancient one. It was granted to the nobly born. If guilt is proven, but Pharaoh wishes to spare the line, the guilty is given a choice. To die by his own hand. Quietly. Then his name is not erased. Then his body is buried. Then his family is left to live."

"And if Pharaoh wishes more?"

Ptahhotep looked at me for a long time.

"The law knows no more, my Pharaoh," he said. "More can be wished only by Pharaoh himself."

* * *

Ani, who all this time had been sitting in the corner on a low bench scratching something on a potsherd with his reed brush, suddenly raised his head.

"My Pharaoh," he said in his loud, slightly muffled whisper, which could be heard across the whole hall. "Will you permit an old scribe a word?"

Everyone turned. Ani did not speak in councils, ever. He only asked for repetitions.

"Speak, Ani."

The old man laid aside his brush. Folded his hands on his knees.

"When I was a boy in the school of scribes," he said, "we were taught to write from the old teachings. We copied them hundreds of times until we knew them by heart." He paused. "One of them I copied so often that I remember every word to this day. Though I no longer remember the name of my first wife." Somewhere in the hall someone snorted. Ani was not embarrassed. "It is the teaching of a Pharaoh. An ancient one. Who was killed. He wrote it for his son. After his death."

"After his death?" Paser asked.

"So it is said there," Ani nodded. "The Pharaoh speaks to his son from the Duat. And this is what he says."

The old man closed his eyes. And began to speak, slowly, in a chant, in quite a different voice from his usual one, young and ringing, as though it were not he who spoke but a boy of the school of scribes sixty years ago:

"'Beware of subjects, for they are nothing. Do not approach them in solitude. Trust not a brother. Know not a friend. Make no intimates for yourself; there is no profit in it. When you sleep, guard your heart yourself, for on the day of trouble a man has no supporters.'" Ani paused. "'I gave to the beggar and fed the orphan. I raised up the poor as I did the rich. But he who ate my bread raised his hand against me. He to whom I stretched out my hands made of them a noose. It was after supper, and night had fallen. I lay down to rest upon my bed, for I was weary. And my heart began to fall asleep. And then the weapons that should have guarded me rose up, against me.'"

Ani opened his eyes. A dead silence stood in the hall.

"So he wrote, my Pharaoh," the old scribe said quietly, in his ordinary voice. "The ancient Pharaoh. Who was killed by his own guards. At night. While he slept." He paused. "We were made to copy this a hundred times. So that we would remember: no one can be trusted."

"And did you remember?" I asked.

Ani looked at me with his faded, watering eyes.

"I remembered, my Pharaoh," he said. "But I have lived seventy years. And I know one more thing that was not in the teaching." He paused. "That Pharaoh trusted no one. No one. Not brother, nor friend, nor guard." Ani spread his hands. "And they killed him all the same."

* * *

"Bring in the prisoner," I said.

Inu and Bata brought him in, one holding each arm. Tall, wiry, his skin stained dark with the juice of some plant; the dye was already peeling in patches, revealing beneath it the skin of an ordinary dweller of the Delta, sunburnt but fair. The scar across his brow, old, white, uneven. An earring in his ear. His hands tied behind his back.

He did not fall to his knees. He stood straight, looking before him. And his face was calm, with the calm of a man who knows he is already dead and therefore fears nothing more.

"What is your name?" I asked.

He did not answer.

"What is your name?" Senusret repeated, and steel rang in his voice.

The prisoner slowly turned his head to Senusret. Looked at him. Smiled crookedly.

"What difference does it make?" he said. His voice was hoarse, his speech of the Delta, soft, sing-song. "You will erase my name anyway. Why should you know it?"

"We do not erase a name we do not know," I said.

He looked at me. Long. For the first time, directly.

"Cunning," he said. "I was told that Pharaoh is cunning. I did not believe it."

"Who told you?"

He fell silent again.

And then I said:

"Bring in Paney."

* * *

The boy came in, barefoot, with a training spear in his hand, which Bata had evidently forgotten to take from him. The arm once broken had long since healed; there was no bandage. He came in quickly, and stopped dead three paces from the prisoner.

He looked at him for a long time.

The prisoner looked at the boy. At first indifferently. Then, eyes slightly narrowed, as if trying to remember. Then...

Then his face changed.

"I promised you, Paney," I said quietly. "That you would look at him. So that he would see you are alive."

Paney did not answer. He stood gripping the training spear and looking at the man with the scar. His lips trembled. But his eyes did not.

"Little Nekhen," said Paney. His voice was quiet, but in the silence of the hall it sounded loud. "A village on the border. Four huts on the bank. Boats. Nets. My mother baked bread in a clay oven in the yard. My father mended a net. His name was Hevi." The boy paused. "You came at night. With a torch. You set our roof alight. I saw your face in the firelight. This scar. This earring." He swallowed. "My father told me to run. And I ran."

The prisoner looked at the boy. And the calm on his face slowly, like dried clay, began to crack.

"I walked two days through the desert," Paney went on. "With a broken arm. With a knife. I thought I would die. I did not die." He raised the training spear, not threatening, merely showing it. "Now I am learning to hold a spear. Bata teaches me. He is a good teacher. When I grow up I will be a warrior. Or a scribe. I have not decided yet." The boy paused. "And you? What will you become?"

The prisoner was silent. Then, very slowly, he lowered his head.

"They told me," he said hoarsely, not raising his eyes, "that the village was empty. That the people had gone. Because of the drought. They told me: burn the empty huts, so that Pharaoh would think the southerners were advancing. They told me no one would be hurt."

"Who told you?" I asked.

The prisoner was silent a long while. Then he raised his head, not toward me. Toward Paney.

"What was your father's name, boy?" he asked.

"Hevi," said Paney. "Hevi the fisherman."

The prisoner closed his eyes.

"My name is Senedjem," he said. "I serve the house of Nebseni. Twenty years. I carried young Khaemuas on my shoulders when he was an infant." His voice broke. "Khaemuas gave me the bracelet. Said: take it north, to Bakenren's people, they will pay in grain. Said: burn the empty huts. Said: no one will be hurt." He opened his eyes, and tears stood in them. "He did not know, my Pharaoh. I swear. He did not know there were people there. He was in the capital. The priests told him. The priests said: empty."

Paney stood motionless. Then suddenly turned to me.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "I have looked. He sees that I am alive." The boy paused. "May I go? Bata is waiting for me. We have a lesson."

"Go, Paney," I said.

The boy bowed, awkwardly, like a child, and went out. Without looking back. Bata, standing at the door, let him pass and, when the door closed, turned to the wall, so that no one should see his face.

* * *

Khaemuas was brought in at midnight.

Not to the throne room, but to the small hall, by the light of a single lamp, without witnesses save those who already knew. Senusret. Paser. Ptahhotep. Panehsy. Ani with his potsherd and brush. And Iti, by the door, in the shadow, with a tablet, writing down every word. I had commanded him to be there. Because someone had to remember.

Khaemuas came in himself. Not under guard; I had ordered that he not be arrested, but simply invited: "Pharaoh wishes to speak with you." He came in his best linen, in bracelets, five on the left arm, five on the right, perfumed, his eyes painted, smiling. The smile held until the very moment he saw who was sitting in the hall.

Then he saw on the table before me the bracelet. Narrow, golden, with interlaced lotuses.

The smile vanished. His face went grey.

The bracelets on his arms chimed, thin, melodious, out of place, like laughter at a funeral. He pressed his arms to his sides to muffle the sound. I had seen that movement before. In a dream, the heavy, recurring dream of Luxor that came to me more and more often: in a courtyard, by a column.

"Sit down, Khaemuas," I said.

He did not sit. He fell to his knees.

* * *

I shall not retell all that was said that night. Iti wrote it down, every word, and his tablets now lie where tablets lie that no one must read. But some things I remember.

I remember how at first he denied it. Said the bracelet had been stolen. That Senedjem was a runaway servant who had slandered him. That Meru was blind and deaf and could not tell one jingle from another. That Pentu was a liar who forged inventories, everyone knew that.

I remember how Senusret silently laid before him the receipt of the temple of Ptah. "Debt paid in full. By the grace of the High Priest." With the seal.

I remember how Khaemuas looked at it. Long. And then covered his face with his hands, and the bracelets on both arms chimed at once, all ten.

"They came to my father," he said dully, through his palms. "Two years ago. Said: tomorrow we take everything. The house. The fields. The servants. The name. My father wept. My father, who had ridden in chariots against the southerners, wept like a child." Khaemuas lowered his hands. His face was wet, the paint beneath his eyes smeared. "And then Bakenren came. Said: the Temple can forgive. If the son becomes a friend of the Temple. A little. Only a little. To listen. To pass things on. To say in councils what was needed." He sobbed. "I thought it was nothing. Words. Only words. I have always said a great many words."

"And the detachments to guard the procession?" asked Senusret. "The commanders with scarabs? The land in the Delta?"

"Bakenren said it was for Pharaoh's safety," Khaemuas whispered. "Said there might be enemies at Luxor. Southerners. Loyal men were needed. Temple men."

"And Little Nekhen?"

Khaemuas froze.

"Senedjem," he said barely audibly. "Senedjem said so?" He raised to me eyes full of horror. "My Pharaoh, I was told it was empty. The priests said the people had gone because of the drought. Said: simply burn the huts, so that... so that you would see the border is unguarded. That someone stronger..." he faltered, "was needed." He fell silent. Then said very quietly: "Were there people there?"

"There were people, Khaemuas," I said. "A fisherman named Hevi. His wife, who was baking bread. Their neighbours." I paused. "And a boy who walked two days through the desert with a broken arm. This morning he stood in this hall. And looked into the eyes of your Senedjem."

Khaemuas doubled over as if struck in the belly. Pressed his forehead to the floor. And howled, softly, terribly, like an animal, as women howl over the dead.

No one in the hall moved.

* * *

When he had quieted, I asked him the main thing.

"Khaemuas. At Luxor. On the last day of the festival. In the inner court. What is to happen?"

He raised his head. For a long time he looked at me with wet, red eyes.

"I do not know, my Pharaoh," he said. "I swear by the life of Pharaoh, I do not know. Bakenren said only: 'Our men must stand at the gates.' What would happen beyond the gates, I was not told. Nefer-Ka never spoke to me himself. Only through Bakenren. And Bakenren..." He swallowed. "Bakenren is gone. He was taken away. The night after the envoy. No one knows where."

"Would you have raised your hand against me?" I asked. "There, in the court?"

Khaemuas looked at me. And suddenly, through the tears, through the horror, through the smeared paint, something like wounded pride flickered in his eyes.

"No," he said. "Never. I am a coward, my Pharaoh. A traitor. A fool. I sold you for my father's debt and for bracelets. But I would never have raised my hand." He stretched out both hands to me, palms up, the bracelets chiming. "Look. I do not know how to hold a weapon. On the hunt I never once hit a bird. Four years ago, in the Delta, remember? I threw the throwing-stick and missed. Everyone laughed."

I remembered. I looked at his hands. At the bracelets. Narrow, thin, with chased lotuses. Ten of them. All narrow.

Khemu had seen a hand. Raised. With a bracelet. A wide one. Golden. Wide.

Khaemuas had not a single wide bracelet.

* * *

"What will the judgment be, my Pharaoh?" asked Ptahhotep, when Khaemuas had been led into the next room to wait. "Deprivation of the name? Or the mercy of the ancients, his own hand?"

All looked at me. Senusret sternly, his jaw clenched. Paser sadly. Panehsy inscrutably. Ani with his head tilted, as if preparing to ask for a repetition. Iti, in the shadow by the door, not lifting his eyes from the tablet, but his brush had stopped.

"Neither," I said.

Senusret stepped forward.

"My Pharaoh..."

"Listen to me, Senusret." I rose. "If we erase his name, tomorrow the whole court will know. The day after, Nefer-Ka. He will understand that we know of the detachments at the gates. He will replace them with others. Ones we do not know. And at Luxor, at the gates, will stand men whose faces we have never seen."

Senusret was silent. The muscles worked along his jaw.

"If Khaemuas dies by his own hand, it will be the same," I went on. "Nefer-Ka will understand." I paused. "But if Khaemuas tomorrow morning comes out to the chariot as he always has, in his bracelets, smiling, perfumed, and laughs, and jokes, and tells Bakenren's men what they wish to hear, then Nefer-Ka will understand nothing. The detachments at the gates will remain the same. And we shall know every one of them by sight."

"You wish to keep a traitor at your side," said Senusret slowly, "all the way to Luxor?"

"I wish," I said, "for the traitor to become my eyes where I have no eyes."

Paser, leaning on his staff, suddenly laughed softly, drily, with a rattle, as old men laugh.

"Mercy as a weapon," he said. "I saw it once. Under your... under the former Pharaoh. He caught a spy of the southerners. And instead of executing him, let him go. With gifts." Paser paused. "A year later that spy brought us half the southern army. To surrender. He said their king executes for mistakes, while ours gives gifts for them. They preferred the one who gives."

"And if Khaemuas betrays again?" asked Senusret. "There, in Luxor?"

"Then," I said, "you will be standing at the gates, Senusret. And you will see it first."

Senusret looked at me for a long time. Then, for the first time all evening, he lowered his eyes.

"You want me to stay at the gates," he said hollowly. "As the blind old woman said."

"I want," I said, "you to be where you can do something. And not where you can only die beside me."

* * *

Khaemuas was brought back. He stood before me, emptied, his paint smeared, his hands trembling, hands in which the bracelets no longer sounded, for he was pressing them to his sides.

I told him what would be.

He listened, and his face changed, first disbelief, then horror, then something I could not read. When I finished, he was silent for a long time.

"Why?" he asked at last. In a whisper.

"Because you said you would never have raised your hand against me," I said. "And I believe you."

"You should not believe me, my Pharaoh. No one should believe me. I do not believe myself."

"I know," I said. "That is why I believe you. Liars always say that they can be believed."

Khaemuas looked at me. Then slowly, very slowly, he took one bracelet from his left arm. Narrow, golden, with interlaced lotuses. Laid it on the table. Beside the one found in Senedjem's jar.

"There were two," he said. "My mother gave them. When I was born. One for me. The second for whoever would save my life." He paused. "The second I gave to Senedjem. So that in the north they would know he came from me." His voice trembled. "Mother said: the second bracelet must not be given away for nothing. It will return to the one for whom it is meant."

He raised his eyes to me.

"It has returned, my Pharaoh."

* * *

Senedjem was not executed. Panehsy took him away by night, south, to Djehuty, to the Rock of the First Ones, where men who knew how to keep silent were needed. I ordered one thing told to him: that his name would not be erased. That it would be written, in the scroll that Iti was writing, beside the name of the fisherman Hevi of Little Nekhen. So that a thousand years from now someone would read them together.

Khaemuas came out in the morning to the chariot. In bracelets, nine, not ten. Smiling. Perfumed. He laughed, joked, clapped the commanders with scarabs on the shoulders. I watched from the balcony. His smile was flawless. Only when he passed the column by the gate, one like that by which he had stood with lowered eyes in my dream of Luxor, he slowed his step for a single moment. And looked up, at my balcony.

I nodded to him.

He nodded in return. And walked on.

* * *

Iti found me on the roof toward morning, where we now met if the garden was impossible. He brought a tablet. On it, the whole judgment. Every word.

"My Pharaoh," he said. "May I ask?"

"Ask."

"The bracelet. The wide one. Which Khemu saw." Iti paused. "If it was not Khaemuas... then who?"

I was looking south. At the dark horizon, beyond which, many days' journey away, lay Luxor.

"I do not know, Iti," I said. "Perhaps no one. Perhaps Khemu was mistaken. Visions are not always exact."

Iti was silent for a long while.

"I have reviewed the inventories of the palace treasury, my Pharaoh," he said at last. Quietly. "All of them. For twenty years. Wide golden bracelets with the uraeus, such as are worn on special feasts, three are kept in the treasury. One belonged to the former Pharaoh. One, to the former High Priest. It is passed on together with the staff." Iti paused. "And the third..."

"The third?"

Iti raised his eyes to me.

"The third is issued to Pharaoh, my Pharaoh," he said. "For the festival of Opet. That he may put it on in the inner court of the god. When he stands before him." His voice trembled. "To you."

I looked at him. Cold rose from beneath my ribs, slowly, inexorably, like water in the well of the nilometer in a good year.

A hand with a wide golden bracelet. Raised. In the darkness between the columns.

"Iti," I said. "The second bracelet, the one passed on together with the staff, who has it now?"

"The High Priest, my Pharaoh," Iti whispered. "Nefer-Ka."

We were both silent. Over the eastern hills the edge of the sun appeared. Somewhere below, baboons cried out.

Five days remained until the new moon.
