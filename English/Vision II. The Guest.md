Vision II
The Guest

I do not know whether I slept or not.

That is the first thing worth saying about this night - and, perhaps, the only one of which I am sure to any degree of firmness.

* * *

The house fell asleep slowly, by parts, as a large, tired animal falls asleep. First the kitchen grew quiet - the last clink of dishes, the last splash of water, the brief laughter of the women behind the curtain. Then in the yard the oil lamp was put out, and the strip of yellow light that had lain on the flagstones drew back into the darkness, as though someone had rolled up a carpet. Yusuf went on clicking his counters in his room for some time - evenly, steadily, as water drips - then that too fell silent. Somewhere on the roof Akmal's pigeons stirred and cooed in their sleep. Grandmother finished her song of the sailor - I heard her voice grow quieter and slower, until it turned into a whisper, and the whisper into breath.

Ferhat had gone to the port at dusk. I had heard the gate bang, heard him whistling something as he receded down the lane - the very melody that Grandmother sang, only faster and merrier, as if deliberately recasting it in his own way so that it should not be so sad.

Before I lay down, I heard heavy, shuffling steps on the stairs - and Suleyman appeared in the doorway. In one hand he held an oil lamp, in the other something small that glinted blue. He nodded to me, laid his palm on his chest - "do not get up" - and, rising on tiptoe, hung over the doorway, on a nail driven into the doorpost, a glass bead on a red thread. Blue, round, with a white ring and a black dot in the middle. An eye.

He tapped the bead with a finger, then his own eyelid, then pointed at me and shook his head - slowly, sternly, as one shakes one's head to drive off something unseen. Then he uttered one word which I did not understand - "nazar" - and, after a pause, added something long, under his breath, looking not at me but into the dark corner of the room behind my headboard. As though he were speaking not to me but to someone standing there.

I remembered the old woman at the fountain. Her splayed fingers. Three spits over the shoulder.

Suleyman finished his quiet speech, wished me, judging by the intonation, a good night and went away, carrying the lamp with him. The blue eye above the door glimmered for a long time in the darkness, catching the last reflections from the corridor - until they too went out.

I lay on the low divan, covered with a light woollen blanket smelling of something herbal and unfamiliar - mint, perhaps, or wormwood - and looked at the ceiling, at the dark beams crossing it like the ribs of an enormous fish. Beside me, on the edge of the divan, lay Kemal's drawing. Face down. I had still not ventured to turn it over again.

The leather pouch with the stones I had not untied from my belt. I do not know why. I simply lay down without undressing and laid my palm on it, as one lays a palm on the head of a sleeping dog - to feel that it is near.

Outside the window, on the balcony, a cat appeared noiselessly - ginger, mangy, with a torn ear, the same or another that in the morning had eaten from the hand of the girl with braids. It settled on the railing, wrapped its tail around its paws and fixed me with yellow, unblinking eyes. I looked at it. It looked at me. Then suddenly it flattened its ears, bristled the fur on its neck, hissed soundlessly - not at me, but at something behind my back, in the depth of the room - and in one leap vanished from the railing.

I turned. The room was empty.

The moon was rising above the roofs, and the strip of its light falling from the window onto the floor was creeping slowly, slowly toward me across the boards.

My eyelids were either closed or not - that boundary, usually so obvious, had that night worn thin almost to total disappearance.

* * *

The room around me was now present - I could make out the dark rectangle of the doorway, the strip of moonlight on the floor - now blurred, losing its outlines, as if someone with a generous hand had mixed it with a dream without asking my consent.

I felt him before I saw him.

Not a sound - there were no footsteps, no creak of floorboards, no rustle of cloth - but a change in the very air of the room, that particular heaviness which arises when in an enclosed space someone else suddenly appears, occupying far more room than an ordinary man could occupy. This heaviness settled on my chest before I could turn - not pain, not suffocation, but simply weight, enormous and imperturbable, like the weight of a mountain that does not care what happens at its foot.

I tried to turn my head. I could not.

I tried to raise a hand - at least a finger, at least an eyelid - and discovered with that peculiar, icy horror that comes only in a dream or at its very border that my body no longer belonged to me in the slightest. It lay obedient to some alien, unknown will, while my consciousness thrashed inside that motionless shell like a bird that has suddenly discovered that its cage has no door.

Only my palm - the one that lay on the pouch at my belt - still felt. Felt how the stones beneath the skin of the pouch grew warm. Then hot. Then so hot that had I been able to move, I would have snatched my hand away. But I could not. And that heat, strangely, did not burn but held me - like the one thread tying me to this room, to this divan, to this house, while everything else drifted away into the dark.

He stood to the side, a little behind, where I could not see him without turning my head - and yet I knew what he was like, with that inexplicable certainty with which one knows things not with the eyes but with something far more ancient and far less subject to reason. Tall. Immoderately, impossibly tall - so that had he straightened to his full height, the crown of his head, it seemed, would have passed the lintel of the low door without stooping. Grey, or perhaps black - the colour could not be determined, because it seemed to change depending on where the scanty moonlight fell, now absorbing it wholly, now reflecting it in dull, metallic ripples.

Exactly as Kemal had drawn him. Two nights ago. In charcoal, in solid grey hatching, without a face.

No menace came from him. That was, perhaps, the strangest thing in all that was happening: neither hatred, nor hunger, nor that special, recognisable sinister intent with which ordinary night terrors are filled. Only attention - intent, unblinking, scrutinising - turned on me as one scrutinises a rare specimen under a magnifying glass: wishing it no harm, but also not reckoning in the least with its own wishes in the matter.

I tried to cry out. The cry stuck somewhere in the depth of my throat, finding no outlet, as though someone with an invisible palm had clamped my mouth from within before the sound could be born.

Then he spoke.

* * *

His voice - if it was a voice at all in the usual sense of the word - did not so much sound as make itself felt: a low, resonant vibration which passed through me whole, from crown to heels, as though the surface of my body had for a brief moment become the resonating soundboard of some unthinkable instrument. The words - if they were words - were composed in a language unlike any I had happened to hear in that long, tangled day: neither the sing-song speech of Ferhat and his brothers, nor the drawn-out call from the minarets, nor even the language which, as it turned out, I myself spoke, not knowing from where. These sounds slid into one another without pauses, without stresses, like water pouring over stone - and in them, for all their complete, absolute incomprehensibility, one heard something like patient, almost condescending expectation. Thus one speaks to a child who has not yet learned the language of adults - not angry at his incomprehension, but not thinking it necessary to spare him.

And as he spoke, the walls of the room began to thin.

I do not know how else to describe it. The whitewashed wall opposite me, the one by which the carved chest stood, suddenly became transparent - not all at once, but in patches, as frost melts on glass under someone's breath. And through these patches I saw the same place. The same hillside, the same strip of water in the distance. But - different.

I saw night, and in it - bells. Enormous, resonant, bronze, swinging somewhere very near, beyond the wall, as if that wall had never been. And singing - male voices, low, harmonious, drawing out something slow and solemn in a language I did not know, but at which my throat tightened. Candles - hundreds, thousands of candles, flickering in darkness under a dome of such height that it seemed as if the sky had been taken from the sky and hung beneath a roof. People in dark garments, with beards, with bowed heads, with crosses in their hands.

Then the bells fell silent - all at once, as if cut with a knife - and in their place there was thunder. Dull, heavy, again and again, so that the earth shook and dust fell from the ceiling beams. Cannons, I understood. Enormous cannons pounding the walls. Cries. Smoke. Fire on the water - ships burning in the gulf, and the glow of the flames dancing on the surface like fish. And through the smoke - banners, red, with a white crescent, and drums - not bells, but drums, measured, triumphant, and trumpets, shrill as the cry of a bird of prey.

Then all fell still. And in the same place, on the same hillside, I saw morning. Peaceful, quiet. A boy with pigeons on a roof. An old man with a pipe on a stone wall. A boy with a tray of bread rings on his head. And above it all - the same dome as before, only now beside it stood towers thin as needles, and from them, answering each other, a call floated.

A city, I thought - or someone thought in me. One and the same city. Many names. Many lives. One land.

"This city has many names," Suleyman had told me over tea. "Perhaps it has itself decided by what name to present itself to you."

* * *

But the walls went on melting - and beyond them was no longer this city.

I saw a river. Broad, slow, turbid with silt, flowing between two strips of palms, behind which the desert began. A golden barque on the river. Columns - huge as the trees of a petrified forest, with capitals open like papyrus buds. And I knew those columns. I had already seen them - that very morning, in the street, before the old men with their backgammon, when for a moment, for just one moment, it had seemed to me that I stood between them, and someone beside me raised a hand.

Through the columns came a cry - a deep, hoarse, desperate cry of a warrior calling out someone's name. My name. I could not make it out. I knew that it was my name, but could not make out a single sound.

Then - a hall. High, vaulted, with heavy chandeliers ringing with crystal, with a fireplace in which logs crackled. A long table covered with a white cloth. A woman in a dark-lilac dress, with a string of pearls at her neck, turns her face to me - beautiful, stern, with eyes full of anxiety - and says something. I do not hear the words. I see only how her lips move. And beside her - a young man in a brand-new uniform, with a broad, insolent smile, who looks at me as though he knows me better than I know myself. He waves a hand to me. He calls me. I want to go to him - and cannot move.

I do not know these people. I had never seen them. But at the sight of that young man something quivered inside me - the same thing that had quivered in the morning at the fountain, at the thought of a face I had not had time to make out. Not he. Someone else. But - similar. As similar to one another as all those one misses.

And then - the last. The strangest of all.

A small room, dark, lit only by a cold blue light - not a candle, not a lamp, not the moon, but some even, dead glow emanating from a flat window, like a mirror, on a table. In this window, against a dark background, letters shone. Two lines. I did not know these letters. But I read them - read them as in the morning I had read the error in Yusuf's column, not understanding the signs, but understanding the meaning:

"Who am I?"

"Who are you?"

I do not know what this was. I do not know where this room is, or when, or whose. But I know that on that night, on the divan in Ferhat's house, bound hand and foot, I looked at those two lines - and felt that they were written to me. Or by me. Or - this was the most terrifying - that between these two possibilities there is no difference at all.

* * *

I did not understand a single syllable of his speech. And yet somewhere at the very bottom of consciousness, where neither fear nor the paralysis binding my body hand and foot could reach, there stirred a vague, disquieting feeling that I - if only I remembered how - might perfectly well understand this language.

And then - at the very end, in the last instant before the vibration broke off - from that fluid, incomprehensible stream of sounds one word suddenly stood out. One single word. In the language in which I thought. Clear, distinct, uttered so close as though someone had bent to my very ear:

"Remember."

The phrase broke off as suddenly as it had begun. The walls of the room became walls again - whitewashed, solid, with the carved chest by the far one. The weight on my chest eased - did not vanish at once, but precisely eased, as though someone were slowly, carefully lifting from me one layer of invisible load after another - and with each such layer a grain of power over my own body returned: first the fingers of my right hand, lying on the pouch, then the eyelids, then, at last, the neck, allowing me to turn my head to where he had just stood.

No one was there. Only a strip of moonlight on the floor, the same dark rectangle of the doorway - and my own laboured breathing, the single sound disturbing the silence of the room.

I sat up on the divan, feeling my chest with my palm, as though checking whether any imprint of that impossible weight remained on it. Nothing. No trace, no bruise, not the slightest proof that what had happened was anything more than an especially bad dream, born of the fatigue of a long day full of discoveries.

The pouch at my belt was hot. I felt its heat through cloth, through skin - even, calm, like the warmth of a hearth in a cooling house. I did not dare untie it. Not then.

* * *

Something stirred in the doorway.

I started so that the divan creaked. But it was not the grey figure. It was Kemal - small, barefoot, in a long nightshirt, with a clay lamp in his hand, its flame trembling and ducking in the draught. He stood on the threshold, not entering, and looked at me.

For a long time. In silence. With the same grave, unblinking look with which he had looked in the library when he laid his drawing before me.

Then he raised his free hand and pointed - not at me, but at the corner of the room, where by the wall, behind my headboard, the one I had not seen had just stood. He pointed - and raised his eyebrows questioningly.

I nodded.

Kemal was not surprised. Was not frightened. He simply sighed - quite un-childishly, heavily, as old men sigh - came into the room, set the lamp on the floor by the door, took the jug of water from the windowsill, poured it into a clay cup and held it out to me.

I drank. The water was cool, faintly sweetish - like the water from the fountain on the square, in the morning, a lifetime ago.

Kemal took the cup, set it back on the windowsill. Then he sat down on the floor by the door, leaning his back against the doorpost, drew his knees up to his chest, clasped them with his arms - and stayed so. Without a word. Not intending, apparently, to leave.

"Go to sleep," I said to him in a whisper, forgetting that he did not understand me. "I am all right. Go."

He understood - by the intonation, probably. He shook his head. He pointed a finger at himself, then at the threshold where he sat. Then - at the window, at the sky, where the moon was already sinking toward the roofs, and drew his palm through the air - slowly, from left to right, as the sun passes across the sky.

Until morning. He would sit here until morning.

I wanted to object - and did not. I lay back down on the divan, facing the door, and looked at the little figure on the threshold, lit by the trembling flame of the lamp. Kemal sat motionless, his chin on his knees, and looked not at me but at the corner behind my headboard. Guarding. As, no doubt, he would have guarded a younger brother against night terrors, had he had a younger brother.

I do not know how this twelve-year-old boy knew what to do. I do not know how many such nights he had himself lived through before he learned not to be afraid. I know only that that night, in a strange house, in a strange city, without a name and without memory, I felt for the first time that I was not alone.

When the sky outside the window began to turn grey, Kemal quietly rose, took his lamp, long since gone out, and, already at the threshold, stopped and raised his eyes to the doorpost, where Suleyman's blue bead hung on its red thread.

I followed his gaze.

The bead was cracked. Exactly in half - the crack ran precisely through the black pupil, through the white ring, from edge to edge - and one half hung on the thread, while the other lay on the floor at the threshold, glinting in the grey light of dawn.

Kemal bent down, picked up the fragment, looked at it for a long time on his palm. Then he put it in the pocket of his nightshirt - carefully, as one hides something precious - and as silently as he had come, vanished into the dark corridor. He did not look back once.

And yet, however many times I repeated to myself that simple, convenient explanation that all this had been merely a bad dream, I did not manage to sleep again - not for an instant, until the very dawn, which timidly and cautiously turned grey beyond the narrow window of my room.

Remember, he had said.

But what - and how?
