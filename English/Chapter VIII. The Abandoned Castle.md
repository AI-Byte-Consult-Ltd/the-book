Chapter VIII
The Abandoned Castle

The darkness after the extinguished candle was so complete that for several moments I ceased to understand where my body ended and the corridor began. I stood with my back pressed against the grille, clutching in my hand a useless candlestick with a still-warm stub, and listened to my heart pounding - the only sound in the whole world.

"Konstantin," I said aloud. Quietly. Then louder: "Konstantin!"

The echo carried his name somewhere into the depth of the corridor, broke it into pieces, returned it to me in scraps - "...tin... tin..." - and died away. No one answered. The piano did not sound. The Castle behind my back was silent as houses are silent in which no one has lived for a long time.

I have ears like a regimental dog, he had said. I shall hear through the whole Castle.

He had not heard. Or - the thought came of itself, uninvited, and I hastily drove it off - here, on this side of the grille, was no longer the sort of place to which sound reaches from the parade hall.

And then warmth struck my face - before I could take even one step forward - a sudden, almost hot gust of air, so alien to the cold stone walls of our Castle that for a moment I doubted whether I was still beneath its roof. My aunt had always loved the coolness of these walls, was proud of it before guests as a special, family merit of the house - here, however, beyond the grille, the air was different: thick, warm, as if borrowed from a quite different climate, a different season, and perhaps a different latitude altogether.

And with the warmth came light - not at once, but gradually, as dawn comes: first I made out my own hand, then the edge of a wall, then the floor, stretching ahead in a long grey strip. The light came from somewhere far away, from the depth of the corridor, even and pale, unlike a candle or a lamp.

I froze on the threshold, listening to myself in search of some explanation - and, not finding one, moved forward, because to stand still in the ringing silence seemed to me a far more frightening occupation than to walk.

* * *

The corridor here proved narrower than before, and the dust that covered the floor in an even, undisturbed layer rose at every step in light, slow little clouds, then settled as unhurriedly, as though it too had nowhere to hurry in this forgotten corner of the house. The tapestries on the walls - the few that still held to their hooks - had rotted so that to recognise the subject once depicted on them was quite impossible: there remained only brown, faded patches where, no doubt, horsemen had once galloped or a stag been hunted. In one place the plaster had fallen away in large flakes, exposing beneath it masonry far more ancient than that of the parade halls familiar to me - stone dark, cracked, as though remembering not decades but centuries.

On the floor, in the dust, I noticed tracks. Not mine - mine stretched behind, deep, distinct. These were different: narrow, light, barely pressing the dust, as though someone weightless had passed along the corridor. The tracks came toward me - and broke off in the middle of the corridor, two paces from me, as though whoever had left them had stopped here, stood - and vanished. No tracks returning, none to the side.

"The White Lady," I recalled Mitka's whisper. "The one who lived here before our mistress."

I skirted the breaking-off tracks, trying not to step on them, and went on, feeling at the back of my head something like a draught, though the air around was motionless and warm.

I passed a niche in which, it seemed, a statue had once stood - the pedestal remained, but the figure itself had vanished without trace, leaving only two pale, inexplicably clean marks where its stone feet had once rested. For some reason it was precisely this detail - not the tapestries, not the dust, but precisely these two empty marks on the pedestal - that for the first time made me seriously doubt that I was still in the house I was accustomed to consider my own.

A little farther on, on the wall, hung a frame. Empty. The canvas had been cut out of it - neatly, evenly, with a sharp knife, along the very edge. Beneath the frame, on a copper plate, as in the gallery where the portrait under crape hung, the name had been scraped away.

I stopped. In the gallery. A portrait under crape, with little stones in his hand. Here - an empty frame. The same plate. The same knife.

I did not know what it meant. I did not want to know. I quickened my step.

* * *

At the end of this endlessly long corridor, the longest in the whole labyrinth that I had been through that evening, I made out a door of imposing size, ajar, with small square windows on either side of the leaf. In one of these windows burned that same faint, even light - not of a candle, not of a torch, but of something else, pale, lacking any tremor of living flame.

Coming closer, I discovered a small room, whose door likewise stood ajar. Inside, with his back to me, someone was arranging something on shelves - methodically, unhurriedly, shelf after shelf. I managed to make out rows of some flat volumes bound in dark leather, several vessels of cloudy glass, boxes with neat labels and something remotely resembling a plan or a map pinned to the far wall - a setting that seemed to me rather an archive or a laboratory than a dwelling. The man himself was clothed in garments of such strange, inexplicable cut that I would not have undertaken to assign them to any epoch or to any social estate known to me: whether uniform, or working smock, or something third whose name I did not know at all, sewn of cloth that glimmered dully and unfamiliarly in that even, incorporeal light. On his hands were thin white gloves, like a butler's - but what he held in them was not a tray but an old book, so carefully as though it might crumble from one careless breath.

I wanted to pass by. But a floorboard creaked under my foot.

The man turned.

He was no longer young - about fifty, with a neat greying beard, in spectacles with a thin metal frame of a kind I had never seen before - and he looked at me with the expression with which a host looks at a guest who has wandered into the wrong room: without alarm, but with slight annoyance.

"Are you from the group?" he asked.

I understood every word. He spoke my language - clearly, without any accent - but pronounced the words somehow differently: faster, more drily, swallowing the endings, as no one spoke among us. And the sense of his question eluded me completely.

"From what group?"

"Well, from the one in the garden." He waved a hand somewhere to the side. "Outsiders are not allowed in here, this is a depository. All your people are in the yard. The exit is there, up the stairs and to the right."

"A depository of what?" I asked.

He looked at me more attentively, over his spectacles. His glance slid over my frock coat, my trousers, lingered on the neckerchief - and in his eyes suddenly flared a lively, almost professional interest.

"Well, well," he said. "What a costume. Are you from the reenactment? They told us there would be people in costume, but I did not think so..." He came closer, squinting. "The cloth is real. And the cut. And the neckerchief is tied..." He broke off. "Who made it for you?"

"My aunt's tailor," I answered, and felt myself how absurd it sounded.

He laughed - briefly, good-naturedly - evidently taking my answer for a joke, for part of that very game in which, by his reckoning, I was a participant.

"Your aunt's. Of course." He shook his head. "Splendid work. Just like in our portraits. You know, in our gallery there is one..." He faltered, as though he had changed his mind about speaking. "Never mind, however. Go to your people, young man, before you are missed. And do not touch anything on the way, I beg you. Everything here holds on its word of honour."

He turned back to the shelves, but before turning away entirely he cast another quick glance at me over his shoulder - and in that glance, as it seemed to me, flickered something like what I had seen that evening at supper in the eyes of Vera Anatolievna: an attempt to recall a face that one cannot quite recognise.

I did not question him. I walked past the half-open door, passed the massive leaves at the end of the corridor and went out into the warm air, saturated with the fragrance of flowering plants.

* * *

Descending the few steps that separated this grandiose door from the ground, I saw a scene that took my breath away.

It was day. Not evening, not the night from which I had just come - a clear, sunny day, at the very height of summer, with a high pale-blue sky and heavy white clouds hanging motionless above the trees. I looked at my watch - and saw that its hands had stopped. Exactly at the minute when I had pushed the door of the grille.

A fairly large crowd of people - I would have counted some twenty, perhaps more - were doing something in the garden of our ancestral Castle. They were dressed in clothing no less strange than that of the man left in the depository behind me - but the strangeness was of another, more disquieting kind, because not one of my comparisons, hurriedly and vainly sorted through, would fit what I saw even approximately.

On some was a cut remotely resembling the military, but in colours such as I had not met in any army known to me - neither grey nor brown, devoid of any hint of parade or distinction. Others wore something like travelling dress, cut, however, in so alien a manner that I would have been at a loss to determine not only the country it came from but even the century in which such a cut could be considered fitting: now too short, now immoderately wide, lacking buttons where they should have been and furnished with fastenings where I had never in my life seen any. The women - I did not at once realise they were women - wore trousers, like men, with bare arms and uncovered heads, and no one around seemed to find anything strange in it.

Some held small, dark, glinting objects in their hands, which from time to time they raised to their eyes or to their ear - the purpose of these objects I decidedly did not understand, as I did not understand a good half of what was in general happening before me on that lawn. One of them held such an object up to the face of another - whether in jest or for some other reason unknown to me - and both afterward laughed with a short, abrupt laugh quite unlike the laughter to which my ear was accustomed.

In the middle of the lawn, on the very spot where my aunt every summer ordered the tent for tea to be pitched, stood an elderly lady in spectacles and some preposterous sun hat, with an umbrella raised above her head - not for shade but, apparently, as a banner which the crowd followed. She spoke loudly, in a sing-song, in that special voice with which people speak who repeat the same thing many times a day.

"...and here, ladies and gentlemen, the famous rose garden was laid out. They say the roses were brought here from the Balkans. The last mistress of the estate, a widow, was very fond of receiving guests - balls were given here, to which the whole province drove..."

I froze. Roses from the Balkans. A widow. Balls. She was speaking of my aunt - of our house, of our garden - speaking as one speaks of people long lying in the earth.

"...and then," the lady went on, lowering her voice for greater effect, and the crowd drew closer to her, "there happened that very story because of which the left wing was closed with a grille. They say that one night a man vanished in the house. Some tell it was a young master, others a servant, others that it was even a guest. He went into the left wing - and..."

At that moment someone in the crowd laughed loudly, someone shouted, calling the stragglers, somewhere some shrill trill rang out - from that same dark object in someone's hand - and the end of the phrase drowned in the general din. When the noise subsided, the lady was already leading her crowd on, toward the pond, telling something about crucian carp.

He went into the left wing - and.

I stood on the steps, and my knees trembled.

* * *

What is happening, I thought in the first, most bewildered instant. Can it be a masquerade - one of those mad balls that my aunt gave for the whole capital public?

But no: however wilful my aunt might be with her entertainments, she would certainly have mentioned such a spectacle to me beforehand, if only to laugh at my inevitable unwillingness to take part. Moreover, these guests behaved decidedly not as one should behave at a social function - they spoke too loudly, almost shouted, laughed without any occasion, ran about the lawn with a looseness I had never met at any reception in our house. One of them had even lain down right on the grass, his head thrown back toward the sky, as though it were the most natural occupation in the world for a guest of a respectable house.

Surely this is another prank of Konstantin's, the eternal jester, I decided, though even this thought, scarcely born, seemed to me unconvincing: the jest would have been too grand, he would have had to hire too many people for it, and Konstantin himself - the only man capable of explaining this madness to me in an intelligible tongue - was nowhere to be seen nearby.

"Mister!"

I started and lowered my eyes. Before me stood a girl of about seven - in a short, above-the-knee frock of bright yellow, with two sticking-out ponytails tied with something shiny, and in strange footwear of white, soft-looking material, with multicoloured laces. In her hand was that same dark, glinting object - flat, the size of a palm.

"Mister, are you real?"

"What?" was all I could manage.

"You look like from a picture," she said, studying me from head to foot. "May I remember you?"

Before I could understand what she meant, the girl turned her back to me, raised the plate before her at arm's length - and I suddenly saw on its dark surface, as in a tiny mirror, her own face, and behind it, over her shoulder, mine. My face. Bewildered, pale, in a dark frock coat and a red-and-blue neckerchief. Moving - repeating my every movement, like a reflection in water. Only reflections, as far as I knew, are never so bright.

Something clicked softly. The plate went dark.

"Thank you!" The girl turned round, beaming, and pressed the plate to her chest as one presses an expensive toy. "Now you will stay with me."

"Katya!" came a sharp woman's voice. "Katya, how many times must I say - do not pester people!"

A woman was hurrying toward us - young, in those same trousers, with a bag over her shoulder. She seized the girl by the hand and smiled at me apologetically.

"Forgive us, for God's sake. She pesters everyone." She ran her eyes over me and likewise lingered on the neckerchief. "What a costume you have... Are you one of the restorers? Or from those who are in historical clubs?"

"I..." I began, and understood that I did not know what to say next.

"Mum, he is real!" the girl broke in. "He does not even know what I have in my hand!"

"Katya!" The woman flushed, tugged her daughter's hand and dragged her away, throwing me over her shoulder: "Sorry again. Have a good day!"

"Now you will stay with me." I looked after them and thought that somewhere in this girl's hands, in a small dark plate, my face now remained - frightened, pale, in a neckerchief. And that I did not know in what year she would look at it.

* * *

I took a step in the direction of the nearest group - two men who were discussing something animatedly right by the flower bed, once, as I recalled, planted with my aunt's roses - intending after all to demand explanations directly, regardless of the whole absurdity of such an undertaking. But before I had come within ten paces, one of them turned his head in my direction - whether at the sound of my steps or for some other reason unknown to me - and his glance, which slid over me, seemed to me so empty, so devoid of any recognition of a living being, that I froze on the spot, unable to force myself to utter a word. He looked not at me - through me, as one looks through glass or through mist. He at once turned back to his interlocutor, as though he had not noticed me at all - but that single second proved enough for me to abandon any thought of seeking explanations from this noisy, alien crowd.

The girl had seen me. Her mother had seen me. The man in the depository - had seen. And this one - had not. As though some people in this garden were here truly, and others - only reflections, shadows, echoes of something long past. Or as though some saw me, and others could not. I did not know which of these was more terrible.

After standing so for a minute in utter stupefaction, feeling cold, clammy fear slowly rise from the very soles of my feet, I noticed in the far part of the garden a bright, orange figure moving unhurriedly in my direction - the only thing in all this unthinkable picture that seemed even remotely commensurate with my understanding of the world. Ignoring the bustle and hubbub around, I made my way toward it, as toward the one island of the recognisable in this sea of oddities suddenly flooding over me.

* * *

Drawing near, I discovered with amazement a monk - clad in bright burgundy robes, remotely recalling the General's tales of distant, hot Asia, of the monasteries of Siam, where he had travelled with the Imperial family. One of the monk's shoulders was bare, his head shaved clean, on his feet were plain sandals, and in his hand he held a rosary of dark wooden beads, which he told with his fingers, without looking, evenly and unhurriedly. His face, weathered but calm, seemed to me a surprisingly timely sight - the first in the last few minutes that did not arouse in me a desire at once to close my eyes tight and hope that all I had seen would prove merely the consequence of overstrain.

He did not belong to the crowd - that was visible at once. He walked by himself, to one side, looking neither at the lady with the umbrella nor at the people with the dark plates. And, unlike them, he looked straight at me. He was walking toward me. As though he had known I was here even before I came out of the doors.

Never before had I happened to meet monks, still less from such distant lands, so invisible to me, and so, obeying habit rather than sober calculation, I greeted him in French - supposing that on that language it would be easier for us to come to terms than on my own, which was native to me but hardly familiar to a visiting wanderer.

To my no small joy, the monk understood me.

"I greet you too," he answered calmly in good, slightly old-fashioned French, with a barely perceptible but unmistakably distinct friendliness in his gaze. "I was waiting for you. Although, I confess, I did not know on which day you would come."

"Waiting? For me?"

"For someone like you," he corrected gently. "For one who would come out of those doors. They are opened rarely."

An inexplicable, almost involuntary feeling of trust stirred in me at that same instant - trust which I did not that evening feel for a single other being around, myself included, perhaps.

"Forgive my bewilderment, Holy Father," I began, trying to make my voice sound steadier than my heart was pounding, "but I seem to have lost my way in the labyrinths of our Castle. I was returning in the evening from supper, chanced upon a part of the house unknown to me - and here I am on this lawn, among people whom I have never before seen and whose garments I decidedly do not understand. Tell me, what is happening here? Who are these people?"

The monk was silent, looking at me a little longer than a simple question required - and in that silence there was a special, calm sadness, such as, no doubt, doctors feel who have guessed the diagnosis before the patient himself.

"They come to look at the house," he said at last. "As one looks at old things, at graves, at what remains of other lives. They do not know who lived here. Legends are told to them. They photograph themselves and leave." He smiled slightly. "People love other people's past. Their own - less."

"But this is our house. My aunt, the guests, supper - all that was an hour ago..."

"Tell me," he interrupted gently. "Where you came from and where, in your understanding, you were going before you found yourself here."

I repeated my story - about the supper, the headache, the long, tangled corridors, the four people who had told me that evening not to go left, the portrait under crape and the black grille whose lock hung open, though the key to it had been lost forty years ago. The monk listened attentively, without interrupting, only his fingers still evenly told the beads of the rosary - and from time to time cast a quick, almost imperceptible glance toward the noisy crowd behind my back, as though checking my words against something known to him alone.

"My aunt, Artemia Aristarkhovna," I added, clutching at that name as one clutches at the banister of a rickety staircase, "remarked this evening at supper that in the Castle there are places better left in peace. I took it then for a hostess's ordinary jest. Now, however..."

"Now, however," the monk gently picked up, "you begin to suspect that she was not joking."

I nodded, unable to force out a word.

"Tell me one thing," said the monk, and his fingers on the rosary froze. "What year is it now? In your opinion."

"What do you mean, what year?" I even smiled at the absurdity of the question. "It is now..."

And fell silent.

I knew the answer. I ought to have known it. I had known it even at supper, when the General spoke of the Porte, when Kutasova spoke of Constantinople - the year was somewhere there, in every word, in every conversation, perfectly obvious, not requiring pronouncement. And now, when I was asked directly, in its place was that same even, smooth emptiness as in the place of Lizonka's patronymic. As though someone had pulled out that thread too.

"I... cannot remember," I said. And heard my voice tremble.

The monk nodded - slowly, without surprise, as though he had received precisely the answer he expected.

"It is nothing," he said quietly. "It happens here. Here years lie upon one another like leaves in a book. It is hard to say which page you are reading now."

As I spoke, his face - until then benevolent and open - slowly but inexorably changed: whether with anxiety, or pity, or that special expression with which one looks at a man not yet informed of the misfortune that has befallen him. He sighed - briefly, almost inaudibly - and for a short instant looked aside into the garden, as though gathering his courage or, perhaps, checking one last time whether there was not another, less cruel way to say what he was about to say.

It occurred to me in that second - absurdly, irrelevantly - of the old mirror in the niche, of the hand that had lagged behind me by a heartbeat, of the great-great-grandfather's gaze that had seemingly shifted after my movement, of the portrait under crape with two little stones in his hand, of the tracks in the dust breaking off in the middle of the corridor. All these trifles, each of which taken singly was no more than a play of shadows and a tired imagination, suddenly lined up in my head into a single, inexplicable line leading straight to this moment.

"Brother," he said at last, softly, almost tenderly, as though softening by that tone the very essence of what he was about to say, "this castle has been abandoned and empty for more than a hundred years."

The earth swayed beneath me before I could grasp even a word of that phrase. My head spun swiftly, ruthlessly; thoughts that a second ago were trying to arrange themselves into the semblance of a question scattered in a disorderly, dazzling whirl; my eyes darkened, as if someone with one movement had drawn a thick black curtain before me - and I fell, without even feeling the pain of striking the grass.

The last thing my memory retained of that night - or that day, I no longer knew - was the monk's concentrated but by no means frightened face, rushing swiftly toward me, and his outstretched hand, frozen halfway between us, like the last bridge connecting me with the world which, it seemed, I had just managed to lose irretrievably. And somewhere very far away, barely distinguishable, as though from behind a thick wall - a voice, calling out my name.

It seemed to me it was Konstantin's voice.
