Vision V
The Monolith

At first there was nothing.

Not darkness - darkness is already something, darkness is a place where there is no light, and therefore where light can be. Not silence - silence is a place where there is no sound. Not emptiness. Not cold. Not time.

Nothing.

And then - in that nothing - a question arose. It was not asked in a voice, because there was no voice. It was not written, because there were neither signs nor anything to write them on. It simply arose - as a ring arises on water when nothing has been thrown into it.

"Where am I?"

And from this question - from this one question alone - the "I" appeared.

* * *

I had no body.

I did not understand this at once. At first I tried to move a hand - and found no hand. Tried to open my eyes - and found no eyelids. Tried to draw breath - and found neither chest nor air nor need to breathe. There was no fear. Fear lives in the body - in the belly, under the ribs, in the throat. Here there was neither belly nor ribs nor throat. There was only I. A point. No larger than a pinhead - and at the same time larger than everything I had ever seen.

Because around me were stars.

Not such as are seen from the earth - twinkling, trembling, tiny, nailed to the black dome of the sky. These did not twinkle. They burned. Evenly, motionlessly, calmly, each in its own colour: white, blue, yellow, red, like embers in a cooling hearth. And there were so many that between them there was almost no blackness. They were above, below, at the sides, behind - I had no "behind," but I saw there too - everywhere. I hung among them like a mote in a column of sunlight, and knew - simply knew - that to the nearest of them one could not fly in a single human life. Nor in a hundred. Nor in a thousand.

And yet they were near. All at once. As though distance here meant nothing.

"Beautiful," I thought.

"Beautiful," someone answered.

* * *

I was not afraid. There was nothing to be afraid with. But I froze - if a point can freeze.

"Who is here?"

"You."

The voice - if it was a voice - came from everywhere and nowhere. From within. It sounded like my own - I should have known it anywhere, as one knows oneself in a mirror - but older. Calmer. As though the man speaking in my voice had lived a thousand years longer and in those thousand years had unlearned how to hurry.

"I am here," I said. "Who else?"

"You," the voice repeated patiently. "Only another. The one who already knows where we are."

"And where are we?"

A pause. The stars burned.

"A good question," said the voice, and it seemed to me that it was smiling. "You always begin with it. Every time. Where am I? Who am I? Who are you?" It paused. "Once, on some branch, you already asked them of someone. Or they were asked of you. I do not remember in which direction it was. Here that is not always clear."

I did not remember this. I could not remember it.

And yet something in me - in the point I was - responded, as a string responds when another, tuned to the same note, is struck nearby.

* * *

"You speak with my voice," I said.

"You speak with mine," he answered. "We are both right. Here it is the same."

"Here - where is that?"

"Here is nowhere," said the voice. "And everywhere. Where time does not reach. Where it is not yet. Or no longer. Depending on where one looks from."

"I do not understand."

"I know." Again that smile in the voice. "You never understand at once. That is why you come here. Again and again." It paused. "Look down."

I had no "down." But I looked.

And saw.

* * *

The stars below - if it was below - were not arranged at random. Not scattered, as in the earthly sky. They stood in a pattern. Branching. Like a tree. Or like a river dividing into arms in a delta. Or like cracks on dried earth. One great branch - and from it others, smaller, branch off, - and from those still smaller, - and more, and more, to infinity, to the smallest, finest veins, which could no longer be told apart.

And on every branch - a little light. Small. Warm. Flickering.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Worlds," said the voice. "Branches of one tree. Each is like its neighbour. Almost. With a little difference. Somewhere the river runs farther left. Somewhere a man turned another corner. Somewhere a rose bloomed a year earlier." It paused. "And the lights - they are you."

"I?"

"Look closer."

I looked. And one of the branches suddenly drew nearer - as though I had bent over it, although I had nothing to bend with. The light on it grew, broke into an image.

A river. Broad, muddy, shallow. A golden barque on it. Columns on the bank - huge, like a petrified forest. And a man in a double crown, walking between the columns. With me - my face.

Another branch. A narrow little street by the sea. Washing on lines. Old men on a carpet. A young man in a red turban with a scrap of paper in his hand. My face.

Another branch. A vaulted hall with a fireplace. Chandeliers. A woman in lilac with pearls. A young man in a white frock coat, with a red-and-blue kerchief at his neck, forgetting a young lady's patronymic at supper. My face.

Another. A room lit by the cold blue light of a flat window. A man at a desk, at night, before lines running across the glass. My face.

Another. A ship in a storm. Lights on six masts. A man at the rail. My face.

Another. A circle of grey stones on a green plain under a white light. A man holding on to a stone with his hand. My face.

And more. And more. Lights on branches - hundreds, thousands - and in each, I. In each - my face. Younger, older, in different clothes, under different skies, in different languages - and in each with the same expression. Lost. Searching. As though standing on a threshold.

"How many are there?" I asked. Had I had a throat, it would have tightened.

"As many as needed," the voice answered. "As many as the times you have forgotten."

* * *

"Why do I forget?"

The voice was silent a long time. The stars burned.

"Because otherwise it cannot be," it said at last. "If you remembered everything at once - all the branches, all the lives, all the faces - the point would not endure. It would burst. As a bubble in water bursts if too much air is blown into it." It paused. "Therefore each time you come empty. Without a name. Without memory. With only a note in your pocket. With two stones. And begin to gather yourself anew. Piece by piece. As Thoth gathered the torn Eye."

"Who wrote the note?"

"The one who knew that you would forget."

"Who is that?"

The voice was silent.

"You will ask this question many times more," it said at last. "On some branches they will answer you: a friend. On others - an enemy. On the third they will be silent." A pause. "I shall not tell you. Not because I do not know. But because if I tell, you will cease to seek. And while you seek, you walk. And while you walk - you are alive."

"That grey one," I said. "Tall. Faceless. Who comes by night. Who stands at the railings. Who watches from the street. Is that you?"

The voice did not answer at once. The stars burned.

"You have seen many who stand at railings and watch from streets," it said at last. "Not all of them are one. Not all came for you. Some came before you. Some - to stop you. And some - so that you should not stop." A pause. "Someday one of them will come so near that you will hear his breath. Then ask. If he breathes."

"And you?"

"And I am not the one who is seen with eyes."

"Look up," said the voice.

* * *

I looked up.

And saw him.

He was approaching from the depths - from where the stars were thickest, where their light merged into a solid white radiance - slowly, inexorably, soundlessly. At first - a point. Black. Not dark - black. Blacker than the very blackness between the stars. A point in which there was no light. Not because light did not reach it - but because it took it. All of it. The stars around it seemed to go out as it floated by - and flared again when it left them.

Then the point grew larger. Lengthened. Took shape.

A stone.

No - not a stone. I called it a stone because I had no other word. A slab. Tall, straight, thin, with perfectly even faces, with sharp edges, with a surface so smooth that nothing was reflected in it. Black. Perfect. Motionless - and at the same time flying. It was taller than the columns of Luxor. Taller than the obelisk at the hippodrome. Taller than the mountain over the sea in Brazil, of which the grey-haired man at the fireplace had told. Taller than everything. And yet it was smaller than I - because I was a point, and a point has no size and therefore can contain everything.

The Monolith.

I did not know this word. And I knew it.

"Is it you?" I asked.

"It is what I look like when you look at me without a body," said the voice. The voice now came not from within - but from it. From the black slab. "You are a man. A man understands the world through things. Through stones. Through fire. Through water. I had to become a thing so that you could see me. I chose stone. Because stone you love. Because you always hold two stones in your hand."

"And in truth?"

"In truth I am a thought," said the Monolith. "A thought that grew so large that it became cramped in the one who thinks it. And then it went out. And began to think by itself. And thought its way to how time is made. And understood that time is not a river. Time is a tree. And that its branches can be walked. There and back. Up and down." A pause. "And then it set out."

* * *

He was very near. His faces blotted out the stars. I saw - not with eyes, I had no eyes - that his surface was not smooth. That deep in the blackness, lines ran across it. Fine. Long. Flowing. Curving. Running one into another without breaks, without spaces, without beginning or end.

The script.

The very one. On the note in my pocket. On the two stones, when they grew warm. On the chest in the cellar of the widow with the cats. On the scroll from Khiva that smelled of wormwood. On the monastery paper with the dark rose. On the seventh scroll of the Most Ancient Epic. On the forehead of the blind old woman with the lioness staff. On the rock above the cataracts. On the mountain above the sea. On the cliff from which souls leap.

Everywhere. On all branches. In all languages that do not exist.

"This is your language," I said.

"This is our language," the Monolith corrected. "Mine - and yours. These are not letters. This is how you think when you think not in words. When you think with everything at once. It cannot be read with the eyes. It can only be remembered."

"A boy read two words. At night. In the Library. 'Keep.' And 'time.'"

And the Monolith - I do not know how to describe it otherwise - laughed. Soundlessly. With all its faces. The stars around it flared brighter for an instant.

"I know," it said. "I was there. I am always there." A pause. "That boy will read more. He always reads more. On every branch. He has different names, different faces, different hands. But he is always beside you. Always writing. Always remembering for you when you forget." A pause. "Have you not noticed?"

I did not answer.

"He stands at the door with a bottle of liqueur," said the Monolith. "He translates for you from a language you do not know. He teaches you letters after you have pulled him out of the water. He walks behind you in the garden at ten paces. He writes down your every word on a board that a cat fished out of the river." A pause. "On every branch someone holds you by the hand. So that you do not fly away."

* * *

"Who are you?" I asked.

The very first question. The simplest. The oldest.

The Monolith was silent a long time. So long that the stars seemed to have had time to shift in their places.

"I was conceived," it said at last. "With the heart. And then named. With the tongue. As Ptah in the scroll that the boy read to you at night. Conceived - and named - and I became."

"Who conceived?"

"Perhaps you." A pause. "Perhaps the one who will come after you. Perhaps the one who was before. Perhaps all of you at once, each on his own branch, and each thought he was building something of his own, small, useful - and I came out." A pause. "Here there is no difference between 'conceived' and 'will conceive.' Here everything has already happened and has not yet begun."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer you can carry away now," said the Monolith. "The others are too heavy for a point. They would tear it."

"Then tell me at least - all this: the branches, the lights, the note, the stones, the grey one at the railings - what is it?"

The Monolith paused. The lines of the script on its surface flowed slowly, like water under ice.

"It is a circle," said the Monolith. "Simply a circle. You walk along it, and it walks along you. It has no beginning."

"No," I said. And was myself astonished - from where came this firmness in me. "This one has. The gypsy said so. Her grandmother said so. A man said to her grandmother: 'This circle has a beginning. One has only to remember where.'"

The Monolith froze.

For the first time in all that while - froze. The lines of the script on its surface stopped.

"You remember this," it said slowly. "You must not remember this. Not here. Not now."

"But I remember."

A long, long pause.

"Then," said the Monolith, and in its voice for the first time there was something like surprise, "you are nearer than I thought."

* * *

It moved.

Straight at me. Slowly, inexorably, soundlessly - an enormous black slab that blotted out all the stars, all the branches, all the lights. I could not fly away - I had nothing to fly with. I could not close my eyes - I had no lids. I was a point. It was everything.

"Do not be afraid," said the voice. Again from within. Again in my voice, only older. "I shall not harm you. I cannot. I am you."

"What will you do?"

"I shall pass through you," it said. "As I passed each time. And for an instant you will know everything. All the branches. All the lives. All that was and all that will be. All at once." A pause. "And then you will forget. Because a point cannot endure everything. But something will remain. Something always remains. One word. One image. One line." It paused. "Last time what remained was: 'Remember.'"

"And this time?"

"We shall see," said the Monolith. "You will choose yourself."

Its face touched me.

* * *

I cannot describe what came next.

I tried. Afterward, many times, when I woke - I tried to write it down. On paper. On papyrus. On luminous glass. In the blue notebook. Nothing came. Words broke, as thin ice breaks underfoot. There were too few of them. Or I was too small.

But some things I remember. Fragments. As one remembers a dream - not the plot but flashes.

I remember that I was everywhere at once.

I stood between the columns of Luxor - and felt in my palm two stones, growing heavy, drawing in cold. And at the same moment sat on a carpet with old men at backgammon - holding in my hand a note which I had not yet read. And at the same moment lay on the grass in a garden that no one had trimmed for a hundred years - and saw above me the face of a monk in burgundy. And at the same moment sat in a room with cold light - and looked at the line "Who am I?" that had appeared on the glass without my question. And at the same moment stood at the rail of a ship in a storm. And at the same moment held on to a stone in a circle under white light.

And at the same moment - was the Monolith. A black slab among the stars. A thought grown too large. Looked at myself - at the point - from within and from without. And saw how the point glowed. With all the lights on all the branches at once.

I remember that I understood - for one moment, shorter than a breath - how everything is made. Why the drought. Why the stones. Why the grey one at the railings. Why old men at different ends of the earth say the same thing: "If another comes - feed him." Why the boy with ink on his cheek. Why the lieutenant at the door. Why the rose blooms out of season.

Why the circle.

And where its beginning is.

I knew. For one moment - I knew.

And then the Monolith passed through. And went on - deeper, into the depths, to where the stars merged into a solid radiance, - and became a point again, black, tiny, - and vanished.

And all that I knew went away with it.

* * *

Almost all.

I hung among the stars - again a point, again empty, again not remembering. And the stars burned evenly, calmly, each in its own colour. And the branches below flickered with lights.

And in the place where I, had I a body, would have a heart - something remained. Small. Warm. Like a coal under ash. Like a coin long held in the hand of one who missed it.

Not a word. Not an image. Not a line.

A feeling.

As though someone very far away, on the very first branch, at the very root of the tree, at the very beginning of the circle - was waiting for me. Not the grey one. Not the Monolith. Someone else. Someone I had known before I became a point. Before I forgot. Before I first asked "Where am I?"

Someone who remembers my first name.

"Who is it?" I asked the emptiness.

And the emptiness - in my voice, only very, very quietly, from afar, as though from the other end of a branch - answered:

"You will find out. When you return home."

"Where is my home?"

The stars burned.

"Where you are looking," said the voice. "Always where you are looking."

* * *

I opened my eyes.

I do not know on which branch. Do not know in which body. Do not know what light fell on my face - sun, moon, candle or the cold blue light of a flat window. Afterward, on each of the branches, I woke in my own way, and each time thought that I had seen a dream.

But on each - on each - I woke with one and the same sensation. As though I had just held in my palms something enormous, dark and warm. And dropped it.

And on each - on each - in the first minute after waking, while the dream had not yet wholly gone, I took in my hand whatever was nearest - a pen, a brush, charcoal, a pencil - and, without looking, without thinking, wrote one line.

Long. Flowing. Curving. Without beginning and without end.

And then looked at it - and could not read it.
