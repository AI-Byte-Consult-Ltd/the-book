Chapter VII
Ferhat and His Family

The room into which Akmal led me proved to be exactly as Ferhat had promised to describe it - and yet reality surpassed the promise. Small, whitewashed, with a low wooden ceiling crossed by dark beams, it kept a coolness astonishing for a house that had stood all day in the full sun. Against the far wall stood a chest of artful carving, a low divan spread with mattresses evidently served as a bed, and a narrow door in the corner led onto that very balcony that had been promised - tiny, scarcely holding one person, but with a view for which, it seemed, any cramping was worth enduring: roofs, minarets, a strip of water touched by evening gold.

On the windowsill stood a clay jug of water covered with a clean cloth, and beside it a copper bowl with rose petals floating on the surface of the water. For washing, I guessed. Someone had seen to this in advance, even before I climbed the stairs - which meant that in this house they had known I would stay before I knew it myself.

Akmal, thrusting his head into the room after me, said something - quickly, excitedly, accompanying the words with broad, almost theatrical gestures - and, receiving from me nothing but a bewildered smile, began to show with his hands still more insistently: first at himself, then at the door, then he mimed something remotely resembling the flapping of a sail or of wings, and at last triumphantly jabbed a finger toward the window, beyond which a strip of water could be made out.

"Ships?" I tried to guess, resorting to gestures too. "The port?"

Akmal's face lit up as though I had just solved the most difficult riddle, and he nodded with redoubled enthusiasm, flinging up his arms again - this time, it seemed, to something like "tomorrow" or "in the morning."

"Tomorrow," I repeated, nodding in tune with him, though I understood from the entire conversation scarcely a tenth. "Good. Tomorrow - ships."

He laughed - openly, ringingly - evidently pleased that he had found a common language with me where no common language, strictly speaking, existed at all. We both, I confess, proved fair actors: without having exchanged, it seemed, a single word comprehensible to the other, we had contrived to arrange tomorrow's outing with the same ease with which experienced traders strike a bargain at a market where no one asks anyone for papers proving knowledge of a foreign tongue.

But he had no intention of leaving. Instead he seized my sleeve and pulled me after him - into the corridor, to a narrow ladder leading somewhere up, right under the roof.

* * *

The attic proved dark, stuffy, smelling of dust, sun-warmed wood and something else - dry, herbal, like the smell of hay. Through the gaps in the tiles thin, string-like rays of the setting sun forced their way, and in them dust motes slowly circled. Along the walls were piled old chests, rolled carpets, stacks of copperware, a broken spinning wheel, fishing nets long unacquainted with water - the things that accumulate in every house over the years because one is sorry to throw them out and has nothing to use them for.

Akmal threaded his way among them with the agility of a cat, glancing back every now and then to see whether I had fallen behind. I had. Twice I struck my head on a beam, once I nearly overturned a copper basin, and each time Akmal burst out laughing, clapping a hand over his mouth.

By the wall, on a nail, hung an old leather belt with tools - carpenter's, judging by their look: a hammer with a shrunken handle, a chisel, an awl. And among them - a small leather pouch on a cord, worn, empty, with a half-effaced pattern embossed on the leather.

I stopped. I do not know why. The pouch was quite ordinary - the kind in which one carries nails, small coins, tobacco. But my hand reached for it of itself, and of itself, before I could think, felt in the pocket of my trousers for the two smooth stones.

Akmal noticed where I was looking, came over, took the pouch off its nail and held it out to me - simply, without any ceremony, as if offering an apple. Then he pointed a finger at my pocket where the stones lay, then at the pouch - and mimed one being put into the other.

"You want me to... This is for me?"

Akmal nodded and said something, pointing at the belt of tools and then at the sky - upward, where beyond the tiles was the evening sky. The gesture was clear without words: the owner of these tools had long ceased to need them.

"Whose belt is this?" I asked, forgetting that he did not understand me.

But he seemed to understand - by the intonation, by my glance at the belt. He fell silent - for the first time all evening Akmal was serious - and said one word which I did not understand, but which sounded as the same words sound in all languages:

"Baba."

Father.

I held the pouch in my hands for a long time. Then I transferred both stones into it - black and white - and drew the cord tight. The stones lay in it so snugly, so exactly, as though the pouch had been sewn for them. I tied it to my belt. Akmal clapped me approvingly on the shoulder - and at once began smiling again, as though a minute before there had been no such serious, grown-up silence.

"Güvercin!" he blurted out and dragged me on, to a dormer window at the far end of the attic.

* * *

The dormer window gave onto the flat roof of the neighbouring shed, and there on the roof stood a dovecote - knocked together of boards, with a dozen round entrances, once painted blue, now peeling. Akmal clambered out, I followed, clumsily, on all fours, to the sound of his stifled laughter.

The sun was setting. The whole city, as far as the eye could reach, lay before us - tiled roofs descending in stairways to the water, domes, minarets, thin threads of smoke above hearths, the masts of ships in the port, resembling from here thickets of reed. And on the roofs - everywhere, on dozens of roofs - stood similar dovecotes, and beside many of them stood boys, just like Akmal, with long poles in their hands.

Akmal opened the dovecote door, stuck two fingers in his mouth and whistled - piercingly, with trills - and from the entrances, beating their wings, a flock burst out: white, blue-grey, speckled, with feathered feet, with tails spread like fans. They soared upward, made a circle above the roof, another, a third, climbing ever higher, and the sun flared on their wings in pink and gold.

And at once, from the neighbouring roofs, other flocks took off. The boys whistled, waved their poles, shouted to one another - and in the sky above the city dozens of flocks of pigeons wheeled, meeting and parting, mingling and breaking apart again, as if someone invisible were weaving in the sky an enormous living carpet.

"Ahmet!" Akmal shouted to a neighbour boy two roofs away, pointing at one of the flocks. "Benim! Benim!"

"Değil!" the other answered. "Benim!"

I did not understand the words, but I understood the game: the flocks mingled, and each boy tried to lure the others' birds into his own dovecote. Whoever lured more - won. Akmal whistled like one possessed, swung his pole, jumped on the spot so that the shed's roof creaked threateningly.

When the flock at last returned, Akmal triumphantly counted the birds at the entrances - and with a wild shriek of delight showed me two fingers. Two strange pigeons. He had lured two strange pigeons.

"Well done," I said, and he seemed to understand.

We sat on the edge of the roof, legs dangling, and watched the sky fade above the city. Somewhere far off, from the highest of the minarets, a voice rang out - long-drawn, high, flowing, as if pouring from the sky. A second answered it, from another minaret, then a third, a fourth - and soon above the whole city, above the roofs and dovecotes, above the water and the ships, a many-voiced call floated, answering itself, which sent shivers down my spine.

Akmal grew still, lowered his head, his lips moving soundlessly.

I did not know what this call was. I did not know the words, did not know the meaning. But I sat on the edge of the roof, listening to the voices answer one another above the darkening city, and felt something inside me - something long, very long clenched into a tight knot - loosen for a moment. As though someone had called me by name. By that very name which I did not remember.

* * *

A little later, when Akmal had run downstairs - judging by the voices that reached me, to help in the kitchen - I went out on my little balcony and found there, at the neighbouring window of the first floor, the second elder, who had been introduced to me as Yusuf. He sat surrounded by some ledgers, turning the counters of a plain wooden abacus, and, seeing me, nodded curtly - whether greeting me or simply noting my presence as a fact requiring no further attention.

Yusuf, as I had already managed to notice at the backgammon, was a far more reserved man than old Suleyman - the one who had conducted the conversation over tea with the same unhurried thoroughness with which, no doubt, he had conducted it for more than a decade. Yusuf, it seemed, was responsible in this house for more practical matters: accounts, trade, the distribution of duties among the numerous nephews and grandsons. While I watched him from the balcony, the youngest of the brothers, Kemal, ran up to him - the same quiet boy who on the first day had silently drawn with charcoal on the flagstones of the yard - and, judging by the intonation, began reporting something, pointing now toward the port, now toward the house. Yusuf listened to him with the same impassivity, put, it seemed, one single clarifying question and dismissed the boy with a short nod, at once returning to his accounts.

I went down into the yard. Yusuf, without raising his head, moved one of the ledgers aside, making room on the bench beside him. The gesture was barely noticeable, but unmistakable. I sat down.

He clicked the counters - quickly, evenly, without looking - and recorded the results in the ledger in small, neat signs. Columns of figures stretched down the page. I looked at them, not understanding the signs, but for some reason understanding the numbers themselves - just as in the morning I had understood the sense of words by intonation. On one of the columns my gaze suddenly stumbled.

I could not have explained how I knew it. I did not know the local numerals. But I looked at the column and knew - simply knew - that the total at the bottom was wrong. That somewhere in the middle one number had been carried wrongly.

I laid my finger on the page beside that number.

Yusuf raised his eyes to me - for the first time all evening truly. Then he slowly recounted the column on the abacus - once, twice. He clicked the counters. Frowned. Took the pen, crossed out the total and wrote a new one.

He was silent for a long time. Then, without a word, he reached into a canvas pouch at his belt, took out a date - large, dark, glossy - and laid it in my palm. And buried himself in his books again.

I ate the date. It was sweet as honey. We sat so for another quarter of an hour - he clicked the counters, I watched - and I found no more errors. But when I rose to leave, Yusuf, without raising his head, pushed another date toward me.

* * *

By the yard wall, in the shade under an awning, sat Orhan. Before him on a cloth lay a heap of walnuts, and he was cracking them - one after another, with the haft of a heavy knife - with the concentrated fury with which one cracks not nuts but someone's heads.

I stopped a few paces away. He raised his eyes to me, and in them there was not a drop of hospitality.

I sat down opposite, on my haunches. I took a nut from the heap, turned it in my fingers. Then I took a second, laid both on my palm and squeezed - one against the other, as one squeezes in a fist. The shell of one cracked.

Orhan looked at me without blinking. Then he snorted, took two nuts, laid them on his palm, squeezed - both cracked at once. He looked at me defiantly.

I took three. Squeezed. One cracked.

Orhan laughed - briefly, reluctantly, as if the laughter had burst out of him against his will. He took four nuts. Squeezed. His face turned crimson, the veins on his neck swelled. Three cracked.

I raised my hands - I surrender.

He snorted again, but this was now quite a different sound. He pushed half the heap of cracked nuts toward me. I took one, ate it. He took another. We ate in silence. Then Orhan suddenly said something - short, gruff - pointing a finger first at me, then toward the street, then miming a seizing of a hand.

Mehmet. The little thief. He was speaking of Mehmet.

I nodded. Orhan shook his head - with vexation, but already without anger - and said something which, by the intonation, meant something like "for three days I ran after him, and you - with one hand." Then he spat aside, wiped his knife on his trouser leg and went into the house.

The nuts he left to me.

* * *

Ferhat found me on the balcony already at dusk - rested, washed, in a clean shirt and with a canvas bag over his shoulder. In an hour he had to leave for the port, for the night shift.

"Well?" he asked, leaning on the railing beside me. "Were you not bored?"

"I was given no chance to be bored," I said. "Akmal showed me the attic, the dovecote and all the pigeons of this city. Yusuf fed me dates. Orhan - walnuts."

Ferhat raised his eyebrows in astonishment.

"Orhan? Walnuts?" He whistled. "You are either a sorcerer or a saint. He does not give me walnuts myself."

"We competed at who could crack more in his fist."

"And who won?"

"He."

"Then it is understandable," laughed Ferhat. "Orhan forgives everyone he wins against."

He was silent for a while, looking at the darkening city. Below, in the yard, an oil lamp had been lit, and from behind a thick curtain on the women's side came singing - a low, slightly cracked old voice drawing out a slow, sad melody that made my chest ache.

"Grandmother," said Ferhat quietly. "She sings every evening. The same song. About a sailor who went to sea and did not return."

"That is a sad song."

"Sad." Ferhat nodded. "Father went to sea when I was ten. Akmal was four, Kemal two. The ship was going to Crete. It did not arrive. Since then Grandmother sings that song every evening. And Grandfather..." He paused. "Grandfather since then never lets a stranger go from his threshold hungry. He says: if you feed a wanderer in your house - somewhere far away someone will feed yours. Perhaps Father is alive. Perhaps he is somewhere on a foreign shore, and someone is pouring him tea right now. Grandfather believes it."

I was silent for a long time. The pouch at my belt suddenly seemed heavy to me.

"Akmal gave me a pouch," I said at last. "In the attic. From the belt with the tools. He said... 'baba.'"

Ferhat looked at the pouch on my belt - and his face changed for a moment. Then he smiled - brightly, without bitterness.

"Father was a ship's carpenter," he said. "That belt is all that remains of him. He forgot it at home that time. Grandmother does not allow it to be touched." He paused. "But if Akmal gave you the pouch - it means it was meant to be. Akmal sometimes knows such things. Like Kemal with his drawings."

"I shall return it," I said. "If Grandmother..."

"No need," Ferhat interrupted gently. "Father would have given it. He always gave everything away." And, after a pause, he added, looking no longer at me but at the water, at the lights of the ships: "Perhaps that is the very thing. You are a stranger on our shore. We feed you. And somewhere, perhaps, they are feeding him."

From below, from behind the curtain, Grandmother's singing still came. Ferhat listened to it a little longer, then shook his head as if shaking something off his shoulders, and began smiling again.

"I must go. The port does not wait. Kemal will show you the library if you wish. It is mostly Grandfather's books, in the old language, you will not be able to read them. But there are maps. Everyone reads maps."

* * *

The library in this house was a small room on the first floor, with one window and walls lined from floor to ceiling with shelves. The books stood on them tightly, spine to spine - thick, in leather bindings, with embossed Arabic lettering; scrolls tied with cords; stacks of yellowed sheets sewn by hand. It smelled of old paper, leather and wax.

Kemal lit a lamp, set it on a low table and, without a word, took from a lower shelf an enormous folio swollen with age - an atlas. He opened it before me at a spread where water was drawn in blue paint, cut by shores, and put a finger on one point - where a narrow strait joined two seas.

"Burası," he said softly. Here.

I looked at the map. At the narrow blue strip of the strait. At the two seas on either side of it. At the city sprawling along both shores - Europe and Asia, I understood suddenly, though no one had told me so. Two worlds, divided by a strip of water along which boats scurry.

Two worlds. For some reason those words echoed in me with the same strange echo as the call from the minarets.

Kemal, while I looked at the map, moved away into the corner, to a shelf stacked with piles of paper. He rummaged in them, took out several sheets and, after hesitating, laid them before me on the atlas.

Drawings. In charcoal, on coarse paper. Akmal with pigeons - alive, laughing, a pole in his hand. Suleyman over the backgammon, with a narrowed eye. Yusuf with the abacus - lips pursed, brows knit, ludicrously like. A ship sailing out to sea - small, lonely, on enormous water.

And the last drawing.

A narrow lane between two houses - I recognised it at once: the very one visible from my balcony, running off into the darkness of an alley. And at the end of the lane, in shadow - a figure. Tall, immoderately tall, taller than a doorway. Drawn not in outline but in solid grey hatching, as if moulded of ash. Without a face.

I raised my eyes to Kemal. He looked at me gravely, unblinking.

"When?" I asked, pointing at the drawing. "When did you draw this?"

He did not understand the words. But he seemed to understand the question. He held up two fingers. Then he pointed at the window - at the sky, where the first stars had already lit - and laid his palms together under his cheek, as though sleeping.

Two nights ago. He had drawn this two nights ago.

Before I came to this house. Before I had even found myself in this city - as far as I remembered, at least.

Kemal gathered up the drawings, folded them neatly and put them back on the shelf - all but the last. That one he left on the atlas before me. Then he put out the lamp, touched my shoulder - as lightly as in the morning he had touched the white stone - and went away, leaving me alone in the darkness that smelled of old paper.

* * *

I was still standing afterward on the balcony, observing this small, harmonious mechanism of a stranger's family, when behind my back light footsteps sounded - whether of one of the women of the house, whom by custom I was not supposed to see, or of one of the boys - and on the threshold of the room appeared a tray with supper: flat bread, something stewed in a clay pot, smelling of mutton and of spices so unfamiliar that I would not undertake to list them even approximately. I turned - and managed to see only a hand setting the tray on the floor: swarthy, narrow, with a henna pattern on the palm and a thin silver bracelet on the wrist. The hand vanished behind the door before I could thank it.

I supped alone, sitting on the low divan - and, I confess, this silence after the noisy, crowded evening in a strange family had a soothing effect on me, like a long-awaited pause between two acts of a play which I watched without understanding a word, but somehow catching the whole sense. Kemal's drawing lay beside me on the divan, face down. I could not bring myself to turn it over again.

This family, I thought, was ordered far more intricately than it had seemed at first glance over tea on the motley carpet: each one here, the boys included, had his own role, his own place in the invisible hierarchy of the house - Suleyman guarded tradition and hospitality, Yusuf ran the household, Ferhat alone of all spoke with the outside world in its own language, Orhan guarded the house from strangers, Akmal knew what to give to whom, and Kemal - saw what others did not see. And Grandmother every evening sang a song about a man who did not return.

It grew dark quickly, as it usually does in southern port cities - without long twilight, in one decisive movement of a curtain. I went out on the balcony one last time before sleep to breathe the evening air, already cooling, smelling of the sea and of hearth smoke - and it was then, at the very edge of vision, where the narrow lane below was lost in the darkness between two houses, that I saw that figure again.

Grey - not in its clothes, but as if wholly moulded of ash and evening dusk - standing motionless, looking, as it seemed to me this time quite distinctly, straight at my window. Standing exactly where it stood in Kemal's drawing.

My heart missed a beat. I blinked, peered - and the figure did not vanish at once, as last time on the square, but lingered another second, two, three, as though deliberately giving me time to be sure it was no trick of vision, no play of shadows - and only then unhurriedly, almost lazily withdrew into the darkness of the alley and dissolved in it without trace.

The pouch at my belt, it seemed, had grown a little warmer - but the evening was close, and I paid it no heed.

I stood on the balcony a long time after that, peering at the place where it had just been, and feeling a cold slowly but surely spread down my back that had nothing to do with the evening cool. And from below, from behind the curtain, ever quieter and quieter, Grandmother sang - of the sailor who went to sea and did not return.
