Vision IV
The Circle

I do not know where I came from to that village.

That is the first thing worth saying - and, perhaps, the only thing I am sure of. I remember the road: white, chalky, dusty, leading up a gentle green slope. I remember the stagecoach that set me down at the crossroads, and the coachman who, having taken his fare, long looked after me and crossed himself. I remember the smell - of mown grass, of sheep's wool, of rain that had passed an hour ago and promised to return. But where I boarded that coach, and why, and who I was to be riding in it with one travelling bag - that I do not remember. As I did not remember at the very beginning of all my other journeys either.

The village was called Amesbury. So it was written on the peeling wooden signboard of the inn where I was given a room under the very roof - with a bed on which every spring creaked, with a window that looked out on a river, narrow, dark, unhurried, overgrown with willows, and with a view - there, beyond the river, beyond the fields, on the horizon, on the ridge of the chalk plain - of something grey, uneven, barely discernible in the haze. As though someone had set stone teeth along the edge of the world.

"The stones," said the landlady, noticing where I was looking. "Everyone looks that way. They come, they look, they go. You too, sir? For the solstice?"

"The solstice?"

The landlady - Mrs Hobbs, stout, rosy, in a frilled cap and an apron on which flour mingled with beer stains - threw up her hands so that a little white cloud fell from her.

"Lord, sir, don't you know? The day after tomorrow is the longest day of the year! The shortest dawn! The sun rises right over the Heel Stone and shines into the very middle of the circle! All the madmen of England gather to see it." She lowered her voice. "This year there are especially many. A learned man from Oxford. Gypsies. A young lady with some machine on a tripod. Our vicar goes about blacker than a thundercloud - says it's paganism. And my husband says, paganism or not, the lodgers pay promptly and drink their beer, and thank you very much."

She set a jug of water on the table, adjusted the curtain and, already in the doorway, turned back:

"Only don't go there at night, sir. Especially alone. People say you mustn't count there."

"Count?"

"Count the stones," said Mrs Hobbs in perfect earnest. "Whoever counts them twice and gets the same number will die. And whoever gets a different one will go mad. And whoever doesn't count at all never came here." She paused. "My late father-in-law counted. Three times. Different each time. Died at ninety-six, in his own bed, but to his dying day swore that the stones move from place to place at night." She tittered. "Well, he drank too. Good night, sir. Supper at seven. Kidney pie."

* * *

Downstairs, in the common room of the inn, by seven o'clock there had gathered a company that any novelist would have thought too motley to be plausible.

At a large oak table by the fireplace, heaped with papers, drawings, rulers, compasses and something resembling a brass spyglass without lenses, sat a man whom I at once christened to myself the Owl. About sixty, bald as a billiard ball, with a fringe of white down above the ears and such luxuriant grey whiskers that they reached almost to his chin. On his nose sat spectacles, over them yet another pair, and a third stuck out of the breast pocket of his tweed jacket. He was calculating something furiously, moving his lips, and from time to time exclaiming "Incredible!" or "It can't be!", each time striking the table with his fist so that the compasses leapt.

"Professor Erasmus Holloway," whispered Mrs Hobbs, setting a pie before me. "From Oxford. Came to measure the stones. Third week he's been measuring. Says the ancient people were astronomers. The vicar says the ancient people were savages. They've nearly come to blows twice already."

At the next table, straight as a pole, in a black frock coat and white collar, sat the vicar himself - the Reverend Silas Whitcombe. Thin, pale, with pursed lips and an expression as though the whole world were personally guilty before him. He ate his pie in small, reproachful bites and from time to time cast at the professor glances capable of freezing the Thames.

In the corner, by the window, on a bench, sat a young woman in a dark green travelling dress, with short, almost boyish hair sticking out from under a small hat - a thing unheard of for a respectable lady - and with hands black to the wrists, as though she had been rummaging in soot. Beside her on the floor stood a bulky wooden box on a tripod, wrapped in black cloth, and several smaller boxes from which came something sharp, apothecary-like - ether.

"Miss Ada Lovell," Mrs Hobbs went on whispering. "A photographer. From London. Alone! Without a companion! Her hands are black from those chemicals of hers - says it's silver. The vicar says it's indecent. She says what's indecent is his sermons." The landlady tittered. "I like her. She pays for her beer in advance."

And by the very fireplace, on a three-legged stool, leaning on a long shepherd's crook, sat an old man - so old, wrinkled and weathered that he seemed hewn from the same grey stone that stood on the ridge of the plain. In a rough canvas smock, in an old felt hat, with a white beard to his waist, he smoked a clay pipe and looked into the fire, paying no attention to anyone. At his feet, curled up, lay a shaggy black-and-white dog.

"And that is old Jedediah," said Mrs Hobbs, very quietly, almost with reverence. "A shepherd. All his life he's pastured sheep on the plain, right by the stones. They say he's ninety. Or a hundred. No one knows. He doesn't seem to know himself. He knows everything about the stones. Only tells no one." She paused. "And today he came himself. For the first time in ten years. Sat down by the fire and sits. As though waiting for someone."

* * *

Professor Holloway noticed me after about five minutes. He raised his head from the drawings, looked at me over two pairs of spectacles - and his eyes flashed with that particular hungry gleam with which the eyes of scholars flash on finding a fresh victim.

"You!" he exclaimed, jabbing a compass at me. "You have come for the solstice? Excellent! Excellent! Sit down! You must see this! No one in this village listens to me!"

To refuse was senseless. I moved over to him.

"Look!" He unrolled before me an enormous sheet covered with circles, lines, angles and figures. "Here is the circle. The sarsens - the big stones. Here - the bluestones, the small ones, inside. Here the horseshoe of trilithons. And here - the axis!" He drew a ruler along the plan. "The axis of the whole structure is directed exactly - exactly, sir! - at the point of sunrise on the day of the summer solstice. Through the Heel Stone. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the builders knew where the sun rises?"

"That the builders were astronomers!" cried the professor, striking the table with his fist. "Scholars! They built a calendar! A stone calendar! Four thousand years ago! When in Egypt they were only just building the pyramids!"

At the word "Egypt" something in me trembled. Columns. Sand. Heat. I blinked - and the vision went.

"Nonsense," said the Reverend Whitcombe in an icy voice from the next table, not raising his eyes from his pie. "These stones were set up by the Druids for their vile pagan sacrifices. Or by the Romans. Or - most probably - by the Evil One himself, as the tradition has it."

"Druids!" the professor turned purple. "The Druids lived two thousand years later! The Romans - three! And the Evil One, Reverend, so far as I know, is not strong in trigonometry!"

"The Evil One is strong in all that leads the soul away from God," the vicar snapped. "Including your trigonometry."

They looked at each other across the aisle like two cocks across a fence. Mrs Hobbs behind the bar rolled her eyes. Miss Lovell in the corner laughed soundlessly, covering her mouth with a palm black from silver.

"And do you know the tradition, sir?" the vicar suddenly asked, turning to me. "About the Heel Stone?"

"No."

"The Evil One," said the vicar, savouring each word, "bought these stones from an old woman in Ireland. He paid her as many coins as she could count while he carried the stones. And he carried them in an instant. The old woman managed to say only 'one.' And the Evil One set the stones here on the plain and boasted: 'No one will ever know where they came from!' And a friar hiding behind a stone cried out: 'That is what you think!' The Evil One seized the largest stone and hurled it at the friar. Hit him on the heel. Ever since, the stone has been called the Heel Stone." The vicar triumphantly sipped from his mug. "The friar, mark you, survived. Because he was under God's protection."

"And in my opinion," a ringing woman's voice suddenly came from the corner, "that story was made up by the monks. So as not to admit that they have no idea who really hauled those stones."

Miss Lovell rose, came up to our table and without the slightest embarrassment sat down on a free chair.

"There is another legend," she said, looking at me with mocking grey eyes. "About Merlin. King Arthur's wizard. That these stones once stood in Ireland, on a mountain, and were called the Giants' Dance. And the giants brought them from Africa - because there was a power of healing in the stones. If you poured water over a stone and then washed a sick man with that water - he would recover. And Merlin moved them here, to England - not with his hands but with a song. Or with machines." She winked at the professor. "Depending on who is retelling it."

"From Africa," I repeated slowly.

"From Africa," Miss Lovell nodded. "The giants - from Africa. The stones - from Ireland. The wizard - from Wales. The sun - from the sky." She shrugged. "Here everything is from somewhere. Nothing is from here. Even you, as I see."

"Me?"

"You have the face of a man who has come from everywhere at once," said Miss Lovell. "I photograph people. I know faces. Yours does not hold one expression longer than a second. As if three men lived in it by turns." She tilted her head, examining me as she must examine through the lens of her box. "May I photograph you? The day after tomorrow. At dawn. At the stones."

"Why?"

"Because," she said simply, "I am curious which of the three will come out on the plate."

* * *

Old Jedediah by the fire had not stirred all this time. But when the conversation died down - when the professor had buried himself in his drawings again, the vicar in his pie, and Miss Lovell had gone to the bar for another mug of beer - the shepherd suddenly took the pipe out of his mouth and, without turning his head, said in a low, hoarse voice like the creak of old wood:

"You. Come here."

The room went quiet. Mrs Hobbs behind the bar dropped her towel.

I went over. Crouched by the fireplace, beside the dog, which raised its head, sniffed my hand - and suddenly licked it, briefly and warmly, as one licks one's master.

"Shep knows you," said the old man, glancing sideways at the dog. "Shep licks no one. Not even me." He drew on his pipe. "Want a riddle? An old one. My grandfather set it to me. And his grandfather to him. And that one - I don't know who. Maybe the one who set up the stones."

"I do."

Jedediah let out smoke and spoke slowly, in a singsong, in the old local dialect, which I understood with difficulty, but understood:

"'Giants stand in a ring, holding hands. Above - crossbeams, below - grass. They are counted, and cannot be counted. At night they walk, by day they stand. Once a year one of them opens an eye. What does it see?'"

I thought for a long time. The fire crackled. The dog laid its muzzle on my knee.

"The sun," I said at last. "Once a year, at the solstice, the sun rises over the Heel Stone and shines into the circle. As if an eye opens."

Jedediah was silent a long while. Then slowly shook his head.

"The answer is the sun," he said. "Everyone answers so. The professor answered so. The girl with the box answered so. Everyone." He took out the pipe and jabbed it at me. "But my grandfather said: wrong. The eye opens not to see the sun. The sun it sees every day anyway. The eye opens to see the one who has come. Once a year. Or once in a hundred years. Or once in a thousand." He stuck the pipe back in his mouth. "Grandfather said: the stones were set up not for the sun. The stones were set up for the guest. So that he would know where to come."

"What guest?"

"The one who comes from above," said Jedediah simply. "And leaves from above. And each time does not remember that he has been before."

A chill ran down my back - that same one, long familiar, from under the ribs, on the left.

"Did your grandfather see him?" I asked.

"My grandfather - no." The old man paused. "But I did."

"When?"

"Seventy years ago. I was twenty. I was pasturing sheep by the stones on the night of the solstice. Alone. Shep was another one - also Shep, all our dogs are Sheps." He stroked the dog's head. "And toward morning a man came. From the side where there is no road. Young. In strange clothes. Stood in the middle of the circle. And waited. And when the sun rose - from above, right over him, a light opened. White. Not sunlight. And there was a sound - as though the very earth hummed. And heat." Jedediah closed his eyes. "And then - nothing. Sun, grass, sheep. And the man gone."

"Perhaps you dreamed it?"

"Perhaps," the old man agreed. "Only Shep - the earlier one - howled for three days after. And on the spot where the man stood, the grass did not grow for ten years. A round patch. Like this." He spread his hands. "The professor says it's chalk. The vicar says the Evil One. And I say - the guest."

He fell silent. Then, not looking at me, he added:

"He had your face, lad. I have remembered it seventy years. Yours."

* * *

That night I did not sleep. I lay on the creaking bed, listened to the willows by the river rustling outside the window, and looked at the ceiling over which moon shadows slid. Somewhere below a door banged. Somewhere a horse neighed.

Toward morning there was a soft knock at my door.

On the threshold stood a woman - short, swarthy, with jet-black hair braided in two thick plaits, twined with red ribbons and strings of small coins that jingled at every movement. She wore a motley dress, a red kerchief, copper rings on her fingers, huge earrings in her ears. She might have been forty, and might have been seventy - impossible to guess: her face was young, and her eyes ancient.

"Zora," she said before I could ask. "A gypsy. The camp is across the river. I have come to tell your fortune."

"I did not call you."

"I know." She smiled, flashing a gold tooth. "No one ever calls me. I come to those to whom I must." She held out her hand palm upward. "Give me your hand. Don't be afraid. I will take no money. From such as you, one doesn't take."

I gave her my hand. I do not know why.

She took it in hers - dry, hot, laden with rings - turned it to the light from the window and looked for a long time. Was silent. Moved a finger. Then raised her eyes to me - and in them there was neither cunning nor mockery.

"There is no life line," she said quietly.

"I have been told," I said. And was astonished myself - who told me? Where? I did not remember. But I knew that someone had.

"There is no life line," Zora repeated. "But there is something else. Here." She drew a nail across my palm, in a circle. "A ring. A line that goes round and comes to where it set out from. I have seen such once. My grandmother showed me. She told the fortune of a man who had such a ring. Long ago. Here. At the stones."

"Who was he?"

"Grandmother did not know." Zora let go of my hand. "Grandmother said to him: 'You walk in a circle, handsome. A hundred times you came, a hundred times you went. And each time you forget.' He laughed. Asked: 'And how can I stop forgetting?' Grandmother said..." Zora paused. "Grandmother told him a riddle. I will tell you the same."

She leaned toward me so close that the coins in her plaits jingled right by my ear.

"'What must one do to get out of the circle?'"

I thought. For a long time.

"Break it?"

"No," said Zora. "You break it - you will walk a new circle."

"Stop?"

"No. You stop - the circle will go on without you."

"Then I don't know."

Zora straightened. Smiled - sadly.

"Grandmother did not know the answer either," she said. "But that man told her the answer. On leaving. He said: 'One must remember where the beginning is.'" She shrugged. "Grandmother said - a circle has no beginning. So there is no getting out. And he laughed and said: 'This one has.'"

She went to the door. Stopped.

"The day after tomorrow, at dawn," she said without turning, "do not stand in the middle. Stand at the edge. By a stone. Hold on to the stone. Perhaps then they will not take you." She paused. "Or perhaps they will. As they took grandmother's."

And left, jingling her coins.

* * *

The whole of the next day I spent at the stones.

Up close they proved bigger than I could have imagined - grey, rough, eroded by the winds, overgrown with yellow and white lichen like ancient old men with beards. Some stood. Some lay, fallen, as if tired of standing. On some, at a height of three men, lay stone crossbeams - just as huge - and I could not understand how people who had neither machines nor steam nor iron had managed to raise them there.

Professor Holloway darted among the stones with rulers and plumb lines like a maddened bumblebee, shouting figures which were written down after him by a boy hired in the village - flaxen-haired, freckled, forever open-mouthed. Miss Lovell set up her box on its tripod by the Heel Stone, paced out the distance, squinted at the sky and murmured something about light. The Reverend Whitcombe stood apart, by the road, arms folded on his chest, with the air of a man who had come to make sure that the thunder of heaven would strike these heathens after all. The gypsies stood camped by the river, singing and burning fires.

And old Jedediah sat on his stool right in the middle of the circle, smoking his pipe and looking at the sky.

I decided to count the stones. I do not know why myself. Perhaps from obstinacy. Perhaps because of Mrs Hobbs and her father-in-law.

I counted slowly, touching each stone with my palm. The big ones. The small. The standing. The fallen. The crossbeams. It came to eighty-three.

I counted again. Eighty-one.

I counted a third time - still more slowly, marking each stone with a chalk stroke from a piece of chalk I had found right there in the grass. Eighty-five.

I stood in the middle of the circle holding the piece of chalk and felt a chill creep up my spine.

"Doesn't add up?" asked Jedediah, not turning.

"Doesn't add up."

"And never will," said the old man calmly. "They don't like to be counted. They're not for that." He took out the pipe. "You found chalk?"

I looked at the piece in my hand. White, soft, staining the fingers.

"I did."

"All the ground here is chalk," said Jedediah. "And in the chalk - flint. Black." He bent down, groped with his hand in the grass by his stool and held something out to me. "Here."

It was a stone. Flint. Black, smooth, almost round, with a white chalk crust at the edge, as if in a shirt.

"White and black," said the old man. "Chalk and flint. One soft, the other hard. One writes, the other strikes fire. Here they are always together. One in the other." He stuck the pipe in his mouth again. "Put them in your pocket. Both. They'll come in handy."

I put them in. A piece of chalk and a piece of flint. White and black.

They lay in my pocket side by side, as though they had always lain there.

* * *

In the evening, at the inn, for want of anything better to do - and because everyone was too excited to sleep - they played charades. English ones, real ones: not riddles in words but a performance in faces. The setter acted out a word syllable by syllable, silently, with gestures, and the others guessed.

Professor Holloway was the first to volunteer - and portrayed something so incomprehensible, leaping, flailing his arms and jabbing a finger at the ceiling, that after ten minutes everyone gave up, and it turned out to be the word "astronomy," which he had been trying to act out in parts: "astro" - a star, "no" - a refusal, "my" - no one ever understood how he intended to show "my." The room roared with laughter to tears. Even the vicar, against his will, twitched the corner of his mouth.

Then Mrs Hobbs set one - the word "pie" - and acted it so expressively, chewing the air with such relish, that it was guessed at once, and the professor's boy ran to the kitchen to check whether any pie was actually left.

Then, to everyone's amazement, the vicar rose. He stood a long time in the middle of the room, straight as a pole. Then slowly, solemnly, he portrayed - in gestures, without a single word - an angel. Arms spread like wings. Gaze upward. Then - a fall: he dropped to one knee, bowed, covered his face with his hands. Then - rose. Portrayed searching for something, looking around. Then pointed at me.

The room went quiet.

"'Fallen,'" said the professor uncertainly. "'Angel'?"

The vicar shook his head. Pointed at me again.

"'Wanderer'?" suggested Mrs Hobbs.

The vicar shook his head.

"'Guest,'" said Miss Lovell quietly.

The vicar slowly nodded. And sat down in his place without a word. And for the rest of the evening never once looked in my direction.

"What was that?" Miss Lovell whispered to me.

"I don't know," I said.

Old Jedediah, sitting by the fireplace, laughed quietly - for the first time in all that time. Dryly, as branches crackle in a fire.

"The parson saw too," he said into the fire, to no one in particular. "Seventy years ago. He was a lad then. The bell-ringer's son. Ran after me to the stones that night. He saw. He's just been pretending for forty years that he didn't." The old man drew on his pipe. "Parsons are good at pretending."

* * *

An hour before dawn we stood at the stones.

The sky in the east was barely beginning to lighten - not pink, not gold, but that grey, pearly, ghostly light with which the sky glows in the shortest night of the year, when night hardly comes at all. The grass was wet with dew. It smelled of chalk, sheep and the smoke of gypsy fires. Over the plain lay mist - low, white, knee-deep - and the stones rose out of it like islands out of the sea.

There were many of us. The professor with his rulers and the boy. Miss Lovell at her box on the tripod, under the black cloth, with a plate that she had just flooded with something from a dark flask. The Reverend Whitcombe - by the road, but nearer than yesterday. Mrs Hobbs with a basket of pies, "in case it is the end of the world - so as not to be on an empty stomach." The gypsies - in a semicircle, at a distance, in silence, without songs. Zora among them - I saw the coins in her plaits glint. And Jedediah - on his stool, in the very middle of the circle, with the dog at his feet.

"Stand here," Miss Lovell said to me, pointing to a place between two huge stones, right on the axis that led from the Heel Stone to the middle of the circle. "The light will be here. Don't move when I say. The exposure is five seconds. For my silver that is eternity."

"Do not stand in the middle," Zora had said. "Stand at the edge. Hold on to the stone."

I stood where Miss Lovell indicated. On the axis. Between two stones. But I stretched out my hand and laid my palm on the cold, rough stone, wet with dew, to my right. And did not take it away.

"It's coming!" shouted the professor. "It's coming! Look!"

Over the horizon, right over the Heel Stone, the edge of the sun appeared. Red, enormous, trembling in the morning haze. And from it through the mist, across the grass, across the whole circle, a strip of light stretched to us - long, straight, golden, like a taut string. It fell on the Heel Stone. Then farther. Nearer. Nearer still.

The professor sobbed with delight, waving his ruler. His boy stood with his mouth open. Miss Lovell dived under the black cloth.

"Don't move!" came dully from there. "One!"

The beam touched my feet.

"Two!"

And in that moment - not from the east, not from the sun - from above, right over me, a light opened.

* * *

I do not know how to describe it otherwise. It did not come - it opened. As an eye opens. White, blinding, so bright that the sun beside it seemed a dim candle-end. It poured from above, from the very middle of the sky, where the last, paling stars still remained - in a straight pillar, down, into the middle of the circle, and I stood at its edge.

There was no sound. At first. And then there was - not a sound, a hum. The earth underfoot began to hum like an enormous bell struck from inside. The hum passed through the soles, through the knees, through the spine - to the very teeth. The stones around me vibrated. I felt it with my palm - the stone under my hand trembled like a living thing, like the flank of a great beast.

And heat. Not the sun's - another. From within. As though someone had lit a fire in me, in the very middle of the chest, where people have a heart, and I - I did not know what I had there. The heat grew, rose, flooded the whole body - and in my pocket, I felt it through the cloth, the piece of chalk and the piece of flint grew hot. So hot that were they in my hand I would have dropped them.

And then the voice came.

It came from above - from the light. Not in words - in vibration, in hum, in heat, all at once. It spoke. I knew that it spoke. I felt each word - with my whole body, with every bone, every drop of blood. But I could not make out a single one. They slid into one another without pauses, without borders, like water pouring over stone, like the flowing lines of a pattern I had seen somewhere, sometime - on paper, on stone, on metal...

And through the hum, through the heat, through the dazzling whiteness I suddenly understood: this voice I had heard before. Not here. Not now. Sometime. When he stood by my bed. When he spoke to me in this same language. When he said one single word that I understood.

"Remember."

I wanted to answer. I wanted to cry out: I do not remember! I do not know what to remember! Tell me! But my throat clenched, and there was no voice.

The light above me began to narrow. The pillar grew thinner, brighter. It called. It drew. I felt my feet leaving the ground - no, not my feet, something else, something inside, as though I were being drawn out of myself, as a thread is drawn from a needle.

And only the hand - the hand that lay on the stone - held.

The stone under my palm was cold. The only cold thing in all that fire. I clung to it - with fingers, with nails, with all I had. Zora had said: hold on to the stone. And I held.

"Not now," I rasped. Or thought. "Not now. I have not remembered yet."

The light froze. For one moment - long, endless, like all my forgotten life.

And then - went out.

* * *

"...five!" Miss Lovell's muffled voice reached me from somewhere far off, from under the cloth. "That's all! You may breathe!"

I stood between two stones, on the axis, in the beam of the rising sun. An ordinary sun - red, warm, morning. The mist was melting over the grass. Somewhere sheep were bleating. Professor Holloway was leaping round the Heel Stone shouting: "Exactly! Exactly on the axis! I was right!" Mrs Hobbs was handing out pies. The gypsies were beginning to sing.

No one had seen anything.

No one - but three.

Old Jedediah stood by his stool - he had risen, for the first time in all that time - and looked at me. The dog at his feet was not howling - it lay with ears pressed down, looking the same way.

Zora stood among the gypsies, her palm pressed to her mouth. The coins in her plaits did not jingle - she did not stir.

And the Reverend Whitcombe - at the very edge of the circle, nearer than he had come in all those days - knelt in the wet grass. His lips moved. He was praying. Or weeping. I could not make out which.

I took my hand from the stone. On the grey lichen, where my palm had lain, a print remained. Scorched. Dark. Five fingers.

And on the grass at my feet, where I had stood - around me, in a ring - the grass was withered, yellowed, as if singed by fire. A round patch. Like this.

* * *

Miss Lovell developed the plate by noon - in her dark room under the roof of the inn, where it smelled of ether and silver. She came down into the common room holding the plate by its edges with the tips of her fingers, and her face was white as her chemicals.

She laid the plate on the table before me.

On it were the stones. The mist. The beam of sunlight from the Heel Stone. All of it crisp, sharp, silvery grey.

And where I had stood, between two stones - I was not there.

There was a silhouette. Tall. Immeasurably tall - above human height, almost to the lintel of the trilithon. Grey. Blurred at the edges, as if moulded of mist and ash. Faceless.

And a hand - one hand of his, stretched out to the side, lay on the stone.

"Five seconds," whispered Miss Lovell. "Five seconds of exposure. In five seconds a man may move, and the image will blur. But he cannot become..." she faltered, "...like this."

I looked at the plate. At the grey faceless silhouette. At the hand on the stone.

I knew him. I had seen him - at my bedside, on the balcony, in the alley, in the street. I had seen him... where? I did not remember. But I knew.

"Who is it?" asked Miss Lovell.

Jedediah, standing by the fireplace, answered for me. Not turning, looking into the fire.

"The same," said the old man. "Only later."

I did not understand. No one understood. Miss Lovell looked at the old man, then at me, then again at the plate.

"May I keep it?" she asked quietly.

"Keep it," I said. "Someone must remember."

* * *

I left Amesbury the next morning. The stagecoach waited at the crossroads. The coachman - the same one - again crossed himself, looking at me.

Mrs Hobbs wrapped a kidney pie for me for the road. Professor Holloway shook my hand and asked me to write if I should "ever find confirmation of the astronomical theory," and I promised, knowing that I would not write. Miss Lovell did not come to see me off - but on my bag I found a small photographic card. Not that one. Another. On it was one stone - the one I had held, with the dark print of a palm on its lichen. And on the back, in pencil, in her hand: "So that you do not forget what to hold on to."

The vicar did not come. But as the coach was already moving, I saw him at the churchyard fence - he stood straight as a pole, in his black frock coat, and, meeting my eyes, slowly, as if with effort, raised his hand and made the sign of the cross over me. Not as over the Evil One. As over a traveller.

Zora I did not see. Only at the very crossroads, on a roadside post, hung a red ribbon with copper coins strung on it. I do not know whether it was hers. I did not take it.

And old Jedediah stood on the ridge of the chalk plain, right by the stones, leaning on his crook, with the dog at his feet - a tiny figure against the grey giants - and looked after me. He did not wave. He simply looked. As one looks at one who is leaving, knowing that he will return. In a year. In a hundred years. In a thousand.

I thrust my hand into my pocket. The chalk and the flint lay there side by side - cooled, ordinary. White and black.

"One must remember where the beginning is," that man had told the gypsy's grandmother.

The coach lurched over a rut, and the stones on the horizon vanished in the morning haze - as though they had never been.

And I, it seems, for the first time in my life knew that I would return.
