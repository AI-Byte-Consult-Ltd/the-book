Chapter II
The Castle of Artemia Aristarkhovna

"You are late, sir," said Ignatiy Savvich, in the tone in which one announces the death of a distant relative: with sorrow, but without superfluous detail.

The old butler stood at the tall double doors of the dining hall as he had stood, no doubt, all his life: erect, grey-haired, in a dark tailcoat with polished buttons, white gloves on his dry hands, and the expression of a man who has seen so many dinner parties in his time that nothing short of the end of the world could surprise him, and even that only if it occurred before the hot course was served.

"Has the turkey been served already?" I asked.

"It has, sir. Madam was pleased to observe that the turkey, unlike certain persons, arrives on time."

"Auntie said that?"

"Madam expressed herself more briefly, sir. I permitted myself to translate it into language fit for repetition."

I could not help laughing. Ignatiy Savvich allowed himself a barely perceptible twitch at the corner of his lips - the highest degree of merriment he permitted himself on duty - and, straightening the knot of my neckcloth with a movement that held not a drop of servility but only the same calm, long-standing care with which he had, no doubt, straightened everything in this house, he threw open the doors before me.

* * *

The great stone hall received guests as it had received them for more than one decade: with towering vaults drowned in half-darkness, with gigantic chandeliers that rang with crystal at the slightest draught, and with a fireplace that crackled and spat somewhere at the far end of the hall, filling the air with the resinous, cosy scent of a pine log.

Above the fireplace, in the most prominent place, was displayed the family coat of arms: a predatory bird with wings spread wide, whether an eagle or something more ancient and less heraldically definite, framed by green, feathery branches. The walls were hung with pelts and antlers, interspersed with guns of the most various eras - from flintlocks to quite modern pieces - and the whole collection gave the hall the look not so much of a dining room as of a museum of trophies in which, through some misunderstanding, a supper had been laid.

The table - gigantic, oval, covered with a cloth of such flawless whiteness that it cut the eye no less than the midday sun - stood exactly in the middle, and around it, in the subdued light of the candles, sat a respectable company: the men mostly in military uniform, the ladies in light evening gowns, crowned with hats of feathers and bows that might have stepped straight from the pages of a metropolitan fashion journal. At the side, in a special floor stand, gleamed the set-aside sabres and firearms of the military guests - an object no less refined than the table service itself.

Not counting the servants, who stood frozen along the walls in respectful expectation, I counted eleven persons engaged in unhurried, low-voiced conversation. The table groaned with delicacies: a whole roast turkey on a silver tray of artful chasing, jellied sturgeon trembling under the lamps like amber, pies with vesiga and with mushrooms, mounds of soaked apples, salted saffron milk caps in a crystal vase, vegetables of every sort, bread - white and black, that very black bread which I, for some reason, loved best of all, though I could not have explained this fondness even under torture - and, of course, an enormous, steaming samovar surrounded by dry rings and bagels.

The maid Dunyasha, rosy, round-faced, with a thick flaxen braid coiled around her head, glided between the chairs with a tray, handing round the cordial, and, passing me, managed to whisper without moving her lips: "Master, I've saved you a pie with vesiga, the last one, under the napkin," - and vanished at once, before I could thank her.

My attire that evening was, as usual, predominantly white: beige, slightly snug trousers, a snow-white shirt, a light-grey frock coat of the softest cloth, and my favourite red-and-blue neckerchief, tied at the throat in an intricate knot - the one liberty I allowed myself in this realm of impeccable etiquette.

"You are late again, mon cher," came a familiar voice from the right, and in the next instant a hand settled familiarly on my shoulder.

Lieutenant Konstantin - in brand-new uniform, fresh from the needle, still retaining that peculiar stiffness of cloth found only in a uniform that has never been caught in the rain - looked at me with the broad, slightly insolent smile that had been his calling card almost from birth.

"I am not late," I objected, sitting down beside him. "I arrived precisely when the turkey was ready to arrive for me."

"A philosopher," he snorted approvingly. "Your aunt has been entertaining Alyosha all evening with tales of your unbearable seclusion. Any moment now she will tell him you live on moonlight alone."

"And what did you answer her?"

"That you live on moonlight only on fast days," said Konstantin imperturbably, piling a slice of turkey onto my plate as if it were his direct duty. "And on the others, on my company. Eat, or you really will turn transparent. I should have no one to travel to the capital with."

I glanced toward the opposite end of the table. Artemia Aristarkhovna, my aunt, sat there in all the splendour of her evening gown - a dark-lilac dress whose only ornament was a string of pearls - and was conducting a lively conversation with the young Lieutenant Alexei, whom she invariably addressed affectionately: Alyosha. He listened to her with that rapt attention usually reserved for very beautiful women or very influential persons - and my aunt, it must be admitted, was both at once.

The grey-haired General Pavel Nikolaevich, who sat at her left hand, listened to this conversation with far less rapture. He kept sipping his tea nervously and snorting into his magnificent, carefully groomed moustache - without once, however, permitting himself a single remark aloud. Lieutenant Alexei, absorbed in the conversation beyond all measure, simply failed to notice the General's reproachful glances, which in my opinion was either the height of innocence or the height of recklessness - depending on what actually bound the General and my aunt, a matter about which no one in the whole house spoke aloud, but which, it seemed, absolutely everyone suspected.

"What do you think," Konstantin whispered to me, leaning so that his words were meant for me alone, "will the General shoot him before dessert is served, or after?"

"After," I said gravely. "He is too well bred to spoil the turkey with bloodshed."

Konstantin snorted into his napkin so unluckily that for a moment the whole table looked in our direction, and I vainly tried to compose my face into the most well-behaved expression of which I was capable.

* * *

To the left of the General sat a lady of venerable years in a lavish white gown and a hat adorned with feathers and emeralds of such size that I could not help wondering whether the hat weighed more than its owner. Evdokia Gerasimovna - Konstantin's aunt, a frequent guest of ours and, by rumour, the object of the General's secret and unrequited (or perhaps requited, but carefully concealed) adoration - sighed at the table no less often than her neighbour, though the reason for her sighs, I suspect, was precisely the same.

Opposite her, on the other side of the General, sat two guests whom I had seen in our house only once before - Nadezhda Porfirievna Kutasova, widow of a provincial marshal of the nobility, a decisive, stout lady with a lorgnette on a chain and a voice capable of drowning out a regimental band, and her daughter Lizonka, a girl of some eighteen years, slight, fair-haired, in a pink dress with so many frills that she resembled a pastry mistakenly seated at table with the guests.

There was no need to guess the purpose of their visit. Nadezhda Porfirievna did not take her lorgnette off the General with the persistence with which a gunner does not take his eyes off an enemy redoubt.

"Pavel Nikolaevich," she sang out, as soon as the General set down his cup, "my Lizonka was embroidering a tobacco pouch for you the other day. With roses! She heard that you are a great admirer of roses."

"Exceedingly touched, madam," replied the General in a voice in which there was exactly as much courtesy as etiquette demanded and not a grain more. "Although, I confess, I have not smoked for twelve years."

"But one need not smoke!" said Nadezhda Porfirievna, not in the least abashed. "The pouch can be for... for something else. For seeds! For rose seeds. Lizonka, tell Pavel Nikolaevich which roses you embroidered."

"T-tea roses, Mamma," faltered Lizonka, blushing to the very frills.

"Tea roses!" Nadezhda Porfirievna announced triumphantly. "Did you hear, Pavel Nikolaevich? Tea roses!"

"I heard, madam," said the General. "Tea roses do not set seed. They are propagated by cuttings."

A silence fell in which one could hear the fire crackling. Evdokia Gerasimovna raised her fan to her face, and it seemed to me that behind the fan she was smiling. Konstantin stepped on my foot under the table with such force that I nearly cried out.

"I cannot bear this," he whispered in my ear, shaking with silent laughter. "That is the third time this evening he has buried her. One more salvo and the pouch of tea roses will go into the fireplace together with Mamma."

"Hush," I hissed, barely restraining myself. "She will hear."

"She hears nothing but the word 'general.' Her ears are tuned to that rank like a hunting dog's to game."

Nadezhda Porfirievna, however, did not know how to retreat. Having suffered defeat on the rose front, she immediately shifted her fire - and, to my horror, onto me.

"And you, young man," she addressed me, levelling her lorgnette, "you, I hear, are a great lover of books? Lizonka adores reading too! Lizonka, say what you are reading now."

"A Nest of Gentlefolk, Mamma," whispered Lizonka, looking at her plate.

"A splendid book," I said, turning to the girl with the most amiable smile I could muster, "though, I admit, its ending is sad. I hope you have not finished it yet, Elizaveta..." I faltered.

The patronymic. I had just heard it - or had I not? Nadezhda Porfirievna was the widow of the marshal, but what was the marshal's own name? I had been told. I had known it for certain a moment ago - known it as firmly as I knew the name of my own aunt - and now in the place of that knowledge yawned an even, smooth emptiness, as though someone had carefully drawn a single thread out of my memory without disturbing the rest.

The pause dragged on. Lizonka lifted her eyes to me - bewildered, frightened.

"Elizaveta Andreevna," Konstantin prompted calmly, as though it were the most natural thing in the world. "My friend, madam, became so absorbed in A Nest of Gentlefolk that he forgot which nest he himself is in."

Everyone laughed. Nadezhda Porfirievna nodded graciously, Lizonka turned pink again, and the conversation rolled on. And I sat looking at my plate and felt a slow chill creeping down my spine. It was a trifle - a forgotten patronymic of a scarcely known young lady, it happens to everyone. And yet in the way it had been forgotten - not faded gradually, as unimportant names fade, but vanished all at once, instantly, as though it had been plucked out - there was something I did not like.

"Thank you," I whispered to Konstantin.

"Don't mention it," he answered, just as quietly, without looking at me. "You are not yourself tonight, mon cher. All evening it is as if you were not here." And, after a pause, he added in his former, mocking tone: "However, if you take it into your head to forget someone's name again, I shall be sitting beside you. That is what friends are for: to remember for you when you cannot do it yourself."

I looked at him. He had already turned away, helping himself to jellied sturgeon, and his face wore its usual carefree expression, as though he had said nothing out of the ordinary. But something in those words - "remember for you" - echoed in me with a strange, warm and at the same time disquieting resonance that I could not have explained.

* * *

"Permit me, permit me, young man," came a creaking voice with a slight German accent from the other end of the table. "I have been observing you for a quarter of an hour. You are pale as lime, you are rubbing your temple, and you have just forgotten the patronymic of a young lady that was given to you no later than the soup. Is it a headache?"

Doctor Karl Fyodorovich Strauch - an old friend of the house, my aunt's physician and, in addition, the only man capable of declining a second slice of pie in her presence - looked at me over his gold spectacles with the professional interest with which an entomologist looks at a rare beetle.

"A trifle, Karl Fyodorovich," I said. "It has ached a little since morning."

"Since morning," repeated the doctor, as though writing it down in an invisible notebook. "And yesterday? And the day before? Have you not had of late... how shall I put it... lapses? Moments when you cannot recall how you came to be in this or that room? Or what you were doing an hour ago?"

It grew quieter at the table. My aunt, breaking off her conversation with Alexei, turned her head in our direction - quickly, too quickly.

"Karl Fyodorovich," she said evenly, "this is a dinner party, not an infirmary reception."

"I beg your pardon, Artemia Aristarkhovna," the doctor bowed slightly but did not take his eyes from me. "Professional habit. Memory is a most peculiar organ, gentlemen. I have treated people for thirty years and still do not understand how it is made. One man remembers what the sky looked like on the day of his christening, yet cannot recall what he ate for breakfast. And another time..." - he paused - "another time a man remembers what never happened to him at all."

"How do you mean, what never happened?" Alexei grew interested.

"Just so, young man. I had patients who described with perfect clarity cities in which they had never been. Streets, smells, faces. One retired major swore that he remembered standing on the deck of a ship bound for shores that are on no map. He described the rigging, the colour of the water, the voices of the sailors. And yet he had never in his life travelled farther than Tula."

I felt the tips of my fingers go cold. The deck of a ship. The salt wind. The shreds that had flickered through my head all that day and which I could neither hold nor explain.

"And how did you treat him?" asked the General with an interest unexpected in him.

"With cold rubdowns and readings of Karamzin, Your Excellency," replied the doctor. "The rubdowns did not help. Neither did Karamzin."

The table laughed, and the awkwardness dispersed. But I noticed that my aunt did not laugh. She looked at Doctor Strauch with a long, heavy look, which he, it seems, understood perfectly, for he did not once speak of memory again all evening.

* * *

Farther down the table, Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich - husband of the quiet, affable Vera Anatolievna - was just finishing another tale of his martial exploits, twirling his very long, longest of moustaches with the same methodicalness with which some people click the beads on a counting-house abacus.

"...and then," he thundered, addressing himself chiefly to his own plate, "the Turkish battery fired a salvo, and we, imagine, were standing in an open position, without any cover whatever..."

"Misha," Vera Anatolievna interrupted him gently, "might we perhaps let the guests finish their turkey before the battery opens fire?"

The Colonel laughed good-naturedly at his own lack of restraint, and I, taking advantage of the pause, at last turned to the plate I had hardly touched so far - too busy was I observing this small, familiar theatre played out at my aunt's table nearly every other evening. Under the napkin, as promised, I found the pie with vesiga, still warm. I caught Dunyasha's eye by the wall and nodded to her almost imperceptibly; she beamed.

It was at that moment that I noticed Vera Anatolievna's gaze turned upon me.

That gaze lingered on my face a fraction of an instant longer than simple politeness requires - one looks thus not at an acquaintance, but at something vaguely recognisable that one cannot quite recall: perhaps a portrait once seen, perhaps a face from an old, half-forgotten dream. Then she blinked, smiled at me with the most ordinary, social smile, and said:

"You look very pensive today, my friend. Is your aunt not making you read too much of an evening?"

"A little," I answered, trying to make my voice sound light, though that first look of hers had somehow lodged in me like an unpleasant splinter. "I fear I shall soon have to ask her for my wages to buy a new pair of eyes."

She smiled again, this time quite ordinarily, and the conversation flowed safely on, to some fashionable new novels brought from Paris or perhaps London - but for some time I kept stealing glances in her direction, trying to understand what exactly I had fancied in that first, overlong look. No explanation presented itself. The list of my inexplicable things today, however, had already swollen to indecent proportions, so I decided to set this riddle aside for the time being in the same place where all the others already rested - until better, clearer times.

The General, taking advantage of the fact that the conversation had turned away from Alexei, thought it possible to put in a remark of his own - as always, terse and to the point.

"The Porte is troubled again," he said, addressing no one in particular, though it was evident that the phrase was directed first of all at the Colonel, the only one at the table capable of appreciating its weight. "The Sultan again asks for assistance. I fear that before a year is out I shall have to pack for Constantinople once more."

At that word - Constantinople - something strange stirred in me: not a memory, no, rather the echo of a memory, a distant reverberation of a narrow lane smelling of the sea and spices, of a carpet with a strange pattern, of two grey-bearded old men at a game of backgammon. I blinked, driving off the delusion - it too closely resembled the earlier feeling at the fountain for me to wish its repetition - and tried to concentrate on the turkey.

"You travel too often to those parts, Pavel Nikolaevich," my aunt intervened, and a shade of real, unfeigned anxiety flickered in her voice. "One day I shall cease to be surprised if you announce to me that you have decided to settle there altogether."

"Duty, Artemia Aristarkhovna," replied the General with dignity, "seldom asks our consent as to the route."

"As does life in general," Konstantin put in with feigned philosophical air, for which he promptly earned from his aunt Evdokia Gerasimovna a ringing slap of the fan on the hand.

"Constantinople!" Nadezhda Porfirievna clasped her hands, scenting a new breach in the defences. "Ah, Pavel Nikolaevich, how I should dream of seeing Constantinople! Lizonka, would you like to see Constantinople?"

"It is said to be very hot there, Mamma," whispered Lizonka.

"Hot, madam," confirmed the General. "And dusty. And dogs everywhere. And, I venture to assure you, absolutely no tea roses."

Evdokia Gerasimovna, behind her fan, emitted a sound which, with a certain indulgence, might be taken for a cough.

* * *

When dessert was served - blancmange trembling on its dish, cranberry kissel, and pastila brought, as my aunt announced with pride, from Kolomna itself - Evdokia Gerasimovna suddenly snapped her fan shut and declared to the whole table:

"Lizonka, my dear, you play charmingly, they say! Will you not delight us?"

Nadezhda Porfirievna beamed as though she had been awarded a decoration. Lizonka flushed, protested, was persuaded, escorted to the piano standing in the corner of the hall beneath the portrait of some stern-eyed ancestor in a powdered wig - and, after hesitating, touched the keys.

She played a romance. I did not know its name - or I knew it, but this evening, evidently, my memory had decided to give me nothing for free. The melody was simple, a little sad, and Lizonka, forgetting her shyness, played it unexpectedly well - with the sincerity of those people to whom it is easier to speak in music than in words. The hall fell quiet. Even Nadezhda Porfirievna lowered her lorgnette. The General, leaning back in his chair, gazed somewhere into the fire of the hearth, and his face, usually so impenetrable, became for a moment strangely young and defenceless. I noticed that Evdokia Gerasimovna was looking not at Lizonka, but at him.

And suddenly, in the midst of that melody, I smelled a scent.

Salt. Spices. Sun-warmed stone. It came from nowhere - not from an open window, the windows were closed, not from the kitchen, where nothing of the kind was being cooked - it simply arose, filled for an instant my whole being and at once vanished, leaving in my mouth the aftertaste of something sweet, honeyed, like a dried fig. The music went on playing. No one around noticed anything.

I raised my palm to my temple. The headache that had plagued me all evening grew sharper.

"You are going away again," Konstantin said softly, without turning his head. "I see how you go away. Where do you go, mon cher?"

"Nowhere," I answered. "Just my head."

He said nothing. But I felt his shoulder - in the stiff, new cloth of his uniform - touch mine for a moment and linger a little longer than chance required. As though he were checking whether I was still here.

Lizonka finished. The hall applauded - sincerely, even the General. Nadezhda Porfirievna wiped away a tear with a lace handkerchief and repeated: "My Lizonka! My little dove!" Doctor Strauch, clapping, watched me closely over his spectacles.

* * *

The evening flowed on in its customary course - laughter, the clink of dishes, snatches of conversation about new books, about the prospects of the harvest, about which of the capital's young ladies had made the better marriage this season. I listened with half an ear, now and then putting in a remark when I felt that my silence was becoming too noticeable - and when Konstantin, inspired, began to question me about some book on heraldry that I had been leafing through in the library the other day, I caught myself talking for almost a quarter of an hour on end, unable to stop, until I noticed that the whole company had long since moved on to another subject, while I was still enthusiastically telling Konstantin the history of family coats of arms, which no one but him, it seemed, had any intention of listening to.

"You are sometimes perfectly insufferable," he remarked with undisguised pleasure, "one moment you cannot be drawn out with pincers, the next you cannot be silenced."

"Then I am unpredictable," I answered. "That is far more interesting than being predictable."

"More interesting," agreed Konstantin. "But more tiring. Especially for those who try to guess what you will be like tomorrow."

"And do you try?"

He looked at me - for a moment seriously, without his usual smirk - and at once looked away, reaching for a bagel.

"Hardly," he said. "As if I had nothing better to do."

The supper, which had apparently been going on for more than an hour, began at last to incline toward its natural end. The headache that had plagued me almost from the very beginning of the evening had grown so much that I thought it best to take my leave before it turned into something more serious.

"Auntie," I said, rising and approaching Artemia Aristarkhovna, "forgive me, but my head today flatly refuses to serve me any longer. Allow me to wish you good night."

She held out her hand for me to kiss - a gesture which over the long years had become almost a ritual between us - and I touched my lips to the thin, ring-laden fingers.

"Go and rest, my friend," she said with that warmth that was meant for me alone and for no one else at that table. "You are too pale today. Karl Fyodorovich is right, though I shall not allow him to say so at dinner."

Then, lowering her voice so much that only I and, perhaps, the General, who sat closer than the rest, could hear, she added - half in jest, half in earnest, with that strange, fleeting sadness which occasionally, against her will, showed in her face whenever the conversation touched, even by its edge, upon the past:

"Do not wander too far along the corridors tonight, do you hear? This castle is older than it seems, and it has, believe me, places that are better left in peace."

"Places?" I asked, involuntarily smiling at so unexpected a warning. "Are you frightening me with family ghosts, Auntie?"

She looked at me for a long moment - and in that look flashed something I had never before noticed in her: not fear, but something neighbouring on fear, the memory of something she would have preferred never to recall.

The General, sitting beside her, slowly lowered his cup onto its saucer. The sound was very quiet - but in the pause that fell between us it rang out like the click of a cocking hammer. I noticed that the General was looking not at me but at my aunt, and that in his look was a question to which she, almost imperceptibly, shook her head.

"Let us leave it," she said at last, restoring lightness to her voice. "It is late, my friend. Go to sleep."

Already on my way to the door, I caught myself in a thought that seemed to me almost sacrilegious: I knew, in essence, nothing of our kinship but the very word "aunt," by which I had called her since I could remember. Never once had she told me whose sister she was - my mother's or my father's. Never once had she shown me a portrait or a letter that would confirm this relationship by anything other than her love for me and my faith in her words. The thought, scarcely arisen, seemed to me absurd and ungrateful, and I hastened to be rid of it - but to be rid of it entirely, it seems, I did not quite succeed.

I bowed to the esteemed company, received parting wishes of good night from the General, the Colonel, Doctor Strauch and Konstantin - the last of whom, however, contrived to shout after me something remarkably spiteful, which I had no time to make out - and left the hall for the dimly lit corridor.

Ignatiy Savvich, standing at the doors just as erect, as though in all those hours he had not moved an inch, handed me a lighted candle in a copper candlestick.

"Shall I see you to your room, sir?"

"There is no need, Ignatiy Savvich. I know the way."

The old man paused. Then he said - evenly, without changing the expression of his face, as though reporting the weather:

"Madam bade me give her regards and remind you: the left wing is locked for the night. Draughts, sir. An old house."

"Draughts," I repeated.

"Precisely so, sir," said Ignatiy Savvich, and looked at me as one looks at a man to whom something has been said and who, regrettably, will most likely not obey. "Good night."

I took the candle and went along the corridor, carrying with me not only the heaviness in my temples but also the strange, itching aftertaste of my aunt's words, lodged somewhere on the very border of consciousness, in the same place where all my inexplicable anxieties of that day had settled.

This castle is older than it seems.

I could not know that evening how soon I should be convinced of the truth of those words - and how bitterly I should regret it.
