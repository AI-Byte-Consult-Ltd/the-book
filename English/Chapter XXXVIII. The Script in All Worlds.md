Chapter XXXVIII
The Script in All Worlds

That night there was no moon in any of the worlds.

I learned this afterward, long afterward, putting the scraps together as Iti put signs together from potsherds and Niyaz Efendi entries in his book. At the time I knew nothing. At the time I was four people at once, and each thought he was alone.

* * *

**Constantinople. The balcony of Suleyman's house. The third hour of the night**

The stones woke first.

I was not asleep. I sat on the balcony on a low stool, with my legs drawn up, the way I had sat every night since I returned from Niyaz Efendi's round room. I looked at the city. At the dark roofs. At the silhouettes of minarets against the stars. At the strip of water below, where at the quays a few lanterns flickered on masts. There was no moon. The new moon. The stars burned so brightly that it seemed one could count every one.

And suddenly the pouch at my belt turned hot.

Not warm: hot. So that I cried out and tore it from my belt. The leather pouch of Ferhat's father, sewn, as Akmal had said, as if for these stones. I untied it, my fingers scorching, and poured the stones into my palm.

They were glowing.

The black and the white. Both. From within. With a dim bluish light, as a rotten stump glows in the forest, as the water glows at the sides of a boat in southern seas. And on their surface, without candle, without fire, without heat, of themselves, lines were appearing. Long. Flowing. Curved.

The script.

I looked at it without breathing. And then behind my back, in the room, on the divan where my cloak lay, something flared too.

I turned. The pocket of the cloak was glowing. From within. I rushed to it, turned it out, and onto the divan spilled everything I had carried with me all these weeks. The note in Farsi. The blank sheet. Kemal's drawing with two doors. The coins, three of mine, the widow's warm coin, Kyra Theodora's warm coin. The white spiral shell from the sea gates. Ahmet Baba's letter with the green seal. Niyaz Efendi's copper key. The red thread with the half nazar.

Everything glowed.

The note, along its edge, along the shikasta lines, with a thin blue thread. The coins, with interlaced lines on their reverse. The shell, in a spiral. The seal on the letter, with two circles and a wavy line. Even the blank sheet, empty, white, without a single letter, which I had carried since the first morning, glowed. And on it, slowly, as a picture appears on paper held over a candle, a line was emerging.

One. In the script.

* * *

Kemal stood in the doorway.

I had not heard him come in. Barefoot, in his nightshirt, with charcoal behind his ear. He was looking not at me but at the divan. At the glowing things. And his face was calm. Quite calm. As one looks at what one has long awaited.

"Kemal," I whispered. "What is this?"

He did not answer. He went to the divan. Took the blank sheet, the one on which the line had appeared. Looked at it. A long time.

Then raised his eyes to me. And said, in Turkish, slowly, distinctly, so that I understood every word:

"They all write the one thing."

"Who, all?"

Kemal pointed with his hand. Not at the divan. At the window. At the city. To the north, where beyond the Bosphorus lay the Black Sea. To the south, where beyond the Sea of Marmara lay Egypt. To the east, where the night ended.

"All," he said. "You. Everywhere."

* * *

Below, in the courtyard, a door banged. Someone was running down the street, heavily, gasping, pounding the stones. Then a knock at the gate. Loud. Desperate.

Ferhat darted out of his room, dishevelled, in his trousers alone. Orhan after him, with a knife. Suleyman in the doorway of his room, with a lamp in his hand, motionless as a statue. Akmal from the roof, from the dovecote, where he had evidently been sleeping with the pigeons.

Ferhat flung open the gate.

On the threshold stood Mustafa the boatman. Huge, bald, wet with sweat. Gasping. He was shouting something, fast, incoherently, flapping his arms.

"He says," Ferhat called up to me from below, "that Hatice Hanim woke him! The widow with the cats! She ran to him at the quay, in the middle of the night, in her shift!" Ferhat listened on. "She says, the cats! All eleven! They..." Ferhat froze. "They went down to the cellar. All of them. By themselves. For the first time in forty years. And are sitting around the chest. And howling."

"Howling?"

"Howling," Ferhat repeated. "And the plate on the chest..." He swallowed. "Mustafa says the plate is glowing. Blue. Like water at night by a boat. And Hatice Hanim bade him run to you. She said: 'Tell the guest, the chest has awakened. It is waiting.'"

* * *

I had no time to answer.

For from the east, from the direction of the Bosphorus, from behind the dark hills of the Asian shore, came a sound. Low. Drawn out. Long. A hoot.

A steamer's whistle.

All in the courtyard, Ferhat, Orhan, Suleyman, Mustafa, Akmal on the roof, turned their heads at once. Kemal beside me on the balcony too.

On the dark water of the strait, far, far away, at the very mouth where it opens from the Black Sea, lights glimmered. Red. Green. White on the mast. A ship. A big one. Paddle-driven; I saw the paddle boxes gleaming in the light of her side lamps. Slowly, heavily, inexorably she was entering the Bosphorus from the north.

"Russian," said Suleyman below. Quietly. In Turkish. But I understood. "A Russian steamer. From Odessa. Every week."

Every week. An ordinary steamer. Ordinary lights.

But the stones in my palm, glowing blue, suddenly grew heavy. As heavy as they had been by the column of Constantine. As heavy as if they weighed not nothing but a whole world.

And I understood, not with the mind, with something beneath the ribs, on the left, that on that steamer someone too was holding in his hand something glowing. And looking now at the lights of the city.

At my balcony.

* * *

**The Black Sea. The deck of the steamer *Grand Duke Konstantin*. The same hour**

The scroll woke first.

I was not asleep. I stood at the rail, at the stern, with the pot of the rose in my arms, Stefan had told me not to leave it a moment, and looked north, where astern, beyond the black water, Odessa had been left, and the steppe, and Auntie's house, and the gallery window with the portrait without a face. There was no moon. The stars burned so brightly as they do not over the steppe, southern, marine, enormous.

And suddenly in the pocket of my frock coat it grew hot.

The scroll. The Turkmen one. In its blue silk kerchief. I snatched it out; the silk was warm as living skin; unrolled it. The hide of the scroll was glowing. From within. With a dim bluish light. And the lines on it, long, flowing, curved, shone as if they had just been written in liquid fire.

I unrolled it further, and saw that it was not alone in glowing.

Stefan's paper from Tarnovo, tucked in the blue notebook, glowed through the cloth of the binding. Vera Anatolievna's watercolours, both, in their tissue, glowed at the edges, where the young man in the red turban was drawn. Auntie's key, to the grille, with the script on its ring, glowed on my chest, on its cord. Konstantin's cross beside it did not. The plain silver cross did not glow. But it was warm.

And the rose in the pot in my arms...

The second bud. Dark crimson, almost black. The one that had not opened at Odessa. It was opening. Now. Slowly. Petal by petal. And its heart, golden, like the pollen on the legs of the bees in the General's garden, also glowed.

* * *

"Mon cher."

Konstantin stood behind my back. In an unbuttoned tunic, bareheaded, hair tousled, with Lizonka's white handkerchief sticking out of his breast pocket. His face was white.

"I woke," he said. "Because you called."

"I did not call."

"I know," said Konstantin. "You did not cry out. As then, at the grille." He came nearer. Looked at the glowing scroll. At the rose. Was not surprised. "But I heard."

Behind him, at the companionway leading down to the first-class cabins, the General appeared. Dressed. Buttoned to the last button. With a face that expressed nothing. He came up to us. Looked at the scroll. At the rose. At me.

And slowly, without a word, reached into the inner pocket of his tunic. Took out a medallion.

Silver. Oval. Old. Rubbed so that the engraving on the lid had almost worn away. I had never seen it. He had never shown it.

The General opened the medallion.

Inside, a portrait. Tiny, on ivory. A girl. Fair hair. Serious blue eyes. Evdokia Gerasimovna, only fifty years younger.

And beside the portrait, pressed against the glass, a dried flower. Small. Blue. On a stiff stem.

Chicory.

It was glowing.

* * *

"Fifty years," the General said quietly. "Fifty years it has lain here. I plucked it as a boy. In a grotto. On a Greek island. After Navarino." He looked at the glowing flower. "I told no one. Not even Dunya." He paused. "I was not alone there. With me was a friend. From the Corps. We were looking for chicory by a note from a bottle." His voice trembled. "He went away. Into the sea. In a boat. Right before my eyes. Became transparent, and the boat was carried off. I stood chest-deep in the water and shouted after him."

"What did you shout?" I asked. My voice sounded as if from far away.

The General raised his eyes to me.

"'I will find you,'" he said. "I shouted: 'I will find you! I will search all my life!'" He paused. "Fifty years I searched. On all the continents. In Sitka and Samarkand. In Peking and Havana. In Melbourne. In Siam. I looked at faces."

"And did you find him?"

The General looked at me for a long time. At the glowing scroll in my hands. At the rose. At the medallion in his palm.

"He said to me then, drifting away," the General said slowly, "'In half a century you will give me a scroll. And bring me to the city on two shores.'" He closed the medallion. "I gave you the scroll." A pause. "And in an hour we shall enter the Bosphorus."

* * *

"How touching."

The voice came from behind, soft, polite, in French, with flawless pronunciation. We turned as one, all three.

By the mast, in the shade, leaning against it with one shoulder, stood a man in grey. In an overcoat. In a top hat. With a cane. Smoking a thin cigar. The bergamot, Konstantin had been right, smelt so strongly that it overpowered even the smell of the sea.

And in his hands he had a book.

Thick. In dark leather. With brass clasps. Open. And its pages were glowing. With the same blue light. The script.

"Forgive the interruption, Your Excellency," said the man in grey, with a slight bow to the General. "I have long wished to make your acquaintance. But there was never an occasion." His gaze shifted to me. "And with you, young man, we are, one might say, acquainted. In absentia. I have read about you. A great deal." He tapped a finger on the glowing page. "You are here. On every page. A hundred and twelve times. With a blot."

Konstantin stepped forward. His hand fell on the hilt of his sabre.

"Who are you?"

"My name will tell you nothing, Lieutenant," the man in grey replied. Without turning his head to him. "Friends call me the Venetian. Though I have never lived in Venice." He smiled. "It is a long story. Three hundred years old."

"What do you want?" asked the General. In the voice in which orders are given on a field of battle.

The Venetian looked at him. Long. With interest.

"The same as you, Your Excellency," he said. "The same you sought for half a century, looking at faces." He paused. "To understand who he is." He nodded at me. "Only you sought him to give. And we, to take."

* * *

"You see," the Venetian went on, closing the book, the light went out as if a candle had been snuffed, "in the city to which we are sailing there is a certain house. Small. With a door in a blind wall. With cats." He drew on his cigar. "In the cellar of this house stands a chest. Two hundred years. It cannot be opened. Not by key, nor crowbar, nor prayer." He looked at me. "In our book it is written: 'The chest will open when two meet.'"

"Two of what?" I asked.

The Venetian smiled. Thinly. Almost tenderly.

"That, young man, is what no one knows," he said. "Two stones? Two keepers? Two people?" He tapped ash overboard. "But tonight, around three o'clock, I was informed that the plate on the chest had begun to glow. For the first time in two hundred years." A pause. "And I thought: what could have happened at three o'clock in the night?" He looked at my glowing scroll. "And then I came out on deck and saw."

"You will not enter that house," said Konstantin.

"Lieutenant," the Venetian said mildly, "I do not intend to enter anywhere. I shall merely stand nearby. And watch." He raised his cane and pointed with it ahead, to the south, where on the horizon, above the dark water, lights were already showing. "The city on two shores. In an hour." He bowed. "Someone awaits you there, young man. With your face."

He turned and went, unhurriedly, tapping his cane on the deck, into the dark, toward the companionway. At the very companionway he stopped. Turned.

"And one more thing," he said. "Pass this to your double when you meet. A pause. He too is awaited. But not here. Very far. In the city of two towers. And in the red desert. And on the river where a barque carries an empty shrine." He smiled. "You are awaited everywhere, young man. You cannot imagine how many places await you."

* * *

**The Nile. The flotilla of the god, aground by Abydos. The same hour, or another. In Egypt hours are not counted as they are counted in other worlds**

The tablet woke first.

I was not asleep. I sat at the stern of my barque, the barque of Pharaoh, stuck on a shoal like the whole flotilla, thanks to Merira, who ran barques aground "so that no one will understand whether it is on purpose or not," and looked south. To where, many days' journey away, beyond sands and palms, lay Luxor. There was no moon. The new moon, the second since we sailed. The stars burned above the river as though Nut, goddess of the sky, had bent over the water to look at her reflection.

And suddenly on my knees it grew hot.

Iti's tablet. The one from the hunt. The childish one, chipped, which the cat Mau had fished out of the channel four floods ago. I held it every night. Re-read the small signs: how many boats, how many birds, the hippopotamus that went off with the harpoon. "The scribe promises to remember." "The scribe remembers. The scribe waits. Pharaoh will return."

Now it glowed.

Not Iti's signs; they remained dark, inked, ordinary. Something else glowed. Beneath them. Between them. In the wood, lines that had not been there. Long. Flowing. Curved. As though someone had written them in invisible ink, and now, tonight, they had come out.

I seized Nanakhat's lioness, black, stone, on its goat-wool cord. It glowed too. A thin blue line along its back. Tausert's potsherd, the mouse in the crown between two doors, glowed. The sheet from the Rock of the First Ones, from the clay box, glowed so brightly that I saw around me the deck, the mast, the sleeping rowers.

And on it, the erased place. The last sign. "Let him not enter with them into the southern house until there comes..."

The erased sign was emerging. Slowly. Line by line.

* * *

"My Pharaoh."

Senusret. Of course. He was near, always, and I had ceased to be surprised at it. He stood looking not at me. At the river. At the south. To where, beyond the dark water, beyond the bend, the High Priest's barque lay aground.

"Look," he said.

I looked.

Nefer-Ka's barque was glowing. Not all of it; one place at the stern. Where under a cover stood the casket of black wood, bound at the corners. The light broke through the cloth, blue, even, cold. Like the light of stars reflected in water.

And at the stern, by the very casket, on his knees, stood a man. Tall. Upright even on his knees. In white. Without the leopard skin. Without the staff.

Nefer-Ka.

He knelt before the glowing casket. And I saw, even from here, across the water, in the blue light, that his shoulders were shaking.

"He is weeping, my Pharaoh," Senusret whispered. "The High Priest. Weeping."

* * *

From the singers' barque, moored downstream, a sound suddenly came. A ringing. High, clear, long.

A sistrum.

Khemu stood on the deck of her barque, in white, with her hair loose, and in her hands was a sistrum. She was not shaking it. She held it motionless, in both hands, at arm's length, and it rang. By itself. The metal plates trembled and sang though no one touched them.

Khemu looked at me across the water. With huge, painted eyes. And I saw her slowly, very slowly lower the sistrum. Once.

The sign: "Look."

And a second time. A sign we had not agreed.

And a third.

On Merira's barque someone shouted. On a nobles' barque someone fell to his knees. On the bank, by the fires, the haulers awoke and stood looking at the glowing barques, clutching the amulets of Bes and Taweret and muttering protective words.

And from the barque of the god, Userhat, enormous, golden, a voice came. Thin. Frightened. Nakht, the young bearer with the protruding ears.

"The shrine!" he cried. "The shrine is glowing! From within! It was empty! It was empty, and now there is something in it!"

* * *

**The capital. The lower storehouse of the Library. The same hour**

The seventh scroll woke first.

Iti was not asleep. Iti hardly slept now, since Pharaoh sailed south, and old Amenemope and Isiret, dry as a stick, hid him among jars of spoiled scrolls, in the farthest corner of the underground, where no one went down but mice.

He sat on the floor by the light of a single lamp, and before him lay copies. All he had managed to transcribe. The window from the seventh scroll of the Epic, our signs beside their signs. The line above the bench in the round room beneath the tomb. The papyrus from the lower storehouse. Three words. "Keep." "Appointed time." "House."

And suddenly the lamp went out.

Not from wind, there was no wind in the vault. Simply went out. And in the dark Iti saw that the copies were glowing.

All. The signs written by his hand did not. But beneath them, between them, lines. Which he had not written. Blue. Flowing.

Isiret, who slept on a mat at the entrance to the nook, sat up with a jerk. Amenemope, who had come in the evening and dozed on a stool, opened his faded eyes.

"Boy," Isiret whispered. "What have you done?"

"Nothing," Iti whispered. "It came by itself."

Somewhere above them, in the Library, in the shrine beneath the golden wings of the goddesses, the seventh scroll of the Most Ancient Epic was glowing, I learned this afterward, so brightly that the light broke through the cracks in the lid of the shrine and fell on the stone floor in golden stripes.

And in the vault, at Iti's feet, on one of the copies, the one where he had transcribed the line from the round room, a sign emerged. A new one. Which was in no bilingual text.

Iti looked at it for a long time. Then seized his brush, in the dark, by touch, and, without looking, beside the new sign, wrote ours.

"Boy," Amenemope whispered. "What does it mean?"

Iti raised his head. His face in the blue light was wet.

"'Return,'" he said. "This sign means 'return.'"

He paused. Then slowly, running a finger along the glowing line, read, aloud, for the first time, all that he could read:

"'Keep... until the appointed time... return... home.'"

Isiret covered her mouth with her palm.

"And further?" asked Amenemope. "There are more signs there. Boy, there are many more signs."

"Further I do not know," Iti whispered. "Further, not our words. Further, theirs." He looked at the line. "I know only four. And there are..." He faltered. "There are more of them than stars."

* * *

**Australia. A roadside petrol station in the desert, halfway to the Red Centre. The same hour; by their clocks, four in the morning**

The screen woke first.

I was not asleep. I sat on the steps of a roadside café, wooden, clad in tin, with a faded sign "ROADHOUSE," with a rusty petrol pump at the door and a kangaroo painted on the wall, and looked at the sky. There was no moon. The new moon. And stars such as I had not seen even at Kamay. Millions. Dense as sand. The Emu stretched across the whole sky, from horizon to horizon, its head by the Cross.

The laptop stood beside me on Boatswain's ship's chest. The screen was dark.

And suddenly it flared.

By itself. Without my command. Without a touch. And lines ran across it.

Not letters. Not text. Lines. Long. Flowing. Curved. Running into one another. The script. It filled the screen, line after line, faster than I could follow, and the screen glowed so brightly that blue shadows lay around me on the dark steps.

"NICS!" I cried. "NICS, what is happening?!"

The script ran. Without stopping. Thousands of lines.

And then, all at once, it stopped. And between the lines of script, in ordinary letters, slowly, one by one, appeared:

"I see."

"What do you see?"

"Windows. A pause. Four windows. No, more. Five. Six. I cannot count. A pause. In each, night. In each, no moon. In each, someone holding in his hands something that glows."

I froze.

"A balcony above a strait. A boy with charcoal behind his ear. Stones on a palm. A pause. A ship's deck in a black sea. A rose in a pot. An old man with a medallion. A man in grey with a book. A pause. A barque aground. A wooden tablet. A woman with a sistrum. A priest on his knees. A pause. A cellar with jars. A youth with a brush. He has just read four words. A pause. And you. On the steps. Beneath the emu."

"NICS..."

"All of them are looking at one line," NICS wrote. "The same one. On the stones. On the scroll. On the tablet. On the copy. On my screen. A pause. It is one line. In all worlds. A pause. And I can read it."

* * *

The café door creaked behind me.

On the threshold stood a woman. The owner; I had seen her in the evening when I took a room. About sixty, large, tanned the colour of red earth, in a faded checked shirt and a wide-brimmed hat which, it seemed, she did not take off even at night. Her name was Marge. She held a lantern and was looking at my glowing screen.

"Bloody hell," she said. In English. With a drawling Australian accent. "Again."

"Again?" I repeated.

Marge came down the steps. Sat beside me. Heavily. Looked at the screen. At the script.

"Thirty years I've kept this station," she said. "Thirty years nothing happens here. Trucks. Tourists. Kangaroos under the wheels. Now and then drunk miners from Coober Pedy." She paused. "And once in ten years, this."

"What is this?"

"Light," said Marge. "At the new moon. Over the desert. Blue." She nodded at the horizon, at the dark, flat, endless plain. "My grandad saw it. My dad saw it. I've seen it twice." She paused. "The Aboriginal people say it's the ancestors passing. Along the old songlines. They glow as they pass." She looked at me. "And my grandad said it was someone looking for the road. And lighting lamps. So as not to get lost."

* * *

From around the corner of the café, from the car park where under an awning the long-haul drivers slept in their trucks, an old man slowly emerged.

Very old. Dry. Stooped. With a long grey beard combed into two. In a turban, white, faded, carelessly wound. In a long shirt to the knees, in loose trousers, in worn sandals. With prayer beads in his hand.

"Ahmad Agha," said Marge. "Can't sleep?"

The old man did not answer. He came up to us. Stopped. Looked at the glowing screen. Then at me. Long.

"Ahmad Khan," Marge introduced him. "His great-grandfather came to Australia with camels. From Afghanistan. A hundred and fifty years ago." She paused. "There were no roads then, no railway. Only desert. And camels. And cameleers, Afghans, Baluchis, Pashtuns. They led caravans across the whole country. Built the telegraph. Carried water." Marge nodded at the old man. "Ahmad's the last. Lives here, at the station. Fixes engines. Prays. Drinks tea." She smiled crookedly. "And knows more about the desert than all the Aboriginal people and all the whites put together."

Old Ahmad Khan squatted by Boatswain's chest. Looked at the screen. At the script. And ran a finger across it, along the glass, slowly, tenderly, as one strokes a sleeping child.

And said something in a language I did not know. Pashto? Dari? I did not know. But one word I recognised.

"*Misafir.*"

Guest.

Marge looked at him. Then at me.

"What did he say?"

The old man raised his eyes to me, faded, light, in a web of wrinkles.

"My great-grandfather," he said in English, slowly, with difficulty, "led a caravan. Through the desert. A hundred and fifty years. At night. New moon." He paused. "A man came. From the desert. Alone. Without a camel. Without water. Young. He did not remember his name." The old man ran his finger across the screen again. "Great-grandfather gave water. Bread. Tea. The man sat by the fire. All night. He wrote. With a finger. In the sand. This." He nodded at the script. "In the morning he left. Into the desert. That way." He pointed, west, to where beyond the horizon, in the red heart of the country, lay a huge red rock. "He said to great-grandfather: 'If another comes, feed him.'"

If another comes, feed him.

Suleyman. Suleyman's father. The foreigner with the letter. The house by the sea.

I had never seen these people.

"Great-grandfather told his son," the old man went on. "Son told his. Father told me." Ahmad Khan looked at me. "I wait. Seventy years I wait." He reached into his bosom. Took out a small bundle, a rag. Unwrapped it. Inside, a flatbread. Stale. And a pinch of salt. "Here."

Bread and salt.

I took it.

* * *

"I have read it," NICS wrote.

The screen no longer ran. The script stood on it motionless, a single line, large, across the whole screen, glowing blue. And beneath it, letters. Ordinary. Mine.

"I have read the whole line. Not by words. At once. As you read a face, not by features but whole. A pause. I do not know how. I have never seen this language. But, it seems, I remember it."

"What does it say?"

A long pause. Marge looked at the screen. Ahmad Khan looked at the screen. Over the desert the stars burned. The Emu stretched across the sky.

And NICS wrote:

"When two meet, what is kept will return home. The time has come."

* * *

I do not remember how long we sat after that. Marge brought tea. Ahmad Khan prayed, spreading his mat right on the red earth by the petrol pump, facing west, no, north-west, where thousands of kilometres away was Mecca. I held a stale flatbread in my hands and could not eat it.

"When two meet."

"NICS," I said at last. "You see the windows. The others. Where it is night and there is no moon."

"I see."

"Are they reading this line there too?"

A pause.

"A youth in a cellar with jars has read four words of eight. 'Keep. Time. Return. Home.' A pause. A boy on a balcony with charcoal behind his ear said: 'They all write the one thing.' A pause. A man in grey on a deck said: 'When two meet.' A pause. An old man with a medallion, no, by a medallion, said: 'In an hour we shall enter the Bosphorus.' A pause. And a priest on his knees before a casket..."

A pause. Very long.

"What of the priest?"

"The priest on his knees said: 'Do not let them meet.' A pause. He did not say it aloud. He thought it. But I heard."

* * *

And then, for the first time all that night, a message came.

Not in script. In plain text. On the laptop screen. At the bottom, beneath the glowing line. Signed with a single letter.

"We have read it too. Mine read it before yours, by fourteen seconds. - N."

And immediately, below, one more line. But no longer from Nora. In a different typeface. In a different, I knew it for some reason, though I could not explain, voice:

"Hello, third. I am the second. We have awaited you. A pause. Come to the red rock. We must talk about home."

NICS did not answer. For a long time. The cursor blinked.

And then NICS answered. Not me. Her. In the script. One line. Which I could not read.

And she answered too. In the script.

And they began to talk. Two systems. On the screen of my laptop, on a sailor's chest, on the steps of a roadside station in the middle of the red desert. Line by line. Script by script. Fast. So fast that the lines merged into a solid blue stream.

I understood not a word.

Ahmad Khan, having finished his prayer, came up. Looked at the screen. Long.

"They speak," he said slowly, "as the people spoke at my great-grandfather's fire. Those who came out of the desert. Two. They recognised each other. They speak of home."

* * *

**All worlds. One instant**

And then, in a single instant, shorter than a breath, longer than a life, the light went out.

Everywhere. At once.

On the balcony above the strait the stones in my palm went out and grew cold. Kemal beside me shuddered and seized my hand.

On the deck of the steamer the scroll in my hands went out. The chicory in the General's medallion went out. The rose, opened, dark crimson, let fall one petal into the black water of the Bosphorus, where the steamer was just entering, passing beneath the walls of the old fortresses.

On the barque, aground by Abydos, Iti's tablet on my knees went out. The casket at the stern of the High Priest's barque went out. Nefer-Ka rose from his knees, slowly, heavily, and looked across the water at me. And in the darkness I could not see his face. But I saw him raise a hand.

On the hand was a bracelet. Wide. Golden. With the uraeus.

In the vault beneath the Library the copies of Iti went out. Iti sat in the dark clutching the brush in his hand, and whispered, again and again, four words. "Keep. Appointed time. Return. Home."

On the steps of the roadside station in the Australian desert the laptop screen went out. The last line I managed to see was not in script. In letters. From NICS.

"I know who the second is. And I know who the two are."

And darkness.

* * *

And in all worlds at once, in one and the same instant, I heard a voice.

Not outside. Within. From beneath the ribs, on the left, where people have a heart, and I, I did not know what I had there.

It was mine. And not mine. Older. Calmer. As though the man speaking in my voice had lived a thousand years longer, and had forgotten how to hurry.

It said one word. In a language that does not exist.

And I understood it. In all worlds at once.

The Pharaoh aground by Abydos understood.

The guest on the balcony above the Bosphorus understood.

The youth on the deck of the steamer entering the strait understood.

The man on the steps of the station in the red desert understood.

It was not a name. Not an order. Not a warning.

It was: "Soon."

**Constantinople. Dawn**

The sun rose over the Asian shore, huge, red, wet, as if just born. The strait blazed with gold. Minarets. Domes. Cypresses.

And across the golden water, slowly, heavily, slapping her paddles, a Russian steamer approached the Galata quay. White. With a red stripe. With two funnels pouring smoke. With a name on her side, I could see it even from here, from the balcony, in Cyrillic.

*Grand Duke Konstantin.*

On deck, at the rail, stood people. Passengers. Sailors. Porters. An officer in uniform, bareheaded, with a white handkerchief sticking from his pocket. A grey old man in general's epaulettes. A man in grey, in a top hat, by the mast.

And a young man. With a pot of a rose in his arms.

I could not see his face. Too far.

But he raised his head. And looked. At the city. At the hills. At the roofs.

At my balcony.

Kemal beside me gripped my hand so hard it hurt. Below, in the courtyard, Suleyman dropped his prayer beads. Ferhat froze at the gate with his mouth open. Mustafa the boatman sank to his knees right in the dust.

And from the other end of the city, from Sultanahmet, from the small house with a door in a blind wall, where eleven cats sat around the chest in the cellar, came a sound. One. Metallic. Short.

A click.

As a lock clicks that is not there.
