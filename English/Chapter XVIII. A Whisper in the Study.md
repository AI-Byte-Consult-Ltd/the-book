Chapter XVIII
A Whisper in the Study

I started the notebook the very day after the salon - a plain one, in blue cloth binding, bought by Konstantin at my request in the district town, in the very shop where my aunt bought her French novels. Doctor Strauch had told me to write everything down. What I did, whom I spoke with, what I saw. Every evening. Briefly.

I wrote.

"Wednesday. In the morning walked in the garden. Stefan was tying up the roses - did not look at me. In the afternoon read in the library about heraldry. Konstantin twice tried to drag me off to play billiards. In the evening Aunt was silent at supper. The General has gone to town on business."

"Thursday. Rain. All day in the library. Dunyasha brought tea with raspberry, said I was pale as the White Lady. Mitka told how at night he again saw a light in the left wing. Agafya Tikhonovna boxed his ears."

"Friday. Nothing in particular."

And on Saturday morning, opening the notebook to write down the previous evening, I saw an entry that I did not remember.

In my handwriting. In my ink - dark blue, from the inkwell on my desk. Dated Friday. Beneath the line "Nothing in particular."

"Drove to the district town. To Sventsitsky. Talked for three hours. He took notes. I told him about the city by the sea. He asked how I knew. I do not know how I know."

I reread it five times. Ten. The letters did not change. The handwriting - mine. The pressure of the pen - mine. Even the blot at the end, where I always linger with the nib before setting the full stop - mine.

I had not gone to town. I had been at home. All day. I remembered: Friday, nothing in particular. I remembered writing those two words and closing the notebook.

"If one day you find in the notebook an entry that you do not remember," Doctor Strauch had said, "come to me."

* * *

Before going to the doctor, I went to the stables.

The coachman Tikhon, huge, red-bearded, with hands like shovels and a voice that made the horses squat, was cleaning my aunt's carriage in the yard and humming something endless and mournful about a driver freezing to death in the steppe. Seeing me, he straightened up and took off his cap.

"Good morning, young master. Off to town again?"

Something inside me snapped.

"Again?"

"Why, yes," Tikhon scratched his beard. "Yesterday I drove you. After dinner. To the district town. To the 'Golden Anchor' inn. You were there some three hours, I drank tea at the tavern opposite. Then you came out - and I drove you back." He paused, looking me over. "All the way you were silent, young master. There - silent, back - silent. And on the way back you laughed too. To yourself. Quietly, like." He lowered his voice. "I thought - maybe some sickness. Wanted to tell the mistress. But didn't. Thought - well, who knows, maybe there's a young lady at the inn."

"Tikhon," I said, trying to keep my voice from trembling. "Yesterday I... how did I tell you where to go? What did I say?"

Tikhon looked at me with a long, heavy look - the look with which peasants regard a master in whom, plainly, something is wrong in the head, but neither rank nor conscience allows them to say so outright.

"You said, young master," he said slowly, "'Take me to the one who collects tales. I must tell him mine.' I understood that to mean - that professor who was at the salon. Everyone knows where he's put up." He put on his cap. "And you said something else - a strange thing, I didn't understand it. You said: 'If I forget that I went - don't remind me. Let the notebook remind me.'"

I stood in the middle of the stable yard, smelling of hay, manure and horse sweat, and felt the ground under my feet become unreliable, like the deck of a ship in a storm.

"Thank you, Tikhon," I said at last.

"Not at all, young master." He took up his brush again. And, already turned away, he added without looking at me: "If you go again, young master - warn me. I'll harness Gnedok, the bay, he's gentle. Yesterday you ordered Voronok, and Voronok is hot-tempered. We nearly went into the ditch by the mill."

* * *

Doctor Strauch received me in the small outbuilding which my aunt had assigned him for a consulting room and dispensary - two little rooms smelling of carbolic, dried herbs and cigars, crammed with cabinets of flasks, books in three languages, and a stuffed owl which, by his account, had come to him with the practice from his predecessor and which he had been meaning to throw out for twenty years.

He heard me out in silence, without interrupting, sitting in an armchair with an unlit cigar in his fingers. He read the entry in the notebook. Then again. Then took a magnifying glass and examined the handwriting.

"Yours," he said at last. "No doubt whatever. Even that habitual blot of yours."

"I did not write it."

"You wrote it, young man. You simply do not remember it." Strauch laid down the glass. "These, you know, are different things. The first is frightening. The second is merely interesting."

"Is it interesting to you?"

"To me, I confess, extraordinarily interesting," said the doctor without a shadow of embarrassment. "For thirty years I have been treating the diarrhoeas, rheumatisms and migraines of provincial ladies. And here - such a thing." He struck a match, at last lit the cigar, spitting on his own prohibition, and blew smoke at the ceiling. "Allow me to tell you a story. Not a tale - a medical case. I read of it in a German journal some twenty years ago. It was recorded by an English physician who observed it."

"Tell me."

"There lived in America, in Pennsylvania, a girl named Mary Reynolds. A pastor's daughter. Quiet, pious, melancholy, bookish. She kept company with no one, walked alone in the woods, read the Bible. At eighteen she once fell asleep - and slept almost a whole day. And when she woke - she knew neither her parents, nor her brothers, nor her own home. She could not read. Could not write. Knew not a single word - like an infant. She was taught everything anew. And she learned - quickly, in a few weeks. But she was now a different girl. Merry. Bold. Noisy. She ran about the woods, laughed, teased her brothers, feared neither bears, nor snakes, nor the pastor. She wrote - in a different hand. She laughed at her former self, whom she knew only from the accounts of her kin, as at some tedious distant relative."

He paused, shaking off the ash.

"And after five weeks she fell asleep again. And woke - as she had been before. Quiet, pious, melancholy. And remembered nothing of those five weeks. Not a single day." Strauch looked at me over his gold spectacles. "And so all her life, young man. There and back. Two people in one body. Each not remembering the other. Each considering the other a dream."

"How did it end?"

"It ended with her, by the age of thirty-five, ceasing to return. She remained the second. The merry one. And lived as her for thirty more years, until her death." The doctor drew on his cigar. "She was asked: and where did that first one go? She answered: 'I don't know. Perhaps she fell asleep. Perhaps she went where it is better for her.'"

I was silent. The owl on the cabinet looked at me with glass yellow eyes.

"Do you think the same is happening to me?" I asked at last.

"I think nothing," said Strauch. "I am a German. I observe and record." He took from his pocket his small black book, which he showed to no one, and opened it. "Here. During the four days of your illness after the railings. You raved three times. The first night you spoke French. The second - in a language I do not know. I recorded the sound." He showed me a page covered in Latin letters which composed themselves into some nonsense: "...sa-la-am... mi-sa-fir... bu-yu-run..." "And on the third night you pronounced one word. Distinctly. Three times."

"Which?"

Strauch closed the little book.

"'Menes,'" he said. "You said 'Menes.' I looked in the encyclopaedia. It is a name. Egyptian. Of the first pharaoh."

* * *

Cold rose from somewhere under the ribs, on the left. Familiar. Old. Which no one in this house had but me.

"I have never read about Egypt," I said.

"I know," said Strauch. "I asked your aunt. She said there is not a single book about Egypt in the library. Not one." He paused. "And her face at that moment was as though I had asked her about a dead man."

He rose, went to the window, stood looking out at the garden.

"Would you like a riddle, young man? A German one. It is set to medical students at Heidelberg, in the first year, in the anatomical theatre. So that it is less frightening."

"I would."

"'Ich habe Städte, aber keine Häuser. Ich habe Wälder, aber keine Bäume. Ich habe Wasser, aber keine Fische.'" He turned. "'I have cities, but no houses. I have forests, but no trees. I have water, but no fish. What am I?'"

I thought.

"A map," I said.

"A map," Strauch nodded. "And do you know why it is set to medical students?" He tapped a finger against his temple. "Because memory is a map too. On it is everything - cities, forests, rivers. But they are not the cities themselves. They are the picture of them. And sometimes the map shows cities that do not exist. Or hides those that do." He paused. "And sometimes, young man, a man finds in his head the map of a foreign country. And does not know where he got it."

* * *

The "Golden Anchor" inn in the district town turned out to be a two-storey wooden house with a peeling signboard on which a golden anchor had once been painted and on which there was now something like a rusty poker. Tikhon drove me there in an hour on gentle Gnedok, squinting over his shoulder all the way, as if checking whether I would begin laughing to myself again.

Sventsitsky occupied a room on the second floor - the cheapest, under the roof, with a window on the market square. He opened the door at once, as though he had been waiting - in a waistcoat, without a frock coat, with a pen behind his ear and an ink stain on his chin. The room was heaped with notebooks, sheets of paper, books, some wooden toys, embroidered towels, clay whistles and bundles of dried herbs. On the table stood a samovar, long gone cold.

"You have come back," he said simply. Without surprise. "Come in. Sit down. Tea? It is cold, but strong."

"Professor," I said, not sitting. "Was I with you yesterday?"

Sventsitsky gave me a long, attentive look from his clear eyes.

"You were," he said. "Three hours." He paused. "You do not remember."

"I do not."

"I thought so." He nodded. "Yesterday you said you would not remember. You said: 'If I come tomorrow and ask whether I was here - show me what you wrote down. Do not tell me. Show me.'"

He went to the table, rummaged in a heap of papers and took out a notebook - thick, in an oilcloth cover, filled with small, hurried handwriting. Opened it at a bookmark.

"Here. I wrote down everything you said. Word for word. As I write down from old men in the villages." He held the notebook out to me. "Read it yourself."

I took the notebook. My hands were trembling.

"...says he remembers a city by the sea. Southern. Hot. Houses wooden above, stone below. Washing on lines between windows. Boys carry bread on trays on their heads. Old men play dice on a carpet. Says that there they call him 'guest.' In their language. Says that there he has two stones - black and white. That they grow hot. Says these stones are 'eyes.' I ask: whose? Says: I don't remember. Perhaps mine."

"...speaks of columns. Enormous. A stone forest. Says he walked between them, and it was dark, and someone raised a hand. Says that was long ago. Or will be. Does not know."

"...speaks of a man who builds a machine. Who sits nights before a luminous window, and the window answers him. Says: 'He asked her who she was. And she asked him who he was.' Says: 'I know he is me, too. But I do not understand how.'"

Next came a drawing. Sventsitsky had evidently copied it from something I had done myself: two circles - one blackened, the other empty - and a wavy line between them.

And still lower, larger, circled several times:

"IMPORTANT. I ask: where are you from? He answers: 'From the circle. The circle has no beginning.' Then laughed and said: 'No. This one has. I have simply forgotten it.'"

I closed the notebook. It was very quiet in the room. Somewhere below, in the market square, a stall-woman was scolding a customer over eggs.

"I know none of this," I said. My voice sounded alien, as though coming from far off. "I have never been in a southern city. I know no columns. I know no man with a machine."

"I know," said Sventsitsky gently. "Yesterday you said: 'Tomorrow I will tell you that I know none of this. Do not argue with me. I shall be right. And yesterday's I is right too. We are both right.'"

* * *

He poured me cold tea all the same. I drank it without tasting. Sventsitsky sat down opposite, on the edge of a bed heaped with embroidered towels, and was silent for a long time.

"Allow me to tell you something," he said at last. "Not for consolation. For information."

"Tell me."

"In the villages," he began, "especially in the North, in the Olonets, Arkhangelsk and Vologda governorates, there is a word - 'obmiranie.' It is said of women, most often of women, though it happens to men also - who suddenly fall as if dead. They do not breathe. Do not stir. Grow cold. Lie a day, two, sometimes a week. They are already about to be buried. And then they rise. And tell where they have been."

"Where?"

"In the next world." Sventsitsky shrugged. "So they say. They are called 'obmiranki.' In twenty years I have recorded more than thirty such accounts. And what is astonishing is that they are alike. The woman from near Kargopol and the woman from near Totma, who have never set eyes on each other and in all their lives never travelled farther than their own churchyard. They tell of a meadow. Of a high light. Of a river that cannot be crossed. Of a guide - tall, shining, without a face. Of how they were shown their past lives. And their future ones."

"Future?"

"One old woman near Vytegra," said Sventsitsky, "swooned at twenty, in her youth. Lay three days. And when she rose she told how she had seen iron horses that run along an iron road and breathe smoke. That she had seen people talking through a wire a hundred versts apart. That she had seen..." he smiled, "women in men's trousers, smoking cigarettes right in the street. She was taken for mad. She lived to a hundred. I recorded her ten years ago. The iron horses were running by then. " He paused. "And the wire and the women in trousers - perhaps they will come running yet."

Women in trousers. Women in breeches, like men. In a sunlit garden. There, beyond the railings.

I set down the cup. My hands trembled again.

"And what do they say, these obmiranki," I asked, "when they are asked why they were taken there?"

Sventsitsky looked at me.

"They all say the same thing," he said. "Almost word for word. 'They told us to remember.'"

* * *

Already on the threshold, when Tikhon below was impatiently calling to Gnedok, Sventsitsky detained me by the hand.

"One more thing," he said quietly. "Yesterday, on leaving, you set me a riddle. I did not sleep all night - could not solve it. Perhaps today you know the answer."

"Which?"

He closed his eyes, recalling.

"'One brother goes forward, the other backward. They live in one house. They have never seen each other. But one cannot take a step without the other.'" Sventsitsky opened his eyes. "You said it was set to you by a red-bearded trader in the city by the sea. And that he himself had forgotten the answer."

I knew no red-bearded trader. I did not know the answer. But somewhere very deep, under the ribs, on the left, something stirred - as if someone within me wanted to say: "The two brothers are you. The one who goes forward and the one who goes backward. In one house. In one body."

"I don't know," I said.

"A pity," said Sventsitsky. "Yesterday's you, it seems, knew. But did not say. Said: 'Tomorrow's will guess it himself. When the appointed time comes.'"

The appointed time. The appointed time again.

* * *

At home a piece of gossip awaited me. It was brought, of course, by Dunyasha - together with the tea, on a tray, that same evening, in a whisper, glancing at the door.

"Young master," she said, setting out the cups with trembling hands, "only please don't be angry. But in the servants' hall they're saying..."

"What are they saying, Dunyasha?"

"They're saying that you, young master..." she blushed to the roots of her hair, "that you go to town to see a young lady. At the 'Golden Anchor.' That Tikhon drove you there and waited three hours. And that you came out of there cheerful." She caught her breath. "Marfa the cook says - to a young lady. Mitka says - to a gypsy, to have your fortune told. And Agafya Tikhonovna says..."

"What does Agafya Tikhonovna say?"

Dunyasha lowered her voice to a whisper that was no longer audible at all:

"Agafya Tikhonovna says - not to a young lady and not to a gypsy. She says - to one who will tell you who you are. And that the mistress fears this worse than fire." Dunyasha swallowed. "And Agafya Tikhonovna also says that someone has touched the crape on the portrait in the gallery again. The night before last. She came in the morning - and the crape was torn down. Entirely. And lying on the floor. And on the portrait..." She fell silent.

"What on the portrait?"

"I don't know, young master. Agafya Tikhonovna won't say. She just hung the crape back and crossed herself three times. And now she carries the gallery keys on her belt." Dunyasha took the empty tray and backed toward the door. "And another thing, young master, Olga Kirillovna, the one in lilac - she goes all over the province saying that Artemia Aristarkhovna's ward has lost his mind. That he was found in the left wing with white eyes. That he talks with ghosts."

"With white eyes?"

"That's what she says, young master." Dunyasha was already in the doorway. "Only don't believe it. Olga Kirillovna always embellishes everything. Last year her cousin died of a head cold - and she told everybody it was consumption and unhappy love."

She slipped out. I remained alone with my cooling tea.

The night before last. The crape torn down. The night before last I had slept - I remembered sleeping. But I also remembered that on Friday nothing in particular had happened.

* * *

Konstantin burst in on me an hour later - without knocking, as always, with tousled hair, in an unbuttoned uniform tunic, with a pack of cards in one hand and a bottle of liqueur in the other.

"Enough sulking, mon cher!" he announced from the threshold. "The whole house is walking on tiptoe as if you were dying. Auntie is silent. The General is silent. Strauch is smoking his cigars right in the library - unheard of! Even Agafya Tikhonovna is not scolding, which is a sure sign of the end of the world. I have come to entertain you. We shall play charades. Just the two of us."

"Two do not play charades."

"We shall be the first," Konstantin said imperturbably, settling on the edge of my bed. "I'll set it. Listen." He cleared his throat, struck the pose of an actor of the Alexandrinsky Theatre and declaimed:

"'My first syllable is the animal that barks at the gate.
My second is the question we ask when we do not know who is coming.
And the whole is that which guards us from misfortune
When we sleep and see strange dreams.'"

I thought.

"Dog... Who... No." I thought some more. "'Dog-who'? What is that?"

Konstantin triumphantly raised a finger.

"'Dog' is dog. 'Who' is who. And the whole is - 'Pestun'! No, wait..." He frowned. "'Dog-who'... 'Dogwho'... Damn. In my head it was 'Watchman,' I thought it up on the road while riding from the regiment, and on the way I lost the syllables." He burst out laughing and fell back on the pillows. "Fine, the charade was bad. But the answer is good. Watchman. The one who guards us from misfortune when we sleep."

"And who is my watchman?"

Konstantin stopped laughing. He looked at me - for a moment serious, without his usual mockery.

"I am," he said simply. "Who else."

He looked away and began shuffling the cards - too fast, too carefully.

"Konstantin," I said. "Do you remember what you promised? After the salon?"

He froze.

"I remember. You promised to tell me first if you remembered."

"I remembered," I said. "Not everything. But something. Yesterday I went to town. To Sventsitsky. And I do not remember it. The notebook remembers. Tikhon remembers. Sventsitsky remembers. And I do not."

Konstantin slowly laid the cards on the blanket.

"I know," he said quietly.

"You know?"

"I saw you leave. From the window. You got into the carriage and did not look back. I shouted to you - you did not hear. Or pretended not to." He paused. "I went to the General."

"Why?"

Konstantin was silent a long while, looking at the cards.

"Because I did not know whom else to go to, mon cher," he said at last. "Because I was frightened."

* * *

That evening the General stayed overnight at my aunt's - he always stayed when business detained him in the province, and he was given the study of my aunt's late husband in the north wing: a large, gloomy room with dark oak panelling, bookcases to the ceiling, an enormous writing desk and a fireplace over which hung crossed sabres.

I did not mean to eavesdrop. I was going to the library for a book - the one on heraldry, which I had begun to read on Thursday, if the notebook was to be believed. The library was at the end of the same corridor. And the door of the study stood ajar - by exactly a hand's breadth, so that a narrow yellow strip of light from the fireplace fell across the corridor floor.

And I heard voices.

"...not for the first time," the General was saying. Quietly, evenly, but with that particular steel note which I had heard in him only once - at the salon, when he turned Sventsitsky out. "Do you understand, Konstantin? Not the first."

"I understand only that Your Excellency explains nothing to me," answered Konstantin. His voice sounded unfamiliar - without its usual mockery, sharp, almost rude. "He forgets names. Forgets what he did yesterday. Speaks in his sleep in languages no one knows. Strauch writes it down. Auntie is silent. You are silent. And he goes to town to a professor who collects tales, and tells him... what? What does he tell him there?"

"I do not know what he tells Sventsitsky," said the General.

"But you know why."

A long pause. The logs crackled in the fireplace.

"Last time," the General said at last, and his voice dropped, so that I had to hold my breath to make out the words, "last time it was the same. First the names. Then the days. Then the languages. Then the railings."

"Last time?" Konstantin nearly shouted it. "When was last time? He is young! What last time?!"

Silence.

"Your Excellency," said Konstantin in a different voice - quiet, almost pleading. "Pavel Nikolaevich. I am his friend. Perhaps his only friend. I promised him - do you understand? - promised that if he remembered, he would tell me first. And today he told me. And I... I can answer him nothing. Because I know nothing."

"It is better so," said the General.

"For whom?!"

"For him." The General's voice became very soft. "We promised, Kostya. I and Artemia Aristarkhovna. Long ago. We promised that he would not learn. Not before the time."

"Promised whom?"

A pause. So long that I thought - that is all, the conversation is over, and now the door will open and I shall be found here, pressed to the wall in the dark corridor.

"The one who brought him," said the General.

I stopped breathing.

"Who brought him?" Konstantin whispered. "Brought him where? From where?"

"That I will not tell you," said the General. "Not because I do not want to. Because I do not know myself. I saw him once. He stood by the railings. On the night when..." He fell silent. Then continued, heavily, as though lifting each word like a stone: "He was tall. Very tall. Grey. I did not see his face. He held him by the hand. And he said to Artemia - not in words, I do not know how to describe it - said that we were to keep him safe. Until the appointed time. And not to tell. Because if we told sooner - he would go. Altogether. To where he had come from."

I stood in the dark, my forehead pressed against the cold oak panel, feeling neither my hands nor my feet.

"And if he remembers on his own?" Konstantin asked barely audibly.

"Then," said the General, "it will no longer be our affair. Then the time will have come."

"And what then?"

A long, long silence.

"I don't know, Kostya," said the General, and in his voice, for the first time in all the time I had known him, there sounded something like fear. "For thirty years I have walked the world. I have seen Sitka and Samarkand, Peking and Havana. I have seen a wave higher than the masts sweep away a city. I have seen an old native sing a song by which one can walk across a whole country. I thought I had seen everything." A pause. "But I do not know what will happen when he remembers. I know only that Artemia fears it more than death. And that I..." he faltered, "I do too."

* * *

A floorboard creaked under my foot.

The voices in the study fell silent at once. I heard a chair pushed back. Footsteps - heavy, quick, the General's - toward the door.

I did not run. I do not know why. I simply stood in the corridor, in the narrow strip of light, and when the door flew open the General saw me - face to face, two paces away.

We looked at each other. For a long time. He - huge, grey-haired, in an unbuttoned tunic, without epaulettes, with a face which in the firelight behind him seemed carved from dark wood. I - I do not know how. White, probably.

From behind his shoulder Konstantin looked out - and froze.

"How much did you hear?" the General asked quietly.

I could have lied. Said: nothing, I just came up, I am going to the library. He might even have believed it. Or pretended to.

"Enough," I said.

The General closed his eyes. For a moment. Then opened them.

"Good," he said. And stepped aside, letting me into the study. "Then come in. Since it is so."

I went in. Konstantin retreated to the fireplace, not taking his eyes off me. The General closed the door - tightly, to a click - and turned the key in the lock.

"Sit," he said, indicating the armchair by the fireplace.

I sat.

The General stood over the fireplace, looking into the fire. Then he reached into the inner pocket of his tunic and took out two things. He laid them on the mantelpiece, side by side.

A wooden raven with a sun in its beak. And a paper crane - white, yellowed, worn.

"The raven that stole the sun," said the General, not turning. "And the crane that must be folded back into a sheet. I have carried them twenty years. They were given to me by people who did not know each other, at different ends of the world. And both said the same thing." He turned to me. "The raven: 'Maybe it will come in useful.' The crane: 'When you find the one who will fold it back.'" The General paused. "I think that is you."

"Me?"

"I don't know," said the General. "I know nothing. I know only that I promised to be silent. And I shall be silent." He looked at me heavily, directly, like a soldier. "But I will not lie to you. Never. Ask me whatever you like. If I can answer - I shall answer. If I cannot - I shall say that I cannot. But I will not lie."

I looked at him. At the raven. At the crane. At Konstantin by the fireplace, pale as linen.

A thousand questions crowded in my throat. Who brought me? From where? Who is the tall grey man by the railings? What happened "last time"? Who am I?

I asked one.

"Aunt," I said. "Is she my own kin?"

The General was silent for a long while. The fire crackled. Konstantin did not breathe.

"That I cannot say," the General answered at last. "But I can say something else." He paused. "She loves you as she has loved no one in the world. Not even her husband. Not even..." He faltered. "No one. That is the truth. Is that enough?"

"No," I said.

"I know," said the General. "It would not be enough for me either."

* * *

He set me a riddle. Later, when the three of us were already sitting by the fire, and Konstantin had poured liqueur into the glasses, and no one drank, and the silence had become unbearable, the General suddenly said, looking into the flames:

"Near Khiva, in the sands, a Turkmen old man set it to me. The one who gave me water. And the scroll. I have never told anyone the answer. Perhaps I shall tell you."

"Set it."

"'I go away every evening and come every morning. I am awaited, but no one knows where I was in the night. I am one and the same - and each time different. I cannot be held, but can be met. What am I?'"

Konstantin thought.

"The sun."

"Everyone answers so," said the General. "The old man said - wrong."

"Then what?" I asked.

The General looked at me - long, heavily.

"A guest," he said. "The old man said: 'A guest. He comes, goes, and does not remember where he has been. He cannot be held. But if you meet him as you should - he will leave you what he brought.'" The General paused. "And then the old man gave me the scroll and said: 'You are not the one it was brought for. But you will meet him. Give it to him.'"

He rose, went to the writing desk, pulled out the bottom drawer - the one that locked with a separate key hanging on his watch chain. Took out something wrapped in an old, faded silk kerchief. Laid it on the desk.

But did not unwrap it.

"Not today," he said, closing the drawer again. "Not today. I promised - until the appointed time. But the time, it seems, is nearer than I thought." He turned to Konstantin. "And you, Kostya - do not leave him. Not by a step. If he goes to town again - go with him. If he goes to the left wing - go with him. If..."

"I know," Konstantin interrupted. His voice was hoarse. "I know, Your Excellency. I have not left him as it is."

The General looked at him. Then at me. Then at him again. And suddenly - for the first time that whole evening - he smiled. Briefly, almost like a father.

"I know you haven't," he said. "Do you think I am blind?"

Konstantin flushed to the roots of his hair and stared into the fire.

* * *

I returned to my room after midnight. Konstantin walked me to the door - in silence, as one escorts the wounded - and, before leaving, stood on the threshold.

"Mon cher," he said quietly. "If you go somewhere again and do not remember - let the notebook remember. But let it be written there that I was beside you. All right?"

"All right."

"Promise?"

"I promise."

He nodded and left. I closed the door, sat at the desk, opened the blue notebook to write down this day - and froze.

On the clean page, beneath yesterday's entry about the journey I did not remember, there was already a drawing. Fresh. The ink still glistened.

Two circles. One blackened. The other empty. And a wavy line between them.

And under the drawing - three words. In my handwriting. With my blot at the end.

"He is near. Wait."

I had not written this. I had only just come in. I was holding the pen in my hand - dry.

I sat over the notebook for a long time, until the candle burned out. Then, in the dark, I took the dry pen, dipped it in the inkwell and wrote below, trying to keep my hand from shaking:

"Saturday. Learned that I went to town. Learned that I spoke of a city by the sea and of columns. Learned that someone brought me. Learned that Aunt loves me. Konstantin was beside me."

I set the full stop. The blot lay in its place.

Outside the window, in the garden, among the rosebushes, Stefan's lantern flickered - he was again circling the white roses beneath the drawing-room windows. And then it stopped. And stood for a long time motionless, the lantern raised toward my window.
