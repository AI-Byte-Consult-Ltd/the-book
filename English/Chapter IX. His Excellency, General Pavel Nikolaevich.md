Chapter IX
His Excellency, General Pavel Nikolaevich

The darkness beneath me was not dense - rather viscous, undulating, like water in a deep well, to the bottom of which I was slowly sinking, unable either to hasten or to slow the descent. From somewhere above, from that part of consciousness that still clung to the surface, scraps of sounds reached me - whether the voice of the monk, or the rustle of grass under someone's hurrying steps, or a distant, muffled cry calling out my name - but to make out anything coherent in them was impossible, as it is impossible to make out words spoken through a depth of water.

My thoughts, torn away from my own body, limply sprawled somewhere on the grass - in a garden which, if the monk was to be believed, no one had trimmed for more than a hundred years - drifted off in a direction wholly unconnected with the dreadful phrase I had just heard - as though reason, unable to bear its weight, had hastened to take shelter behind the first, safest thought it could find in its storerooms.

That thought, for a reason unknown even to me, turned out to be General Pavel Nikolaevich.

Perhaps because of all the people I knew he alone seemed to me a man who could not be lost. He was like the stone wall of an old house: however the wallpaper and the guests might change, the wall stayed in place. If the Castle has been empty for a hundred years - then where is the General? The thought was absurd, childish - and yet I seized on it as a drowning man seizes a plank.

I knew a great deal about him - far more, perhaps, than a mere family acquaintance ought to know - not because he himself was talkative about his own affairs (on the contrary, Pavel Nikolaevich was extremely sparing in speaking of himself), but because over the long years spent in my aunt's house scraps of his biography had accumulated in me of themselves - from table talk, from the Colonel's hints, from rare, chance-dropped phrases of my aunt herself, usually pronounced with that special, respectful intonation with which one honours only those whose past weighs far more than their present ranks.

* * *

He was an acting aide-de-camp general at the court of Alexander the Second - a rank granted not for mere length of service but for the particular trust with which the Sovereign honoured a bare few. He headed, besides, the Cabinet of Ministers of the Russian Empire, and in all questions concerning the policy of the Near and Far East his word weighed almost more than the word of certain ambassadors who had sat for years in distant missions.

About his part in the Russo-Turkish War of 'twenty-eight and 'twenty-nine they composed at court if not legends then something very close to them: then a young officer, he had managed to distinguish himself in such a way that his career afterward shot forward with a swiftness that falls to few. The Colonel, perhaps the only one who allowed himself to recall those years aloud in my presence, told how it was precisely then, under fire, that Pavel Nikolaevich first showed that special, icy calculation which later made him indispensable at negotiating tables far more peaceful but no less dangerous.

The experience of armed clashes on the distant frontiers of the Empire and in foreign lands made of him a man without whose opinion afterward no question of any importance touching relations with the Porte was decided.

The Ottoman Empire, as far as I remembered from table talk, was coming apart at the seams: torn by petty ethnic feuds, weakened by wars and uprisings for independence on its outskirts, it had long ceased to be the formidable neighbour it had been reputed a century earlier. And yet on the southern borders of our own Empire the Porte remained a rival serious enough that each of its internal crises echoed in our corridors of power as well.

Not long ago - by historical measure, of course, not by the measure of a single human life - the Egyptian viceroy, Pasha Muhammad Ali, had pursued his own policy, ever more independent of Istanbul, and, taking advantage of the Porte's weakness, undertaken a military campaign that came near to crowning itself with the capture of Constantinople itself. Only the timely help of the Russian Empire - and, by general admission, the personal participation of Pavel Nikolaevich in the negotiations - kept the Sultan on his throne at that critical hour. Since then, it was whispered, the Sultan had felt toward the General a gratitude rarely accorded any foreign subject, and Pavel Nikolaevich's influence extended not only at the court of Alexander the Second but in the Porte itself and, perhaps, at other royal courts of Europe and Asia.

He alone - and no one else of the entire suite - invariably accompanied the Imperial family on its journeys to the summer residence of the Kingdom of Siam; he alone was present at all the negotiations and trade agreements which the Sovereign so loved to conclude in his beloved Siamese palace.

* * *

But all this was information that any capital gossip could relate. I knew something else - what the General told himself, on rare evenings when guests were few, the fire burned hot, and my aunt, leaning back in her chair, asked: "Pavel Nikolaevich, do tell us something. Only not about the war."

He never refused her. Anyone else - he refused. Her - never.

One such story I remember so clearly it is as though I had heard it yesterday.

"About Sitka?" he asked that evening, setting his cup on the saucer. "As you wish, Artemia Aristarkhovna. Though it is not a cheerful story."

"Where is it, Sitka?" Konstantin at once put in, sitting on the carpet by the fireplace because all the armchairs were taken.

"At the edge of the world, Lieutenant. At that edge to which the sun goes to spend the night. An island in America, by the coldest sea. Novo-Arkhangelsk - the capital of Russian America. Was."

"Was?" Evdokia Gerasimovna repeated, raising her eyebrows above her fan.

"Was, madam. Until that October day when I stood on the hill above the harbour and watched them lower our flag."

He was silent. The fire crackled. My aunt looked at him without taking her eyes away.

"The Sovereign sold Alaska to the Americans," said the General evenly, as one reports an accomplished fact. "For a sum about which they argue in the capital to this day, whether it was cheap or dear. I was sent to be present at the handover. As a man who knows the East - though, God sees, I had seen nothing more eastern than Sitka in my life. The journey there took more than half a year. Across all Siberia, across the Sea of Okhotsk, where a storm battered us for three days, and then across the ocean, where the fogs are such that from the stern you cannot see the bow of your own ship."

"And what is it like, this Sitka?" my aunt asked softly.

The General looked into the fire, and his face, usually so impenetrable, suddenly became almost dreamy.

"Mountains, Artemia Aristarkhovna. Mountains covered with forest to the very summits, and above them - snow, even in summer. The water in the harbour is dark as ink, and in it stand islands - dozens of islands, green, shaggy, like the backs of sleeping bears. It rains almost every day - not hard, but fine, as if the sky were weeping but shy of it. On the hill - the governor's house, wooden, large, which we called Baranov's Castle. Below - a church with a green dome, warehouses, a shipyard, little houses. And everywhere - Indians."

"Indians!" Konstantin leaned forward, his eyes lit like a boy's. "With feathers? With tomahawks?"

"Without feathers, Lieutenant," the General smiled into his moustache. "Koloshi. They call themselves Tlingit. A proud, warlike, intelligent people. Their boats are hollowed from a single cedar, so long that thirty oarsmen sit in one. And in front of their houses they set up poles - enormous, taller than this ceiling - and on the poles they carve their ancestors. Raven, eagle, bear, whale. One above another, to the very top."

"And what did they say when the flag was lowered?"

The General did not answer for a long time.

"There was one old man," he said at last. "A chief. His name, as I remember, was Kashket - though perhaps I garble it. Grey-haired, in a cloak of mountain-goat wool, woven in a pattern that dazzled the eyes. He stood beside me on the hill while the American soldiers raised their flag and our sailors lowered ours. Our flag, incidentally, got tangled in the ropes at the very top of the mast and would not come down. A sailor had to climb after it. The ladies wept. And old Kashket turned to me and said through an interpreter - through the Creole Ivan Koshkin, son of a Russian trader and an Indian woman, who served at the office and knew all the languages on that coast:

"'You have sold what did not belong to you. The land was here before you. It will be here after them. People come and go from this hill as salmon come and go from the river. Only the salmon, at least, return.'"

The silence in the drawing room became so deep that one could hear the clock ticking on the mantelpiece.

"And what did you answer him, Pavel Nikolaevich?" my aunt asked almost in a whisper.

"Nothing, Artemia Aristarkhovna." The General took his cup, discovered that it was empty, and set it back. "What can one answer a man who is right? I took off my hat and bowed to him. He nodded to me. And went off down the hill, to his boats. I never saw him again."

"And the Creole? This Koshkin?" asked Konstantin.

The General suddenly smiled - for the first time in the whole tale.

"Koshkin saw me off all the way to the ship. And at parting he gave me this."

He reached into the inner pocket of his uniform and took out something small and dark - and laid it on his palm, holding it out to the firelight. It was a wooden figurine the size of a finger: a bird with an enormous curved beak, in which it held something round and shining - whether a pebble or the sun.

"Raven," said the General. "The Koloshi have a tale: the world was dark until Raven stole the sun from an old man who kept it in a box, and released it into the sky. Koshkin said: wear it, Your Excellency. Raven is a thief, but a thief who brings light. Perhaps it will come in handy."

He put the figurine back in his pocket - and I noticed how his fingers, before releasing it, lingered for a moment, as if checking that it was in place. Since then I had more than once seen the General, lost in thought at table, touch that very pocket. As though something far more important than a wooden bird lay there.

* * *

But Alaska was not the only region to which his service carried him.

Of the Asian lands he valued most of all Turkestan, with its ancient cities steeped in centuries of history - Tashkent, Bukhara, Khiva, Samarkand - where for centuries the trading and spiritual roads of almost all mankind had crossed.

"Have you been to Samarkand, Pavel Nikolaevich?" Vera Anatolievna once asked.

"I have, madam. Right after the city was taken by our troops." The General paused. "There is a square there which they call the Registan. A sandy place. Three enormous madrasas look at one another across the square, like three old men who have been quarrelling for four hundred years and cannot come to an agreement. Their portals are faced with tiles - blue, turquoise, azure - and when the setting sun falls on them it seems that the walls are made not of clay but of sky. On one portal tigers are painted, chasing deer, and above the tigers - a sun with a human face. I was told that it is against their faith to depict living things. And yet it was depicted. And it has stood for more than two hundred years."

"And what else is there?" asked Konstantin.

"The tomb of Tamerlane, Lieutenant. Beneath a dome like a melon. The stone on the grave is dark green, almost black, jade - the largest piece of jade I have seen in my life. The old watchman told me that a curse is written on the tomb: whoever opens the grave will unleash a war more terrible than Tamerlane himself." The General shrugged. "I asked whether anyone had opened it. He laughed and said: 'Not yet, sir. But you Russians have restless hands.'"

Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich burst into loud laughter, slapping his knee. The General did not laugh.

"I remembered that watchman," he said quietly. "For some reason it seems to me that he was right."

The General and the Colonel were bound by an old friendship, dating from the Turkestan days - that special, taciturn fondness of two men who have seen enough together to cease forever to need superfluous words between them. It was enough to see them exchange a brief, almost imperceptible glance over their glasses to understand: behind that glance stood something inaccessible to anyone else at the table - to me, or even to my aunt herself.

The Colonel, once only in my memory departing from his usual loquacity, told me - in an undertone, late one evening, when the other guests had already gone - how near Khiva their small detachment found itself cut off from the main forces at the end of a three-day waterless march, and how it was precisely Pavel Nikolaevich, then not yet a general at all, who took upon himself a decision for which another would have paid with his head, but which in the end saved if not all then the majority.

"What decision?" I asked then.

The Colonel looked long into his glass.

"He went to the Turkmen," he said at last. "Alone. Without weapons, without an interpreter. Straight into the camp of those who for three days had been driving us across the sands. We thought we should not see him again. And he returned at dawn - with a guide and with skins of water. How he came to terms with them, in what language, what he promised them - he did not say, neither then nor afterward. Neither to me nor in his report."

"Perhaps he knew their language?"

"Pavel?" The Colonel shook his head. "He then did not know a word of Turkmen. But here is what I shall tell you, boy. When he returned, he had a scroll inside his coat. Leather, old, with writing - with such squiggles, you know. I saw it out of the corner of my eye. He never showed it to anyone afterward." The Colonel finished his wine. "I asked him once. He said: 'It is payment for the water, Misha. Not my payment - theirs. They said that this thing had long awaited the man who would come for it. And that I was not that man. But I must pass it on.'"

"To whom?"

"He did not say." The Colonel rose, making it plain that the conversation was over. "And you do not ask him. He looked at me then in such a way that I never asked again."

The General himself, when I once let slip a mention of this conversation in his presence, only remarked coldly that the Colonel, as always, exaggerated, and at once changed the subject to something far less entertaining. But I saw that at that moment his hand of itself lay on that pocket of his uniform where the wooden raven lay.

* * *

Then there was China.

"Peking?" my aunt once laughed. "Pavel Nikolaevich, do not tell me you were in Peking too. I shall not believe you."

"You will not believe me - and rightly so, Artemia Aristarkhovna," the General answered imperturbably. "Into Peking itself I rode twice. But I lived there half a year. So you are right: to be in Peking is not at all the same as to live in it."

He told of Kyakhta - a little town on the very border, where in enormous merchants' courts caravans were exchanged: our furs, broadcloth and leathers for Chinese tea, silk and porcelain; where tea was such a chief commodity that it was used to pay in place of money, pressed into bricks hard as masonry. Of the endless road through Mongolia - through steppes where the sky is so huge that a man feels like an ant on a plate, and through the Gobi desert, where at night the sand sings. Of the Great Wall, running like a snake along the ridges of the mountains as far as the eye can see.

"And the city itself, Pavel Nikolaevich?"

"A city in a city in a city, madam. Like a matryoshka. Outside - walls. Behind them - other walls. Behind them - a third. And in the very middle - the Forbidden City, where the foot of no European has trod, save perhaps prisoners. The roofs there are yellow, golden, like ripe wheat - yellow can be worn only by the emperor. And on the ridges of the roofs sit little clay beasts - dragons, lions, phoenixes - lined up one behind another, as if guarding the roof from evil spirits."

"And did you see the emperor?"

"No, madam. No one sees the emperor." The General paused. "But I saw Mandarin Liu."

He recalled this mandarin with a particular, almost tender respect. A dignitary of the ministry of foreign relations, a small, wizened old man with long, polished nails enclosed in silver tips, and a round cap crowned with a ball of blue stone - the sign of high rank. With him the General had spent more than a hundred hours negotiating the border along the Amur - and had not lost a single argument to him, and had not won a single one.

"He never said 'no,'" the General related. "Not once in half a year. He said: 'That is a very interesting thought, it must be carefully considered.' And he considered. A month. Two. Three. Until your patience ran out, and his - never." The General smiled. "When I was leaving, he came to see me off at the gates. And gave me a fan. Paper, painted. On it was written a single poem, his own. I asked the interpreter - Hieromonk Nifont of our spiritual mission - to read what was there."

"And what was there?"

The General closed his eyes, recalling.

"'Two rivers flow from one mountain. One - to the east, the other - to the west. They will never meet. But in each is reflected the same moon.'"

My aunt was silent for a long time.

"Beautiful," she said at last. "But sad."

"Mandarin Liu told me that it is not sad," the General objected. "He said: 'You northern people think that if rivers do not meet, that is parting. But we think that if there is one moon in them, they have not parted.'"

Two rivers. One moon. I do not know why this fan, which I had never seen, came to my mind now, in the darkness, beneath a depth of black water where I was drowning. But it came - and for some reason comforted me.

* * *

And then he told of America - not of cold Sitka, but of another, hot, noisy, enormous one.

In 'sixty-three, when the North American States were in the throes of their terrible civil war - North against South - the Sovereign, to the amazement of all Europe, sent two squadrons to their shores: one to New York, the other to San Francisco. And Pavel Nikolaevich, on some secret commission of which he never told in detail, set out with the first.

"When our frigates entered the harbour," he told, "the Americans decided that we had come to save them. The whole city poured out onto the quays. They shouted 'hurrah' to us, threw flowers, orchestras played. We were driven through the streets in open carriages, balls were given in our honour - such that yours, Artemia Aristarkhovna, forgive me, are as far from them as the moon."

"Oh, come now," my aunt snorted.

"A thousand guests, madam. A ballroom the size of St Isaac's Square. Forty thousand candles. And the ladies - ladies in crinolines of such width that they went through doors sideways."

"And did we really come to save them?" asked Konstantin.

The General looked at him with an impenetrable face.

"We came, Lieutenant, because the English and the French were preparing to fight us over Poland. And a fleet locked in Kronstadt is useless. And a fleet standing in American harbours can strike English ships anywhere." He paused. "But to the Americans we did not say this. It pleased them to think that they were loved. And it pleased us that we were fed for nothing."

The Colonel roared with laughter again. The General permitted himself a barely noticeable smile.

"There was a captain of theirs," he continued, "Ebenezer Hall, from a steamer that ran from New York to the West Indies. Red-haired, huge, with a beard like Chernomor's, and a voice that made the windowpanes rattle. He made friends with our officers and once took me with him on a voyage - to Cuba and the islands. So I found myself in Havana."

* * *

"Havana," repeated Evdokia Gerasimovna, dreamily closing her eyes. "Is that where the cigars are?"

"There, madam. And sugar. And rum. And a heat that makes the air tremble, as above a samovar."

He described the white walls of the fortress above the entrance to the harbour and the lighthouse on its rock. Motley houses with wrought-iron balconies, painted pink, blue, yellow - like pastries in a confectioner's. Narrow streets where overhead, from house to house, washing hung - "just as in Constantinople, Artemia Aristarkhovna, only louder." Music that sounded from everywhere - from windows, from taverns, from squares - drums and guitars so fast that one's feet began to move of themselves. Women with high combs in their hair and fans. Black slaves on the sugar plantations - at this memory his face turned to stone, and he spoke of it little and dully.

"In Havana," he said, "I went into a tobacco factory. An enormous, bright hall where at long tables hundreds of people sat rolling cigars. And on a dais in the middle of the hall sat a man and read aloud to them. A book. Loudly, with expression, for the whole hall. I was explained that such was the custom: the work is long and dull, and the workers themselves pay the reader to read to them while they roll the leaves. Do you know what he was reading?"

"What?"

"The Count of Monte Cristo," said the General. "About a man whom everyone thought dead, and who returned under another name. Hundreds of people rolled cigars and listened, holding their breath. One old man beside me wept."

I remember that at that moment I raised my eyes to my aunt. And saw that she was looking not at the General but at me.

From Cuba Captain Hall took him to St Thomas - a little Danish island, where in the harbour stood ships under the flags of half the world, where in the market they spoke at once English, Danish, Spanish, French and some other sing-song dialect which no one understood but the locals - and from there, a month later, by steamer bound for Rio de Janeiro.

* * *

"Brazil," said the General once, when my aunt for the umpteenth time asked for "something not about war." "There, Artemia Aristarkhovna, there is nothing about war at all. There is only sun, mountains and sea."

He told of the bay of Rio - so enormous and beautiful that sailors, entering it for the first time, take off their caps as though entering a temple. Of a mountain like a sugarloaf, rising straight from the water. Of the forest which began immediately beyond the city - not even a forest, but a green wall, where creepers thick as an arm hung from trees tall as a belfry, where butterflies the size of a palm fluttered, blue as azure, and parrots - red, yellow, green - shrieked as if arguing about politics.

"And their emperor," added the General, "is the quietest of men. A scholar. Speaks a dozen languages, corresponds with the learned of all Europe, studies the stars. I was presented to him at a reception. He asked me about our Academy of Sciences - and listened as attentively as no minister in Petersburg has ever listened to me."

"And the Amazon?" Konstantin suddenly asked. "Did you see the Amazon?"

"I saw it, Lieutenant. The mouth." The General paused. "A river so wide that at the mouth the other bank cannot be seen. The water in it remains fresh for many miles out in the sea - one can scoop it up and drink, standing on deck in the middle of the ocean. A Portuguese pilot told me this. I did not believe it. I scooped up a bucketful. Tasted it. Fresh."

"It cannot be!"

"It can, Lieutenant. In this world almost anything can be." The General looked into the fire. "I have not seen anything impossible in my life. I have seen only what at first I did not believe."

It seems that it was then, that evening, that I first heard from him what he afterward repeated more than once, in different words, but with one and the same meaning:

"The world is bigger than it seems to you, young people. Much bigger. And much older. When you travel - and you will, I know - do not look at palaces. Look at faces. Sometimes in the farthest corner of the earth you will meet a face that seems familiar to you. Do not be frightened. It happens."

"Has it happened to you, Pavel Nikolaevich?" asked my aunt.

The General was silent for a long time.

"It has, Artemia Aristarkhovna," he said at last. And told nothing more that evening.

* * *

The General's family life, however, had turned out far sadder than his service life. He had lost his wife many years ago, on one of the Asian campaigns - she could not endure the climate there and died of an illness before which the doctors proved powerless. Since then Pavel Nikolaevich, it seemed, had abandoned all hope of his own family happiness, entirely absorbed in service to the state, and paid not the slightest attention to the young ladies who from time to time tried, with varying success, to secure as a husband so enviable, though no longer youthful, a match.

Scarcely an evening of my aunt's passed, it seemed, without one of her newly-minted friends making persistent attempts to become better acquainted with him. I myself more than once observed this simple comedy - no further back than today, with Lizonka Kutasova's pouch and the tea roses: the young lady would, as if by chance, find herself beside his armchair; the General would meet all her civilities with that impeccable, icy courtesy that disconcerts more surely than any direct rudeness - and within a quarter of an hour the luckless aspirant would retreat, carrying away with her wounded vanity and a firm intention henceforth not to waste effort in vain.

It was also whispered that the General himself, in spite of all his stern imperturbability, was not wholly indifferent to a certain person - but who exactly this mysterious lady was remained at court a secret which Pavel Nikolaevich kept with the same impenetrability with which, no doubt, he kept far more important state secrets. I, I confess, more than once caught myself thinking that the answer might lie far closer to home than idle capital gossip supposed - but I took care not to think this thought through to the end even alone with myself.

Once, however, I happened to become the involuntary witness of a scene which strengthened these suspicions of mine far more firmly than any gossip. It was at one of my aunt's evenings, near midnight, when most of the guests had already left; I, having lingered in the library over a book, chanced to see through a half-open door how Evdokia Gerasimovna, supposing, no doubt, that no one saw her, straightened in passing the disarranged collar of the General's uniform - with a gesture so natural, so devoid of any social, showy timidity, that it could belong only to a woman accustomed to doing it more than once. The General did not draw away. He only closed his eyes for a moment - briefly, almost unnoticeably - and at once put on again that impenetrable mask of courteous indifference with which he appeared before the rest of the world.

And then he said to her - so quietly that I barely heard:

"Dunya. If I go away again - you will wait, won't you?"

And she answered - also quietly, without a shadow of coquetry:

"I have been waiting for you since you were twenty, Pavel. What are another five years to me?"

I never discussed that subject again, with anyone, myself included.

* * *

He had no children - perhaps precisely for that reason he extended such boundless patronage to Lieutenant Alexei, in whom, as they whispered behind his back, he saw some reflection of a non-existent son. However much this frivolous, ever-enthusiastic young man sometimes vexed him, the General never once permitted himself a harsh word against him - except in those rare cases when the Lieutenant's flippancy touched Artemia Aristarkhovna, whom Pavel Nikolaevich respected and protected with an almost chivalrous scrupulousness. Once, I remember, I came upon him reprimanding Alexei - under his breath, at the very doors of the hall - for some innocuous, in essence, quip about my aunt's age; the General's face at that moment was so impenetrably severe that the poor Lieutenant did not lift his eyes to my aunt for the rest of the evening after that conversation.

"Why do you defend her so, Pavel Nikolaevich?" I asked him once, having screwed up my courage.

The General gave me a long, strange look.

"Because she once defended something that was very dear to me," he said. "And paid for it more than you can imagine."

"What exactly?"

"Someday," said the General, "she will tell you herself. Or will not tell. That is her right." And, after a pause, he added so quietly that I was not sure I heard rightly: "You are like him, you know. Sometimes - very."

"Like whom?"

But he had already turned away, and that evening I never ventured to ask again.

* * *

On his ancestral estate, which the General visited seldom, a truly luxurious garden had been laid out - with a snow-white arbour in the very centre of a living labyrinth of bushes and flowering plants. This arbour was entwined by a rose bush of astonishing beauty, brought from the Bulgarian Tsardom and presented to Pavel Nikolaevich in memory of his visit to that country; it was said that in spring the scent of this bush spread almost through the whole garden, drowning all other flowers at once. Roses in general were the General's true passion - it was hard to imagine, looking at his stern, at times almost coarse face, that behind this exterior hid so ardent a lover of flowers. On one of his numerous visits to the Balkan peninsula he had collected a whole collection of roses - white, pink, of the tea-tree shade and, of course, scarlet, dearest to his heart - and, by rumour, personally supervised their transplanting whenever some gardener dared to show initiative of his own without his approval.

To tend this garden the General had hired several men directly in the Balkans, reasoning rightly that no one understands roses as they are understood where roses bloom almost all year round. Stefan, his favourite gardener, was from Veliko Tarnovo - a glorious Bulgarian city, then part of the Ottoman Porte - and in the several years spent on the General's estate he had managed to turn the already blooming garden into a truly stunning park which, by general opinion, had no equal in the whole Russian Empire. Silent, taciturn, he appeared at the common table very rarely, preferring the company of his roses to the company of people - and the General, it seemed, valued in him precisely this reserve no less than his gardening talent.

Once, walking with Konstantin in the General's garden, I came upon Stefan at work: he was tying a scarlet rose to a trellis and quietly singing something in his own language. Seeing me, he fell silent, straightened - and looked at me so long that Konstantin had to cough. Then Stefan said - slowly, with a strong accent, as though with difficulty choosing foreign words:

"Roses remember. People forget - roses remember. Who planted, who watered, who picked. They remember everything." And turned back to the trellis.

"What was that?" asked Konstantin when we had walked away.

"I do not know," I said. And it was true.

Of all the corners of the world where Pavel Nikolaevich had happened to be, the Balkan peninsula remained, perhaps, his favourite place on earth: the warm waters of the Adriatic, the hot springs and healing muds of the Bulgarian Tsardom, the strong red wine from the western shore of the Black Sea, the winding streets of Constantinople, the strongest black tea which only the Turks knew how to brew, and, of course, the Greek principality - with its transparent waters and remarkably hospitable people.

But the true attachment of the General's heart was stolen, if one believed his own scanty admissions, by the Kingdom of Siam: its people, kindest and radiant, its marvellous nature, its ever-warm weather, in spite of the rainy season so characteristic of those latitudes.

From his Asian wanderings he had brought many curious trinkets, but he prized most of all a statue of the seated Buddha - white, visually almost weightless stone, so wonderfully harmonising with his garden of fragrant roses. By one version circulating among the servants, this statue had been presented to him personally by one of the Siamese monks in gratitude for some service about which the General never spoke to anyone; by another - he had bought it himself, at one of the distant markets, paying a price which he afterward preferred not to recall. Which of these versions was true I would not undertake to affirm - but the very existence of several mutually contradictory legends spoke better than any words of how dense a veil the General drew around absolutely everything touching his personal attachments.

Monks. Siamese monks in burgundy robes. The thought surfaced from the depths - and I suddenly remembered the face bent over me only a few moments ago, there, in the sunlit garden. Burgundy robes. A bare shoulder. A calm voice. Was he not from there? Not from those monasteries to which the General travelled with the Imperial family?

Pavel Nikolaevich did not consider himself a religious man, but he invariably respected the religious convictions of others, and of all the teachings known to him it was precisely the ideas of Buddhism that seemed to him closest to his own cast of mind. In his travels through Far Asia he had managed to visit, it seemed, a good half of the monasteries of the Kingdom of Siam and the Malay Sultanate; during visits to Siam itself he was invariably received in the royal palace, where the most ancient relics were kept, inaccessible to the ordinary mortal - but for Pavel Nikolaevich, to all appearances, there existed in all Asia no doors that would not open before him at the first necessity.

Sitka. Samarkand. Peking. New York. Havana. Rio. Bangkok. This man had seen more of the world than anyone I knew. And yet, remembering him now, in the darkness, I for some reason thought not of palaces and not of harbours, but of his hand laid on the pocket of his uniform. Of the wooden raven that stole the sun. Of the leather scroll with squiggles that the Turkmen gave him - "not to the right man, but that he pass it on." And of the words spoken under his breath by the fireplace: "Sometimes in the farthest corner of the earth you will meet a face that seems familiar to you. Do not be frightened. It happens."

* * *

...Somewhere at the very border of this strange, half-connected stream of thoughts I again felt the darkness surge beneath me, like the surface of that same deep well - and felt hands, insistent and careful at once, take me under the shoulders, drawing me back toward that unsteady, not yet quite distinguishable boundary separating oblivion from waking.

There were now several voices around - but whose exactly I never managed to make out, however I tried to catch with my consciousness even one of them. It smelled for some reason not of grass nor of a summer garden, but of candle wax, dust and old wood. One voice - if it was a voice at all and not the play of a tormented mind - seemed to me for a moment familiar, far more familiar than the meagre crumbs of memory still at my disposal allowed me to hope.

"I told you - shout," said this voice, quite close, angry and frightened at once. "I told you..."

A second voice, low, dry, accustomed to command, answered him something brief - and it seemed to me that somewhere right by my face, by someone's uniform, the button of a pocket creaked, that very pocket where the wooden raven lay.

But before I could hold on to this thought, the darkness closed over me again, this time finally, leaving not a single support to which I might cling.
