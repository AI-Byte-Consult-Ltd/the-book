Chapter XIII
The Note in Farsi

After the morning in the Grand Bazaar, the second day in Ferhat's house flowed unhurriedly, like thick honey pouring from a clay pot. I sat on the balcony with a cup of tea which Akmal had brought me, and looked at the city - at the roofs, the minarets, at the strip of water where among the masts somewhere stood a ship like the one which ten years ago had gone to Crete and not returned. Below, in the street, Akmal was once again chasing the neighbour's cat, ginger and impudent, which every time the boy almost caught it leapt to the fence and sat there, narrowing its yellow eyes mockingly. From behind the curtain on the women's side came voices, laughter, the knock of a pestle - something spicy was being pounded there for supper.

In my pocket lay three papers. The note in Farsi. The blank sheet folded in four. And Kemal's drawing - two doors and a man between them. I did not take them out. I merely felt their weight - light, almost weightless, like the weight of letters not yet read.

By noon, however, this calm was broken - and in the noisiest way.

* * *

"Misafir! Misafir-efendi!"

I would have recognised that voice anywhere now. Hasan Amca, red-bearded, resonant, rolled into the yard as a heavily laden barge rolls into a harbour - and behind him, barely keeping up, scurried a second man. A dried-up, small old man in a white turban wound so high that it seemed twice as large as his head, in a long dark robe, with a sparse grey goatee and a rosary in his hand. His eyes were sharp, quick, mocking - such as belong to people who have lived all their lives among books and never learned to take any of them seriously.

"Hodja Nureddin," Ferhat introduced him, looking out from his grandfather's room. He was at home - a rare day off, begged from the port authorities. "The imam of our mosque. Grandfather has called them both. Hasan Amca knows Farsi. The hodja knows Arabic. Grandfather says - together they will read everything. The three of them with him - even what is not written there."

"And will the two of them not quarrel?" I asked.

Ferhat grinned.

"They will quarrel for certain. That is the most interesting part."

I was called downstairs - to a small room on the ground floor which, judging by its furnishings, could be called something between a study and a prayer room. Its walls were hung with shelves of old books and scrolls, the floor was laid with carpets - one over another, so that the foot sank as into moss. On the wall hung a board with an inscription in gold on black, and under it a thin prayer mat, folded in four. In the corner a censer smoked, and the smoke rose from it in a thin blue thread, smelling of sandalwood and something bitterish.

On the low table before Suleyman lay my note. Beside it - another sheet, covered with small, neat annotations made evidently by a different, firmer hand. Hasan, puffing, lowered himself onto a cushion on one side of the table, Hodja Nureddin on the other, carefully gathering the skirts of his robe. Suleyman - at the head. I sat opposite. Ferhat settled by the window, to translate. And in the doorway, leaning against the doorpost, silently stood Kemal - and no one drove him off.

* * *

Hasan took the note, held it up to the window and looked a long time against the light, squinting so that his eyes sank in his russet brows.

"Shikasteh," he said at last. And repeated, for me, slowly: "Shi-kas-teh. 'Broken.'"

"He says," Ferhat translated, "that this is a special script, Persian. 'Broken writing.' It is written not for strangers but for one's own. The letters run into one another, the dots vanish, the words grow together. Whoever is not used to it will read nothing. Thus are written letters to beloveds, poems for oneself and testaments." Ferhat paused, listening. "Hasan Amca says that in his youth he carried silk to Tabriz, and there an old merchant wrote him accounts in shikasteh so that the clerks should not understand. Hasan Amca spent half a year learning to read them. He says it cost more than the silk itself."

"And what is written here?"

Hasan jabbed a thick finger at a line.

"An address," Ferhat translated. "The same as yesterday. The Sultanahmet quarter, the lane behind the bathhouse, an inconspicuous door in a blank wall, near an old column..." Ferhat hesitated. "He says 'column' here may perhaps not be a column. Perhaps a pillar. Or a stone. In shikasteh it is one and the same word, as you turn it."

Hodja Nureddin, who until then had been silently telling his rosary, suddenly leaned across the table and said something - quickly, with evident mockery. Hasan answered - louder. The hodja answered still faster. In a moment they were already arguing, interrupting one another, jabbing fingers at the note, and Suleyman, leaning back on the cushions, watched them with the pleasure with which one watches a cockfight.

"What are they about?" I asked Ferhat in a whisper.

"The hodja says that Hasan Amca reads shikasteh as a drunken boatman reads the stars." Ferhat was choking with laughter. "And Hasan Amca says that the hodja knows Arabic only from the Koran, and in the Koran there is no word 'bathhouse,' therefore the hodja is jealous."

Suleyman raised a hand - and both fell silent as on command.

* * *

"Further," said Suleyman.

Hasan, still puffing from the argument, bent over the note again.

"The first word he translates as 'return,'" Ferhat translated. "Or perhaps 'returning' - in Farsi it may mean both, depending on the context in which it stands."

"Bāzgasht," said Hasan in Persian, savouring the word. And suddenly, without waiting for a question, he closed his eyes and recited something in a chant - slowly, low, with such sorrow in his voice as I had never expected from this resonant, merry man.

All in the room fell quiet. Even the hodja lowered his rosary.

"These are verses," Ferhat whispered. "Persian. Very old. Rumi. All know them who know Farsi. Hasan Amca says the word in the note is the same as in these verses." He paused, picking his words. "It is about the reed. About the flute made of reed. Listen to the reed, how it complains, says the poet - how it tells of separation. Since I was cut from the reed bed, men and women weep at my weeping. Whoever is torn from his beginning seeks the day when he will return to it."

Whoever is torn from his beginning seeks the day when he will return.

I sat motionless. Hasan opened his eyes - and they were moist. He snorted in embarrassment, wiped them with his sleeve and became again the old resonant Hasan.

"The second word is harder," Ferhat continued, having listened to his grandfather. "Grandfather says it is like 'to keep' - but with a shade for which in our language there is no exact equivalent. Not merely 'to preserve a thing,' but rather... 'to preserve until the appointed time.' To wait until the time comes."

To keep until the appointed time. I repeated these words to myself several times. Return - to where? To keep - what, if not these very two stones, resting now in the leather pouch at my belt?

* * *

"There is one more," Hasan said suddenly, and his voice grew quieter. He pointed to the very edge of the note - to where the paper was most worn. "Here. One word. Yesterday I did not read it. Today - I read it. But I do not know how to understand it."

"Which?"

"Khāneh," said Hasan. "Or khāna. If 'khāneh' - it is 'house.' Your house, my house, a native house. But if 'khāna' - it is a cage. A cell. As on the backgammon board - each cell is called 'khāna.' In shikasteh they cannot be told apart. There is no dot."

A house. Or a cage. Or a cell on a board along which alien hands move counters.

I remembered Karapet's riddle: "Where he looks - there home."

Hodja Nureddin, who had been listening in silence, suddenly laughed - with a dry, rattling old man's laugh - and said something, shaking a finger at the ceiling.

"The hodja says," Ferhat translated, smiling, "that it is like in that story about Nasreddin."

"What story?"

Hodja Nureddin, not waiting for translation, himself turned to me, adjusted his enormous turban and began to speak - slowly, expressively, with gestures, as though telling children. Ferhat translated after him:

"'Once at night Hodja Nasreddin crawled on his knees under a lamp in the middle of the street, looking for something. A neighbour asked: "What are you looking for, Hodja?" - "I lost the key to my house." The neighbour began to search with him. They searched and searched - did not find it. The neighbour asked: "And where exactly did you lose it?" - "At home," answered Nasreddin. "Then why are you looking here, in the street?" - "Because it is lighter here."'"

Hasan roared with laughter, slapping his knees. Ferhat laughed. Even Suleyman smiled into his beard.

And I did not laugh. The hodja looked at me over his little beard with sharp, mocking eyes - and in those eyes there was no mockery.

"He says," Ferhat added more quietly, "that you, perhaps, also look where it is lighter. In strange cities, in strange notes. And the key, perhaps, is where you lost it. Within."

"I do not know where I lost it," I said.

The hodja heard the translation and spread his hands - with the expression with which people spread their hands who know the answer but do not think it necessary to pronounce it.

"The hodja says," said Ferhat, "that Nasreddin did not know either. That is why he crawled under the lamp. But he at least knew that he was looking for a key. And not for a lamp."

* * *

"Since the hodja tells tales," Hasan thundered, unwilling to yield his rival an inch, "I shall tell mine. A Persian one. A real one. Not about asses and lamps."

The hodja rolled his eyes. Suleyman waved a hand - tell. Hasan settled himself more comfortably, so that the cushion beneath him sighed piteously, and began - in a chant, with that special expression with which tales are told in bazaars for a copper coin. Ferhat translated, barely keeping up:

"'Once all the birds of the world gathered - the nightingale and the falcon, the peacock and the owl, the duck and the heron, and the little hoopoe, the wisest of all. And they said: all peoples have a king, and we have none. And the hoopoe said: we have. His name is the Simurgh. He lives beyond seven valleys, on Mount Qaf, at the edge of the world. One of his feathers once fell to earth, in China - and all the artists of the world have since drawn only that feather, because nothing else of him has been seen. Let us go to him.'"

"And did they go?" I asked.

"'They went. But the nightingale said: I cannot, I love the rose. The peacock said: I cannot, I am too beautiful to tire. The duck said: I cannot without water. The owl said: I love my ruins. And each bird found a reason to stay. And those who went - went through seven valleys. The Valley of the Quest. The Valley of Love. The Valley of Knowledge. The Valley of Detachment. The Valley of Unity. The Valley of Wonder. And the last - the Valley of Poverty and Annihilation. And in each valley birds died. Of weariness, of fear, of thirst, of doubts. Thousands and thousands set out. And to Mount Qaf only thirty flew.'"

Hasan paused - long, solemn. Even the hodja stopped telling his rosary.

"'Thirty birds,'" Hasan continued quietly, "'wounded, bedraggled, barely alive. They came to the palace of the Simurgh. And the doorkeeper opened the door to them. And they entered. And saw...' - he spread his hands - 'a lake. Smooth as a mirror. And in it - themselves. Thirty birds. And then they understood. In the language of birds, "si murgh" means "thirty birds." They had been seeking a king beyond the edge of the world - and the king was they. All this time. They themselves were what they sought.'"

The room was quiet. The censer crackled. Kemal in the doorway did not stir.

"They sought beyond the edge of the world," Ferhat repeated slowly, no longer translating but as though thinking aloud. "And found in a mirror."

Hodja Nureddin coughed.

"My Nasreddin," he said drily, "said the same in a single line. And without thirty dead birds."

Hasan turned crimson, opened his mouth - and then Suleyman laughed. Loudly, from the heart, as I had never yet heard him laugh. And after him - first Ferhat, then the hodja, then Hasan himself, shaking with his whole huge body, so that the cups trembled on the table.

And I looked into the corner of the room, where in the half-dark hung a small copper mirror - and saw in it my own face. Strange. Unfamiliar. The face of a man without a name, seeking beyond the edge of the world what, perhaps, he had long carried within.

* * *

"A riddle!" Hasan demanded, when he had done laughing. "Hodja, you boast of your Nasreddin - set a riddle that the guest will not guess. I stake a sack of cinnamon."

"I stake a little bag of dates," responded the hodja, without blinking. And, turning to me, uttered something short. Ferhat translated:

"'What comes to everyone, but no one has seen how it came? And leaves everyone, but no one has seen how it left?'"

"Sleep," I said.

The hodja nodded, pursing his lips in displeasure. Hasan guffawed and rubbed his palms.

"Now mine!" He leaned to me, and his eyes sparkled. "'Two brothers live in one house. One always looks forward, the other - always back. They have never seen each other. But one without the other cannot take a single step.'"

I thought. Two brothers. Again two. Black and white. Day and night. Sun and moon.

"Tomorrow and yesterday?" I suggested.

Hasan shook his head in triumph.

"Eyes?" Ferhat suggested.

"No!"

"Feet?" Kemal squeaked from the doorway - the first word I had heard from him all day.

"No!" Hasan slapped his palm on the table. "No one! The sack of cinnamon stays with me!"

"Then what is the answer?" asked the hodja, narrowing his eyes.

Hasan opened his mouth - and froze. His face slowly lengthened.

"I do not remember," he said in bewilderment. "My father set it to me. Forty years ago. The answer... I do not remember."

Hodja Nureddin laughed so that his enormous turban slipped sideways.

"There!" he said, straightening it. "That is how Persian riddles end. Two brothers, and no one knows who they are. Like our guest." He nodded at me. "He too does not know the answer. Perhaps because the answer has not yet been devised."

* * *

And then Hasan, who still held the note against the window, suddenly stopped.

"Hey," he said. Then louder: "Hey-hey-hey... Suleyman agha. Bak. Look."

He held the note right up to the glass, so that the sun passed through the paper. All bent toward him - Suleyman, the hodja, Ferhat, I.

In the thickness of the paper, where no ink blot touched it, a sign showed through. A watermark - such as masters put on good paper so that all should know whose work it is. Quite pale, barely distinguishable, it was visible only against the light.

Two circles. One - a little darker, one - a little lighter. And between them - a wavy line.

The room grew so quiet that I heard the coal crackle in the censer.

Kemal, who all this time had stood in the doorway, slowly came to the table. Looked at the paper against the light. Then - at me. And nodded - once, very gravely, as one nods when what you have long known is at last confirmed.

Hodja Nureddin stopped telling his rosary.

"The boy has been drawing this for the second day," said Suleyman slowly. Ferhat translated, stammering. "Two circles and a road between them. I thought - a child's amusement." The old man took the note from Hasan, held it long against the window, looking at the watermark. Then lowered it. "Paper with such a mark was made at one old mill. I know. Forty years I have traded in what people write on and what they wrap things in. Such paper I saw once in my life. Long ago. When I was young, like Ferhat."

"Where?" I asked. "Where did you see it?"

Suleyman was silent a long time.

"On such paper," he said at last, "was written a letter which a foreigner brought to Istanbul. He came into my father's shop, asked the way to Sultanahmet. Showed the letter. My father could not read it. The foreigner went away." The old man paused. "I remembered him because he was dressed neither as we are nor as the Franks. And because he said a strange thing to my father. He said: 'You are good people. If another comes - feed him.'"

I looked at the old man and could not utter a word.

"I shall come once more. Perhaps not I, but I shall come," said the nameless stranger in the shop of Karapet's great-grandfather.

"If another comes - feed him," said the nameless stranger in the shop of Suleyman's father.

Ferhat translated his grandfather's last words - and fell silent himself, looking now at his grandfather, now at me.

"Grandfather," he said softly, in his own language, but I understood by the intonation. "Is that why? Is that why you feed all wanderers? Not only because of Father?"

Suleyman answered nothing. He folded the note, laid it on the sheet with Hasan's annotations - and for a long time held his palm over both sheets, as though warming them.

* * *

The silence was broken by the curtain. From behind it, from the depths of the women's side, a voice came - aged, cracked, the very one that every evening sang the song of the sailor. Grandmother. She said something - quietly but firmly - and the men in the room fell silent at once, as men fall silent in all the houses of the world when the eldest of the women speaks.

Ferhat listened with bowed head. Then turned to me.

"Grandmother asks," he said slowly, "whether there is in the note the word 'sea.' Or 'ship.'"

Hasan looked at the note. Shook his head.

"No," Ferhat translated. "Hasan Amca says - no."

Behind the curtain there was a long silence. Then Grandmother said one more phrase - shorter, quieter.

"Grandmother says: good," Ferhat translated. "Then you are not from the sea. Then you have not come in his place."

I did not understand. Ferhat, seeing my face, explained in an undertone:

"She feared that you were a messenger. That you had come to say that Father..." He did not finish. "Grandmother believes: when a sailor does not return, the sea sends a stranger in his stead. So that the house should not stand empty. She feared that you were such."

From behind the curtain a hand was thrust out - swarthy, wrinkled, in silver bracelets - and set a small saucer on the floor by the threshold. On it lay three dried apricots and a piece of honeycomb with honey. The hand vanished.

"This is for you," said Ferhat. "Grandmother says: since you are not 'instead,' then - from above. For a guest - honey."

* * *

"Sultanahmet," I said at last, when I could speak. "Is it far from here?"

Ferhat translated the question to his grandfather, and this time Suleyman answered himself, nodding briefly toward the window, beyond which could be made out the roofs of the city descending to the water, invisible from here.

"Not very," Ferhat translated. "Over the hill and along the shore, where the new Palace is being built. Grandfather was there in his youth, when he traded in silk. He says the place is quiet, old, with narrow lanes and many little mosques."

"We shall go there in two days, as we agreed," I reminded him.

The old man nodded, then uttered a few more words - this time slower, as though choosing them with particular care. Ferhat, having listened, looked at me a little longer than usual before translating.

"Grandfather asks," he said at last, "whether you are sure that you want to go there. He says - no offence meant - that a note hidden so deep in the pocket of a foreigner's cloak rarely leads to good news. More often to a debt that must be repaid. Or to something better left untouched."

"I have no choice," I answered honestly. "It is the only thread I have. Without it I do not even know where to begin the search for my own name."

Hodja Nureddin suddenly leaned to me across the table and said - without translation, in my own language, broken, laboriously assembled, which I had not expected to hear from him:

"Name - not key. Name - lamp. Key - where lost."

And leaned back on his cushion again, telling his rosary as if he had said nothing.

Suleyman heard all this out, was silent, then slowly nodded. He folded both notes - mine and the one Hasan had written over - together and held them out to me across the table.

"Take both," Ferhat translated his last words. "Hasan's decipherment may prove useful there, on the spot, if you have to explain to someone what exactly you are seeking."

* * *

Hasan and the hodja left together - and on the threshold began quarrelling again. I heard Hasan assert something resonantly about Persian poets, and the hodja drily object something about Arabic grammar, and how their voices receded along the street, now merging, now parting, like two streams flowing from one source in different directions. At the gate Hasan turned, waved to me - "Misafir!" - and I waved back.

Kemal left last. He stood on the threshold looking at me, then came up, took my hand, opened the palm and laid in it something small and sharp.

A fragment of blue bead. The very one split in the night. The half which he had picked up from the floor.

He closed my fingers around the fragment - firmly, like an adult - and went away without a word.

I remained alone in the small room smelling of sandalwood. On the table where the note had just lain, only a pale square of sunlight from the window remained. I sat and held in one hand the folded sheets, in the other the bead fragment, and at my belt felt the light, cool weight of the pouch with the stones.

Return. Keep until the appointed time. House - or cage.

And two nameless strangers who had come to this city before me - one with stones, the other with a letter - and both left behind one and the same thing: a promise that someone else would come.

I did not yet know what these words meant. But I felt - as clearly as I had felt the warmth of strangers' hospitality all this time - that this term was approaching far faster than I should have liked to think.

And that I, perhaps, was the very "other" whom they had been told to feed.
