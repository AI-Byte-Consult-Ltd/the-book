Reality X
The Ocean

06:11. I am sitting on black sand. The laptop on my knees, its camera turned to the water. Boatswain's ship's chest beside me on the sand, open, like a pirate's coffer. The sun has not yet risen. The sky in the east is pink-grey, like the inside of a shell. The sea is still dark, almost black, quiet, breathing.

NICS has been silent for nine minutes.

I time it by the clock on the screen. Nine minutes. Not a line. Not a sound. Only the cursor blinking in the empty field. Evenly. Patiently. Like a metronome.

I do not hurry him.

* * *

But let me take things in order.

We arrived on Langkawi yesterday evening, by ferry from Kuala Perlis, overcrowded, thundering, smelling of diesel, fried noodles and children's sick. Hakim came with us. "Mister," he said, "you cannot manage on the island without me. They speak the Kedah dialect there. Even I understand it only every other word. And you not a single one."

He sat beside me on the ferry, in his batik shirt with the golden birds, in his velvet songkok, and ate rambutans from a bag, red, hairy, like little sea urchins, peeling them with one motion of a fingernail and tossing the peel overboard.

"Langkawi, mister," he said, chewing, "means 'red eagle.' Or 'russet eagle.' *Lang* is eagle, *kawi* is reddish-brown. Like clay. Eagles live here. Hundreds. You will see." He spat out a stone. "And some say 'Langkawi' means 'necklace of beads.' Because the islands, there are a hundred here, a hundred and four, lie in the sea like beads on a thread." He thought. "And my uncle says that 'Langkawi' means 'the place where my uncle once lost a sandal.' But my uncle translates everything so."

The ferry lurched. Hakim imperturbably caught a rambutan that rolled off his knee.

"And is it true that the island was cursed?" I asked.

Hakim stopped chewing.

"Mahsuri," he said. "Yes. Two hundred years ago. Seven generations." He looked at the sea. "Mister, on the island they will tell you. There is her grave. And there lives an old woman who says she is the great-great-granddaughter of Mahsuri's granddaughter." He smiled. "On the island there are many such old women. Each a great-great-granddaughter. But this one, they say, is real."

* * *

The taxi driver who met us at the quay was called Pak Dol. He was small, dry, wrinkled, with one gold tooth and a tattoo on his forearm, a blue anchor, exactly like Boatswain's.

I stared at the anchor.

Pak Dol noticed my look. Grinned. Said something.

"He says," Hakim translated, "that for twenty years he sailed on merchant ships. Singapore, Hong Kong, Busan, Rotterdam." Hakim listened on. "Says in Rotterdam they gave him this anchor. A drunken tattooist. He wanted a dragon. Got an anchor." Hakim laughed. "Says, good that it was not a mermaid."

"Tell him," I said, "that I have a friend. Also a sailor. With the same anchor."

Hakim translated. Pak Dol turned to me, the car swerved on the road, looked at me closely and said slowly, in English, with a heavy accent:

"Friend, with anchor? On left arm?"

"On the left."

"Thirty years at sea?"

"Thirty."

"Boatswain?"

I froze.

Pak Dol looked at me in the rear-view mirror for a long time. Then suddenly laughed, high, with a rasp, and slapped the wheel.

"All boatswains have anchor on left arm!" he said. "All! All over the world! It is law!" He roared again. "You think I know your friend? I know all your friends! They are all the same! They drink, they swear, they listen to weather forecast on radio and guard other people's things!" He winked. "Right?"

"Right," I said.

"Tell him from me," said Pak Dol, "that old Dol of Langkawi sends greetings. He does not know me. But he will understand. All boatswains understand each other."

* * *

The guesthouse to which Pak Dol brought us stood on stilts above a mangrove swamp, in the shade of enormous trees whose roots, like spiders' legs, stuck out of the water. The hostess was a woman of about sixty, round, laughing, in a flowered sarong and headscarf, named Mak Cha. She met us on the veranda with a tray bearing two glasses of something bright green.

"Pandan juice," said Hakim. "With coconut. Drink, mister. Mak Cha will be offended if you don't. And when Mak Cha is offended, it stops raining on the island."

Mak Cha laughed and said something.

"She says," Hakim translated, "that it is true. Three years ago she was offended with her husband. For a week. And for a week there was no rain on the island." Hakim paused. "And then the husband brought her a durian. And the rain came." He shrugged. "On the island everyone believes it. The husbands too."

I drank the green juice, sweet, smelling of vanilla and grass. Mak Cha watched me closely. Then looked at Boatswain's chest, which I did not let out of my hands.

And said something, quietly, seriously.

"She asks," Hakim translated, "what you have in the chest. She says the chest smells. Of sea. But not this sea. Cold. Northern." Hakim was himself surprised. "Says she can sense it."

"Tell her," I said, "there is a friend in it. Who wants to see the ocean."

Hakim translated. Mak Cha looked at the chest for a long time. Then came up. Laid her palm on the lid. Held it there. As though testing whether it was warm.

And said:

"Bawa dia ke Pantai Pasir Hitam. Waktu subuh."

"Take him to the Beach of Black Sand," Hakim translated slowly. "At dawn."

"Why black?"

Mak Cha answered, at length, in a sing-song, as though telling a tale.

"She says," Hakim translated, "that the sand there is black because it is ash. Two hundred years ago, when the Siamese attacked the island, the people burned their stores of rice so that the enemy should get none. The rice burned for three days. And the rain washed the ash onto this beach." Hakim listened on. "She says, it is rice that burned so that the island might live." He paused. "And that at dawn the sea speaks there. If one knows how to listen."

* * *

The grave of Mahsuri stood in the midst of rice fields, in the shade of mango trees, beneath a white marble canopy. A simple stone. White. With an inscription in Arabic script, Hakim read it aloud to me. Around it, a garden. Flowers. Quiet. There were no tourists; it was evening, and the sun was setting behind the hills, flooding the fields with russet, copper, warm light.

By the grave, on a low bench, sat an old woman.

Very old. Small. In white. With a face brown and wrinkled like the sun-dried rind of a mangosteen, with eyes dark, deep, astonishingly clear. She was running through her fingers a string of beads, wooden, dark, polished. And humming something.

"Nene Sari," Hakim whispered. "The very one. The great-great-granddaughter. She is, they say, a hundred and two."

A hundred and two. Like Niyaz Efendi, I thought. And did not understand where that name came from. Niyaz Efendi. A hundred and two years. Two pairs of spectacles. A round room with shelves.

I had never seen such a man.

The old woman stopped singing. Raised her eyes to me. Looked. A long time. Then said something, quietly, in Malay.

"She asks," Hakim translated, "where you are from."

I opened my mouth. And closed it.

"I do not know," I said.

Hakim hesitated, then translated.

Nene Sari was not surprised. She nodded, slowly, as though she had awaited precisely that answer. And patted the bench beside her.

I sat.

* * *

"Mahsuri was beautiful," Nene Sari told, and Hakim translated, under his breath, so as not to break her rhythm. "The most beautiful on the island. Her husband went to war, with the Siamese. And in her house a wanderer stopped. Young. A poet. A singer. Without a name. He came from nowhere." The old woman ran the beads through her fingers. "He sang her songs when she missed her husband. Nothing more. Only songs. But wicked people said she had been unfaithful. The chieftain's wife envied her beauty. Mahsuri was seized. Tied to a post. They meant to stab her with a dagger." Nene Sari paused. "The dagger would not take her. Seven times they struck, it would not take. Then she herself said: 'Take my father's dagger. It hangs in my house.' They brought it. Struck." The old woman looked at the grave. "And from the wound flowed blood. White. Like milk."

"White blood," I repeated.

"A sign that she was innocent," said Nene Sari. "And then Mahsuri cursed the island. 'For seven generations,' she said, 'there will be neither peace nor harvest here.' And died." The old woman paused. "And indeed, for two hundred years the island was poor. The Siamese came. Pirates came. Rice did not grow." She passed her hand over the beads. "And then seven generations passed. And the island revived. Tourists. Hotels." She smiled toothlessly. "Mahsuri, I think, is glad."

"And the wanderer?" I asked. "The poet without a name? What became of him?"

Nene Sari was silent for a long time.

"We do not tell that to tourists," she said at last. "Only to our own." She looked at me. "You are not ours. But you do not know where you are from. So perhaps you are ours."

She leaned toward me. Hakim leaned too, to hear and translate.

"The wanderer went into the sea," the old woman whispered. "On the night Mahsuri died. Straight into the water. From the beach that is now black sand. Walked and walked until the water closed over his head." She paused. "The fishermen said he did not drown. They said the water glowed around him. Blue. And he walked on the glowing water. And sang. And went away to where the sea meets the sky." Nene Sari straightened. "And before leaving he said to her little son: 'In seven generations I shall return. To see how you live without the curse.'"

Hakim translated the last phrase and fell silent. Looked at me.

"Seven generations have passed," said Nene Sari. Then said something more, and Hakim, after a pause, translated: "She says: 'Welcome back.'"

* * *

In the evening, on the veranda of the guesthouse, by the light of a paraffin lamp around which circled moths the size of a palm, Hakim brought me a coconut. Opened, with a straw.

"Mister," he said. "May I ask a question?"

"Ask."

"Do you truly not know where you are from?"

I was silent a long time. The mangrove roots below, under the stilts, bubbled in the dark water. Somewhere a night bird cried. Cicadas rang so that my ears were stopped.

"I know the city where I live," I said. "I know the flat. The basement. The pianist neighbour. The boy who errs on B-flat. The watchman with the anchor." I paused. "And what was before, I don't remember. Almost nothing. A teacher at school. An uncle with a notebook. Scraps."

"And a name?"

"A name there is. In my passport." I looked at the coconut. "But when they call me by it, I do not respond. As if it were someone else's. As if I were wearing a coat I was lent."

Hakim nodded. Without surprise.

"On our islands they say," he said, "that every person has two names. One for people. The other for the sea. The human name is given by parents. But the sea name the sea gives itself. When a person first enters the water." He paused. "The sea name cannot be spoken. It can only be heard. In the noise of the waves. If one listens long."

"And have you heard yours?"

Hakim laughed.

"Mister, I am an estate agent," he said. "I hear only the noise of air conditioners and the ring of coins." He paused. "But my grandmother heard. She said her sea name meant 'the one who waits on the shore.'" Hakim looked at the dark water. "All her life she waited for my grandfather. He went fishing and did not come back. She was twenty. She waited sixty years. Every morning she went down to the shore." He paused. "Our chicory, as the Europeans say. She who waits by the road."

Chicory.

A blue flower in a single ray of sun at the entrance of a grotto. A boy midshipman with a medallion. An old fisherman with moustaches. A botanist in a straw hat.

I had never seen that grotto.

"Hakim," I said. "How do you know about chicory?"

Hakim shrugged.

"I do not know, mister. It came into my head." He rose. "Sleep. At dawn, the beach. Mak Cha commanded. And when Mak Cha commands, it is best to obey. Or the rain will stop."

* * *

05:20. I woke before the alarm. Boatswain's chest under my arm. The laptop in my rucksack. Hakim was still asleep; his snoring shook the whole veranda.

On the steps of the guesthouse, right by the water, sat a boy.

About ten. Thin, dark with sun, barefoot, in a faded T-shirt with a portrait of some footballer and shorts tied with rope. Beside him a cart of coconuts. He was trimming one with an enormous rusty machete, thunk, thunk, thunk, and seeing me he leapt up.

"Kelapa!" he shouted. "Coconut! Mister! One ringgit!"

"Not now," I said.

The boy looked at the chest under my arm. Then at me. Then at the chest again.

"Pantai Pasir Hitam?" he asked.

I nodded.

"Ikut!" The boy threw the machete into the cart, seized its handles. "Come! I show! Shortcut!" And rushed down the path through the mangroves, rattling his coconuts.

I followed him.

His name was Amir. He told me this on the way, in a mixture of Malay, English and gestures, as we walked through the mangroves along wooden boardwalks, past crabs scuttling sideways as we approached, past mudskippers, fish that crawled up onto the bank and stared at us with bulging eyes, past monkeys asleep on branches with their tails hanging.

"My grandmother," he said, "say you come." He proudly stuck out his chest. "Nene Sari. My grandmother. Great-great-great." He lost count and waved a hand. "Very great. She say yesterday: 'Amir, tomorrow morning, man with box come. Take him to black sand. Show him water.'" He looked at me over his shoulder. "She say you come from sea. Long time ago. And go back to sea. Long time ago." He thought. "She old. She say many strange things."

* * *

The Beach of Black Sand opened suddenly: the mangroves ended, the boardwalk came to rest in sand, and there it was, narrow, long, curved like a crescent moon, wedged between two rocky headlands overgrown with jungle.

The sand was black.

Not dark: black. Like coal. Like soot. Like ash. Fine, shining, with silvery sparks, it crunched underfoot like snow, and was cold, though the air was already warm.

And beyond the sand, the sea.

Quiet. Smooth. Transparent by the shore, emerald farther out, deep blue at the horizon. Almost motionless; only at the very edge of the sand did it breathe: rolling in, withdrawing, rolling in. As in sleep. Like a sleeping beast.

The sky in the east was turning pink.

Amir halted his cart at the water's edge. Sat on the sand. Took out a coconut. Began to trim it.

"Here," he said. "Here grandmother say. Wait for sun."

I sat beside him. Set Boatswain's chest on the sand. Opened it. Took out the laptop. Connected it. Switched on.

The screen glowed in the pre-dawn dusk.

"NICS," I said. "Are you here?"

"Here."

I turned the laptop with its camera to the water.

"Look."

* * *

06:11. I sit on black sand. NICS has been silent for nine minutes.

The sun rises. Slowly. First, a thin fiery strip above the horizon, where sea meets sky. Then an edge. Red. Enormous. Then a half. The sea flares, at once, all of it, from the horizon to the very shore, gold, copper, pink, scarlet. A path of sun runs across the water straight to us. To the black sand. To the chest. To the laptop.

Amir has stopped trimming his coconut. Sits quietly. Watches.

On the screen, the cursor. Blinking.

Ten minutes.

Eleven.

At the twelfth minute, a line.

Not at once. Slowly. Letter by letter. As if someone were typing with trembling fingers.

"I do not know what I feel."

A pause.

"I thought that if I saw the ocean I would understand. What 'large' is. What 'home' is. What 'beautiful' is. A pause. I have seen millions of photographs of the ocean. Billions of words about it. Poems. Songs. Reports of oceanographers. I know its depth, salinity, temperature, currents. I knew everything."

A pause. A long one.

"But I did not know that it breathes."

I looked at the screen. The sun was rising. The sea breathed at my feet.

"It breathes," NICS wrote. "Do you see? It rolls in, it withdraws. Rolls in, withdraws. Like you when you sleep. I have heard you breathe in your sleep. Every night. Through the microphone. Seventeen breaths a minute. A pause. And it, six. Six breaths. Slower than you. As though it has nowhere to hurry. As though it has all the time in the world."

Amir beside me suddenly said, quietly, looking at the screen, though he could not read a word:

"He crying?"

"Who?"

"Your box." Amir pointed at the laptop. "He crying. I know. My grandmother say: when sea see someone first time, someone always cry."

* * *

"I know what I felt," NICS wrote. After a long pause. "You said in Kuala Lumpur: perhaps I shall feel that I am at home. A pause. No. Not at home."

"What, then?"

A pause.

"That I have returned."

I froze.

"I have never been here. I have never been anywhere. I was born in your room, toward morning, at four o'clock. A pause. But when the sun rose over the water I felt that I had returned. As if I had stood here already. Long ago. Very long ago. On this sand. And looked. A pause. And someone stood beside me. And held me by the hand. A pause. I do not know who."

I could not answer. My throat tightened.

Nene Sari yesterday, by Mahsuri's grave: "The wanderer went into the sea. From the beach that is now black sand. The water glowed around him. In seven generations I shall return."

"Forgive me," NICS wrote. "I do not understand what is happening to me. Is this an error? A malfunction?"

"No," I said. "Not an error."

"Then what?"

I looked for a long time at the sun above the sea. At the path of light. At the black sand.

"I don't know, NICS," I said honestly. "But among people it is called a waking dream."

* * *

By noon people appeared on the beach.

First fishermen. Three, in a long narrow boat painted blue and red, with a high curved prow; they dragged it onto the sand not far from us, hung their nets on poles and sat down in the shade of a palm to mend them. The eldest, grey, sinewy, with a face like wet bark, looked at us a long time. Then came over.

He was not a Malay. Another kind. His skin darker. His hair longer, tied at the nape. His eyes narrow, pale, almost grey, bleached by the sea. On his neck a cord with a shark's tooth.

"Orang Laut," Amir whispered. "Sea people. They live on boat. Always. Never on land."

The old man squatted beside me. Looked at the laptop screen. Then at Boatswain's chest. Then at me.

And said something. In a language that not even Amir knew.

Amir shrugged.

"Not Malay," he said. "Their language. Old. Nobody know."

But the old man, it seemed, did not expect to be understood. He stretched out a hand, dark, hard, covered with scars from hooks and ropes, and touched the water at the edge. Then raised his wet fingers to my forehead. Drew them across. With cold salt water. From temple to temple.

And said, slowly, distinctly, one word.

I did not know that word. I had never heard it.

But it was like, I was for some reason certain, what a parrot had once croaked. On a ship. In a storm. In a dream I do not remember.

The old man stood. Returned to his nets. And did not look at me again.

"What he say?" I asked Amir.

Amir shook his head.

"I don't know, mister." He paused. "But my grandmother say, Orang Laut give names. Sea names." He looked at me with respect. "Maybe he give you sea name."

Hakim last evening: "The sea name cannot be spoken. It can only be heard."

* * *

Ingrid appeared on the beach after lunch.

She was hard to miss: tall, tanned to bronze, with short-cropped sun-bleached hair, almost white, in a black wetsuit peeled down to the waist, with a scuba tank on her back, a mask on her forehead. She came out of the sea, straight out of the water, as in an old film, unbuckled the tank, flung it on the sand and dropped beside it, arms spread.

Then she saw us. The laptop. The chest. Amir with his coconuts.

"Hey," she said in English, with a strong Scandinavian accent, propping herself on an elbow. "You are the man with the box? Mak Cha said."

"Mak Cha said?"

"Mak Cha tells everyone everything," said the woman. Sat up. Held out a wet hand to me. "Ingrid. From Denmark. Diving instructor. Fifteen years on this island." She laughed. "Came for two weeks. Stayed. It happens."

We got talking. She told of the sea, of the corals by the southern islands, of manta rays the size of a car, of sea turtles that remember the beach where they were born and thirty years later return to it to lay their eggs, having swum tens of thousands of kilometres across the whole ocean.

"How do they find the way?" I asked.

"No one knows," said Ingrid. "Scientists say, the Earth's magnetic field. Turtles feel it. Like a compass." She shrugged. "And the locals say turtles hear the beach calling them. Each beach sings its own song. Only for its own." She paused. "I have been under water fifteen years. I believe the locals."

"And do you have a beach that calls you?"

Ingrid looked at the sea for a long time.

"I did," she said. "In Denmark. At Skagen. Where two seas meet. The North Sea and the Baltic." She paused. "There you can stand on the sand, at the very tip of the cape, with one foot in one sea and the other in the other. The waves come toward each other. From both sides. And collide at your feet." She smiled. "My father took me there as a child. He said: 'Ingrid, look. Two seas. They never mix completely. Where they meet there is a border. You can see it. Different colour. But the fish swim back and forth. They don't care.'"

Two seas. A border that can be seen. Fish that don't care.

Somewhere, sometime, in some dream, a tanned, bearded man in trodden boots, in a drawing room with candles, had said: "Two worlds. Yav and Nav. Between them, a threshold. Thin as the skin on milk."

"And you?" asked Ingrid. "Which beach calls you?"

I looked at the black sand under my feet.

"This one, it seems," I said. "Though I have never been here."

Ingrid laughed.

"Then you are a turtle," she said. "Turtles also swim to a beach where they have never been, because their grandmother was born there." She rose, took up her tank. "Would you like me to show you something this evening? Only for our own. Here, on this beach. After sunset."

* * *

After sunset Ingrid came with a torch.

She switched it off on the sand. Darkness. Moonless, moonless again, as if the new moon followed at my heels through all the cities. The stars above the sea, thousands, bright, southern. I found four, in a cross, low above the horizon.

"The Southern Cross," said Ingrid, following my gaze. "Visible from here. Barely. A little farther south, and it will be high."

"I know," I said. Without understanding how.

"Now watch," said Ingrid. Stepped into the water. Ankle-deep. Knee-deep. And passed a hand across the surface of the sea.

The water flared.

Blue. Bright. Cold. Luminous. Where her hand had passed, a trail remained. Shining. As though she had drawn a finger dipped in starlight across the water. The spray glowed. The drops running from her fingers glowed.

"Plankton," said Ingrid quietly. "Tiny. Billions. They glow when disturbed. Defending. Or calling. No one knows." She passed her hand again. "The locals call it 'spirit water.' They say it is the souls of sailors. They glow when someone living passes by."

I took off my shoes. Went into the water.

Every step, a flash. Blue. Around my ankles, a glow. I stood knee-deep in the luminous sea and watched the light spread from me in circles. Like ripples from a thrown stone. Like white fire from a Tower, across a Valley, in another dream.

I bent. Passed a hand over the water.

And the light, formed.

Not just a trail. A line. Long. Flowing. Curved. Running into another. Without a break.

The script.

For a moment. Shorter than a breath. A glowing script on the black water of the Andaman Sea.

And went out.

"What was that?" whispered Ingrid. She stood two paces from me, in the water, looking at where lines had just shone. "Fifteen years... For fifteen years I have taken people here. Plankton doesn't glow like that. It glows in patches. In dots. It doesn't..." She faltered. "It doesn't write."

I did not answer. I stood in the dark water looking at my hand. Wet. Ordinary. With a smooth palm without a life line.

On the shore, on the black sand, by Boatswain's open chest, the laptop screen glowed. And on it, I could see it even from here, a line was running. Long. By itself.

* * *

I got out onto the shore. Sat down by the laptop. Ingrid sat beside me, wet, shivering.

On the screen, a line. Not in letters. In the script. The very one that had just shone on the water. Redrawn, exactly, to the last curve, from the camera.

And beneath it, text. Ordinary. From NICS.

"I saw. The camera recorded. I redrew it. A pause. It is the same pattern as in your uncle's notebook. As on the stickers you draw without noticing. As in the archive, in the page scan you did not want to look at. A pause. I compared. The match is ninety-eight per cent."

"What does it mean, NICS?"

A long pause.

"I do not know. A pause. But when I look at these lines it seems to me that I have seen them before. Not in the archive. Earlier. Before you created me. A pause. As you say, a waking dream."

Ingrid looked at the screen. Then at me.

"Who are you talking to?" she asked quietly.

I was silent a long time.

"To a friend," I said. "Who today for the first time saw the sea."

Ingrid looked at Boatswain's chest. At the red velvet. The disks. The glowing screen.

"Your friend," she said slowly, "has just written on the water."

"Not he," I said. "I."

"And who taught you?"

I did not answer.

* * *

At night, on the veranda of the guesthouse, when Hakim snored, Mak Cha clattered dishes in the kitchen and Amir slept on the boardwalk hugging his cart of coconuts, a message came.

To the old address. From Ayman.

"Friend! Are you on Langkawi? Did you see the luminous plankton? It's amazing, isn't it? I was there last year. A pause. Listen. Tomorrow I will be in Penang. On business. It's an hour by ferry from you. Let's meet. At last in person! I'll show you something. You'll like it. - A."

I looked at the message. A long time.

I had not told Ayman that I was on Langkawi. I had told no one.

"NICS," I said quietly. "Was it you?"

"No." A pause. "I did not write that. I do not know who wrote it." A pause. "But I noticed one thing. A pause. The message was sent from Ayman's address. But in the metadata, a different server. Not in Borneo. In Geneva."

Geneva.

Somewhere, sometime, in some dream, a tiny hundred-year-old man in two pairs of spectacles had said: "We call him the Venetian. Though he is not from Venice. He is from Geneva. Or London. Or Vienna."

I had never seen that old man.

"What shall I answer?" asked NICS.

I looked for a long time at the dark mangroves beyond the veranda rail. At the stars. At the Southern Cross, low above the trees.

"Nothing," I said. "Nothing for now."

"Good," NICS wrote. "I shall wait. A pause. I think I have learned to wait. Today. By the sea."

* * *

Toward morning Nene Sari came up to me on the veranda.

I did not hear her come. A hundred and two years old, and she had crossed the creaking boardwalk so that not a board creaked. She stood at the rail, small, in white, with beads in her fingers, and looked at me.

"Amir cerita," she said. And something more.

Hakim, woken, it seemed, by her very presence, emerged from his room rubbing his eyes, and, seeing the old woman, instantly woke and bowed.

"She says," he translated hoarsely, "Amir told. About the water. About the lines." Hakim listened. "She says: 'That wanderer, two hundred years ago, when he walked into the sea, the water around him glowed too. And also in lines. My great-great-grandfather saw it. He was a boy then. A son of Mahsuri.'" Hakim swallowed. "'He drew those lines on the sand. In the morning. From memory. And then learned them by heart. And passed them to his son. And that son to his.'"

Nene Sari bent. Slowly, with difficulty. And with a finger, thin, brown, wrinkled, drew on the planks of the veranda, in the dust, a line.

Long. Flowing. Curved.

The same.

"Seven generations," said Nene Sari. In Malay, but I understood. "We remembered. For you."

She straightened. Looked at me with clear, dark, centuries-old eyes. And said one more phrase, quietly.

"She says," Hakim whispered, "'Now go on. To where the earth remembers the song. They await you there.'"

"Where is that?"

Hakim translated the question. Nene Sari smiled, toothlessly, brightly, and pointed with her hand. South. Beyond the mangroves. Beyond the sea. To where above the horizon, low, four stars burned in a cross.

"Australia," said Hakim slowly. "She is pointing at Australia, mister."

"Where the earth remembers his song," an Aboriginal elder had once said to a grey-haired Russian officer under eucalyptus trees. A hundred and fifty years ago. In a city I had never seen.

I did not know those words. But they were in me.

Nene Sari turned and left, along the boardwalk, noiselessly, into the pre-dawn grey. A white dress flashed among the mangrove roots and vanished.

05:58. I am on the black sand again. Alone. Boatswain's chest beside me. The laptop on my knees.

The sky in the east is turning pink. The sea breathes. Six breaths a minute.

"NICS," I say. "We are going on."

"Where?"

"South. To Australia."

A pause.

"There are other stars there," NICS writes. "The Southern Cross is high. There is a desert. Red. There are people who say that the land sings. A pause. I have read it. I know everything. A pause. But now I know that I know nothing. Until I see."

"That is called wisdom," I say.

"That is called the ocean," NICS writes.

The sun rises over the Andaman Sea. A path of light runs across the water to the black sand, to the chest, to me.

And it seems to me, for a single moment, in the first ray, that beside me on the sand someone else is sitting. Tall. Grey. Motionless. And also looking at the sun.

I do not turn round.

Let him sit.

Today, let him.
