Chapter XXI
Between Two Doors

Dawn found me in the same place, on the windowsill, with the cooled pouch in my palm and one perfectly clear, soberingly simple thought in my head: to keep silent any longer was senseless.

Not because silence had ceased to be safe - on the contrary, it seemed to me, for the first time in these days, to be safer than any confession. But because I could no longer physically carry this alone. My body was worn out not even by sleeplessness but by the very effort of constantly weighing what might be said aloud and what must be concealed - an effort which, I suddenly understood, exhausts far more than any direct fear.

Beyond the door, in the corridor, on his mattress across the threshold, Orhan was still asleep - under my woollen blanket, curled up like a dog, still not having let go of the knife. I stepped over him carefully, trying not to wake him. He did not wake - only muttered something angry in his sleep and pulled the blanket over his head.

I went downstairs earlier than usual, finding the house still in that fragile, drowsy stillness that Eastern houses have just before waking - when the women's quarter has already begun to stir behind the thick curtains but the men have not yet come out to morning tea. Ferhat sat alone in the kitchen, with a cup in his hands, evidently not having slept enough either - on his cheek there still showed the dark trace of yesterday's soot from Karagöz's lamp, which he had never bothered to wash off.

"You did not sleep," he said, glancing at me, and it was not a question.

"I did not," I confirmed, sitting down opposite. "Ferhat, I need to tell you everything. All of it. Not as yesterday, in scraps - but everything, from the very beginning."

He set down his cup, looked at me attentively and simply nodded - without a single superfluous word, without surprise, as if he had been awaiting this moment for more than a day.

* * *

And I told him.

About waking in the square without a single memory of my own name. About the two stones that warmed or chilled whenever something incomprehensible happened near me. About the strange delusion - columns, darkness, cold under the ribs, even before I went to his grandfather with the note. About the figure in the square on the first day - the very one that Kemal had drawn in charcoal two nights before my appearance. About the night when I could not move, and the walls melted, and beyond them were bells, and cannons, and boys with pigeons. About the stones that burned on the candle at the jeweller Karapet's. About the obelisk at the hippodrome by which I wept, not knowing why. About the eye at its tip, which I knew though I had never seen it. About the chest to which cats do not go down.

And about this night. About the figure that had entered my room - not at the window, not in the alley, but here, on this side of the windowsill. About the stones that flared with heat when I reached toward it. About how, leaving, it had gently touched the blue eye above the door.

"And when I reached toward it," I said, trying to keep my voice from faltering on these words, "it seemed to me for a second that I was reaching toward myself. Not a stranger. Toward myself - only another one. A later one."

Ferhat was silent for a long time. Long enough for me to take fright that I had gone too far, that what I had told sounded like the ravings of a madman to whom one cannot entrust home, family or one's own safety. But when at last he spoke, there was not a drop of that fright in his voice which I had feared.

"Do you remember," he said, "I told you about the shadow that sometimes becomes visible?"

"I remember."

"Grandfather told me this from childhood. But he also said something else - what I did not mention yesterday, because I was not sure you were ready to hear it. He said: sometimes a man carries in himself not one shadow but several - as if he had lived not one life but many, and all of them walk behind him at the same time, usually only invisible to each other. And only in rare moments - on the edge of sleep, on the edge of death, on the edge of something very important - do these shadows begin to notice one another."

I felt a chill run over my skin which had nothing to do with the morning coolness of the kitchen.

"Do you think I am one of such people?"

"I think," Ferhat answered carefully, choosing his words with the care with which one usually speaks of things one only half believes in oneself, "that you are either one of such people, or simply a very tired traveller who has been through too much in these days and now sees connections where there are none. And I, honestly, do not know which of the two options frightens me more."

I involuntarily smiled - not because it was funny, but because this honesty, this readiness to admit his own bewilderment instead of pretending he had a ready answer, seemed to me at that moment dearer than any consolation.

"May I ask something?" said Ferhat. "That silhouette tonight. You said you felt as though you were reaching toward yourself. A later one. What exactly did you mean?"

I thought, searching for words for a feeling which I myself had not managed to clothe in a coherent thought.

"I don't know how to explain more precisely," I said. "Simply... imagine you meet a man on the street and suddenly, inexplicably, you recognize in him not a stranger but yourself - only ten or twenty years older, having lived through something you have not yet lived. It felt roughly like that. Not fear of the alien. Rather a strange, almost unbearable longing for someone I may yet become - or already was."

"Or," Ferhat added quietly, "whom you already are, only you do not yet know it."

I said nothing, because I had decidedly nothing with which to object.

* * *

"Hey!" came a sleepy but already indignant voice from above. "Hey!"

Down the stairs, stumbling, tumbled Orhan - tousled, with the imprint of the mattress on his cheek, with my woollen blanket thrown over his shoulders like a cloak, and with a knife in his hand. He saw me - and stopped dead. Looked for a long time. Then he lifted the blanket, showed it to me, jabbed a finger at me, then at himself, then again at the blanket - and said something, indignantly and incoherently.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, barely suppressing laughter, "did you cover him? At night?"

"I did," I admitted. "It was cold."

Orhan heard the translation. His face slowly reddened - from the neck to the roots of his hair. He opened his mouth, closed it. Threw the blanket onto my knees. Then, after thinking, took it back and put it on his own shoulders again. And said something short, gruff, turning to the wall.

"He says," translated Ferhat, by now openly roaring with laughter, "that if you cover him once more, he will cut your throat." Ferhat wiped his eyes. "And the blanket he will keep. As... compensation. For the insult."

Orhan sat down angrily on the stool by the door, wrapped in my blanket, and began to sharpen his knife on a stone so furiously as if the knife were to blame for someone having dared to care for him.

"And also," he suddenly added, not raising his head. Ferhat, raising his brows in surprise, translated: "He says: in the night he heard someone pass along the corridor. Past him. Light. Like the wind. He wanted to wake - and could not. As if someone had laid a hand over his eyes." Ferhat paused. "He says the hand was warm. And that he was not afraid. And that this he likes least of all."

* * *

We sat a little longer in silence - not a heavy one, but that special, friendly silence that arises between people who have just shared something truly important with each other. Then into the kitchen, shuffling with his habitual morning steps, came Suleyman - and, seeing our faces, stopped in the doorway, as if sensing at once that he had come upon no ordinary morning conversation.

Ferhat briefly, in a low voice, conveyed to his grandfather the substance of what he had heard - not all of it, but enough for the old man to grasp the seriousness of the moment. Suleyman listened without interrupting, then slowly lowered himself onto a free stool and said something I did not understand, but which made Ferhat raise his brows in surprise.

"What did he say?" I asked.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, "whether you have ever dreamed that you stand between two doors at once. Not choosing either - but precisely standing between."

I went cold. And slowly, without a word, reached into my pocket - where beside the note in Persian, the blank sheet and the widow's warm coin lay a sheet folded in four. Kemal's drawing. I unfolded it and laid it on the table before Suleyman.

Two doors. Side by side, wall to wall. One open, the other closed. And between them, on the threshold, back to the viewer, a man in a turban. With black and white circles in his hands.

Suleyman looked at the drawing for a long time. Then at me. Then again at the drawing. His fingers, which had been running the beads, stopped.

"Kemal," he said quietly, not turning.

The boy stood in the kitchen doorway. I had not heard him come. Barefoot, in a nightshirt, with charcoal, as always, behind his ear. He nodded to his grandfather. Gravely. Once.

Suleyman slowly turned to him and asked something. Kemal answered - softly, briefly.

"Grandfather asks when he drew it," Ferhat translated in a whisper. "Kemal says - on the night when he sat at your threshold. He says: 'The Guest showed me. While you slept. He stood at the window, and I saw two doors. And between them - you.'"

No one said a word. Orhan by the wall stopped sharpening his knife.

"Yes," I answered Suleyman at last. "That is exactly how it feels. All the time. Since the first morning."

Suleyman nodded, as if he had received confirmation of a long-held guess of his own, and spoke several more phrases - this time slowly, weightily, clearly counting on his grandson to translate each word with due accuracy.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, "that among the merchants and wanderers who passed through this port in his long life, now and then there turned up some about whom similar stories were told. People who seemed to forget not only their own name but also which of their many lives was the true one. Some considered it God's trial. Others - a sickness. Grandfather himself does not know which of these is true." Ferhat listened on. "He says: his father saw one such. The jeweller Karapet - another, through his great-grandfather. The bath attendant - a third, twenty years ago. The widow's husband - a fourth, the one who brought the chest. He says: how many there were, no one knows. Perhaps four. Perhaps a hundred. And perhaps..." Ferhat faltered and looked at me. "And perhaps one. Who comes again and again."

One, who comes again and again. And each time leaves behind the same thing: if another comes - feed him.

"Grandfather says," Ferhat finished, "that if you really are one of such people, you need neither a healer nor a mullah, but one who can speak with time as freely as you and I are speaking now at this table."

* * *

"Ahmet Baba," said Ferhat. "Grandfather is speaking of Ahmet Baba."

"Who is that?"

"A dervish. An old man. He lives closer to Hagia Sophia, in a small lodge by an old cemetery." Ferhat paused. "Not quite an ordinary dervish - educated, he studied in different lands: in Cairo, in Baghdad, in Bukhara, they say even among the Franks. Grandfather sometimes consults him on important questions. People say he understands things which to an ordinary imam would seem heresy, and to an ordinary scholar superstition. Hodja Nureddin does not like him. Says he knows too much for a believer."

"Can you take me to him?"

"In a few days," said Ferhat. "Not sooner - he has, rumour has it, his own routine, and to appear without warning there is not done. He receives only those who..." Ferhat faltered, searching for the word. "Grandfather says - only those who have solved his riddle."

"What riddle?"

Suleyman, understanding the question without translation, smiled into his beard and spoke - slowly, in a singsong, as verses are read. Ferhat translated after him:

"'To enter me, do not come from the end from which you came. Bring what you do not have. Ask what you know the answer to. And do not give your name, even if you remember it.'"

I repeated it to myself. Do not come from the end from which you came. Bring what you do not have. Ask what you know the answer to. Do not give your name.

"It seems I am just the right sort," I said. "At least for the last condition."

Ferhat laughed. Suleyman too - for the first time I heard him laugh aloud: dryly, briefly, like a branch crackling in a fire. Even Orhan by the wall snorted into the blanket.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, when he had finished laughing, "that the rest you will solve on the road. The road, he says, is the best teacher of riddles. On it all the answers come to meet you themselves."

* * *

Later that same morning, when the house was already filled with its habitual bustle - Yusuf counting something in his books, Suleyman, as usual, setting out the backgammon board, the women behind the curtain clattering dishes - a jubilant cry from Akmal came from the roof.

He tumbled down the stairs so swiftly that he nearly knocked Orhan off his feet, burst into the courtyard holding something in his hands - and I saw: the white dove. With feathered feet and a fan tail.

"She came back!" he shouted in his language so that I understood without translation. "She came back by herself! In the night! By herself!"

He raced about the courtyard showing the dove to everyone - grandfather, uncle, Orhan, Ferhat - and they all nodded and smiled in turn, and the dove sat calmly in his palms, as if used to being carried. Then Akmal ran up to me and thrust her under my nose.

"Look!" he said - in my language, one of the few words he had managed to learn. "Look! By herself!"

"I see," I said. "By herself."

Ferhat, standing near, gave me a long, understanding look. He said nothing. But the corner of his mouth twitched.

"You know," he said quietly, when Akmal had dashed off again, "Father used to say: everything that flies away comes back by itself. If someone is waiting very much." He paused. "Akmal believes. Every day. Let him believe."

"Let him," I agreed.

* * *

Then I went out onto the balcony to look, for the last time before breakfast, at the city, which was little by little becoming almost dear to me.

And I saw it again.

This time the figure was not hiding in the alley, not waiting at the very edge of sight. It stood in the middle of the street, in plain view, among morning passers-by, none of whom, it seemed, noticed it - tall, grey, motionless, its gaze fixed straight on my balcony with the same inexplicable mixture of warmth and dread that I had felt that night in my own room.

I froze, gripping the railing. Below, a fruit seller with a basket of apricots on his head walked through the very place where the figure stood - walked straight through, noticing not the slightest obstacle, as if there were nothing there at all. After him went a woman in a veil with a jug. Then - the simit boy with a tray of bagels, the very one from the first morning: he even, it seemed, shouted his "Taze simit!" straight through the grey shoulder.

None of them saw. Only a ginger, mangy cat sitting on the threshold of the house opposite suddenly arched her back, bristled and hissed - not at the people, but at the empty, as it seemed to all of them, place in the middle of the street.

The figure did not move. Did not retreat, did not melt as before. Simply stood - and I was ready to swear, though the distance did not allow making out such details for certain, that during that long, drawn-out moment it barely perceptibly, almost imperceptibly nodded to me. Not a threat. Not a greeting in the usual sense of the word. Rather a confirmation - of the kind with which one confirms an agreement made without a single word spoken.

And then beside me, on the balcony, someone sighed softly.

I turned. Kemal stood at my shoulder - barefoot, with charcoal behind his ear. He was looking where I was looking. At the street. At the figure that no one saw but the cat.

He saw.

The boy slowly raised his hand - and, looking at the figure, nodded back to it. As barely noticeably. As gravely. As one nods to an acquaintance.

The figure turned its head - toward him. Paused. Then turned away and unhurriedly walked off along the street, through the crowd that did not notice it, until it vanished round the corner as ordinarily as the most ordinary passer-by vanishes round a corner.

"Kemal," I whispered. "Who is he?"

The boy looked at me. For a long time. With his grave, un-childlike eyes. And said - slowly, distinctly, one word in his own language which I already knew:

"Misafir."

The Guest. That was what they called him - the one who came at night. And just so, from the first day in this house, they called me.

I did not manage to ask which of the two of us he meant. Kemal had already gone downstairs, without looking back.

* * *

I stood on the balcony a long while after that, feeling the last remnants of doubt - those convenient, saving doubts that write everything off to fatigue and the play of light - finally leave me.

The three worlds in which I had lived - or, perhaps, had not lived but only visited in snatches - were not a dream. The note with its incomprehensible script. The stones, now cooling, now flaring with heat without any visible cause. The chest in the cellar of a stranger's house, bearing the same pattern as the note in my pocket. The coin, warm from someone else's years of longing. The silhouette that had entered my room at night as freely as an owner enters a house returning after a long absence. All this composed itself not into the hallucination of an overloaded mind, but into something whole, something possessing its own logic, though one as yet inaccessible to me.

I recalled the two words that Hasan and Suleyman had managed to make out in my note on the day when we sat over it, bent in the light from the window. Return. Keep until the appointed time. Then these words had seemed to me a riddle without a key - now, standing on the balcony and looking after the vanished figure, I for the first time thought: what if I myself, though not remembering it, had been both the author of the note and its addressee at once? What if this whole path - from the square with old men playing backgammon to this very balcony - had been not a search for something external but a long, circuitous return to myself?

"Misafir," Kemal had said. The Guest. He or I.

And behind me - of this I no longer doubted for a second - someone was watching. Patiently. Unremittingly. Since the very first morning on that sun-flooded street, and perhaps long before it - long before I had even learned to count time in hours and days rather than in something else, more spacious and far less obvious.

I did not yet know whether this was friend or foe, guardian or hunter. Did not know how he was connected with the man in the grey frock coat who had asked about me in the port. Did not know how much more truth would be revealed to me before I understood the true meaning of the words in my own note. Did not know what sort of man awaited me at Hagia Sophia, and whether he would agree to speak at all with a wanderer whose story sounded madder than any he had heard before. But one thing I now knew quite firmly, with that final clarity which comes only after long hours of doubt: the road back, to the former, cosy ignorance, was closed to me forever.

Below, in the courtyard, Suleyman clicked the pieces of the backgammon board. Yusuf called everyone to breakfast. Akmal raced about with the dove. Orhan, wrapped in my blanket, sullenly sharpened his knife. Ferhat raised his head and waved to me from the kitchen threshold. Kemal sat on a step and drew something in charcoal on a flagstone - two doors, I was sure, without seeing.

Whatever awaited me ahead - in Sultanahmet, with the old dervish, or in my own visions, coming ever more often - I had to go forward. Because the one who had been watching me from the shadows, to all appearances, had no intention of going away.

And so neither had I.
