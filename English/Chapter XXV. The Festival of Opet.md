Chapter XXV
The Festival of Opet

The god came out of the sanctuary at dawn, and the whole city fell to its knees.

I watched it from the topmost step of the great temple - from where Pharaoh was supposed to stand to greet the god - and I think I shall never forget how it looked: as though a gust of wind had passed over a field of ripe barley. From the temple steps to the very river, along the whole Avenue of Processions, on the roofs, on the walls, on the trees into which boys had climbed since the night - everywhere the eye reached, thousands of people in a single instant sank to their knees and touched their foreheads to the ground. And silence. Such a silence that I heard, far off, by the river, a lone heron cry.

And then the sistra rang out.

* * *

The festival of Opet did not begin in Luxor. Everyone knew this, but few remembered it: before the god sets out to the south, to his southern home, he must come out to the people here, in the capital - show himself, receive gifts, hear petitions. For ten days his barque stands at the great temple, and every day people come to it. And at the new moon the barques set out on their journey along the river.

Ten days. I had ten days.

"My Pharaoh," Senusret whispered to me, standing, as always, at my right hand, a little behind. He was in full ceremonial dress - in a striped headcloth, in a broad collar, with a bronze sword at his belt - but he was looking not at the god but at the crowd. "Two detachments by the Avenue - those very ones. Of those that are to go as the procession's guard. Their commanders have not taken off their scarabs."

I looked where he indicated with his eyes. Below, on both sides of the Avenue of Processions, stood warriors with spears - a living wall dividing the god's road from the crowd. And on two of them, the commanders, something blue glinted on the chest. Temple scarabs.

"I see," I said.

"Khaemuas is standing near them."

I saw that too. Khaemuas, in the finest linen, in the costliest bracelets, stood at the start of the Avenue and was speaking quietly with the commanders. Laughing. Clapping one on the shoulder.

"Let him stand," I said.

* * *

From the dark maw of the sanctuary, from behind the high cedar doors plated with gold, the barque slowly floated out.

Not the real one - the river barque that would bear the god along the Nile waited at the quay. This one was portable: narrow, long, carved of cedar and covered from prow to stern in gold, so that it hurt to look at it in the sun. The prow and the stern of the barque ended in rams' heads - golden, with curled horns, with eyes of lapis lazuli. In the middle, under a canopy of the finest linen, stood a closed shrine - a small temple surrounded by carved figures of gods with outspread wings. Inside, I knew, stood the god. No one but the High Priest and Pharaoh ever saw his face.

The barque was carried on shoulders. Twenty-four priests, twelve on each side, on long poles - shaven, in white, barefoot. They walked slowly, in time with the sistra, and the barque floated above their heads so smoothly that it seemed truly to sail upon water.

And before the barque walked Nefer-Ka.

The High Priest in full vestments - in a leopard skin over white linen, with a staff crowned by the Eye, with a censer in the other hand from which rose thick, sweet, intoxicating smoke. He walked, looking neither at me nor at the crowd. Only forward. And his face was calm, almost serene - the face of a man who has nowhere to hurry.

Behind the barque came the singers. Twenty young priestesses in white, with loose hair, with sistra in their hands - and they sang. Sang so that shivers ran down my back: high, in unison, in a melody ancient as the temple itself, in which there was not one note familiar to me but which for some reason seemed familiar. Among them - I saw her at once - was Khemu. In the second row, at the edge. She sang like the others. But when the barque drew level with me, Khemu for an instant raised her eyes - and barely perceptibly, almost imperceptibly, lowered her sistrum. Once. Then raised it again.

The sign we had agreed upon through Iti. "Watch closely. Something is being prepared."

* * *

The barque halted at the foot of the steps. The priests lowered the poles onto their shoulders, straightened. Nefer-Ka turned to me and bowed - exactly as far as custom required, not a finger lower.

"Son of Ra," he said loudly, so that all in the square could hear, "the god has come out to his people. Greet him."

I descended the steps. Everything had been learned beforehand, dozens of times, from scrolls brought to me by Ani the Elder, keeper of ceremonies: three steps, a bow, a libation of milk from a golden jug, a libation of wine, the burning of incense, the words of greeting. I did everything as it should be done. My hands did not tremble. Nor my voice. The crowd was silent, holding its breath.

And then Nefer-Ka said what was in no scroll.

"Son of Ra," he said, raising his voice. "The people suffer. Three years of drought. Three years without a flood. The people ask - why? And the people want to hear an answer. Not from the priests. Not from Pharaoh. From the god himself." He turned to the crowd. "The god has come out to you! Ask! The god will answer!"

A rumble rolled across the square - at first timid, then louder, louder, until it turned into a roar.

The oracle. Nefer-Ka had opened the oracle. Right now, on the first day, at the temple steps, before the eyes of the whole city.

I knew how it goes. People come up to the barque and ask a question. The barque answers: if it sways forward, toward the questioner - "yes." If it draws back - "no." If it freezes - the god does not wish to answer. So it had always been. And so it had always been - I knew this, as every Pharaoh knew - that the priests carrying the barque move as they are bidden.

Senusret behind my back drew in air noisily.

* * *

The first to approach the barque was an old man - grey, bent, in a ragged loincloth. He fell to his knees right at the poles and cried in a hoarse, trembling voice:

"O God! Will the water return?"

The barque swayed forward. Slowly, solemnly. Yes.

The crowd gasped - and burst into cries of joy. The old man sobbed, kissing the ground.

The second to come out was a middle-aged man, in clean clothes, with a blue scarab on his chest. He did not fall to his knees. He stood straight before the barque, drew himself up and cried so loudly that his voice carried across the whole square - too loudly, too clearly, too rehearsed:

"O God! Hast thou turned away from the throne?"

The square froze.

I saw Nefer-Ka, standing at the prow of the barque, barely perceptibly turn his head toward the bearers. Saw twelve priests on the right side slightly tense their shoulders. Saw the barque begin slowly, very slowly, to draw back - from me, from the steps, from the throne. "No." No - meaning he has not turned away? Or... I did not understand. The crowd did not understand. A whisper ran across the square.

And then happened what no one expected.

One of the bearers - the youngest, in the back row on the left side, a thin youth with a shaven head - suddenly stumbled. His foot turned on a step, he lurched, the pole jerked on his shoulder - and the barque, instead of drawing smoothly back, sharply listed. To the left. Toward the right side of the square. To where beyond the Avenue of Processions, beyond the wall of the temple court, the long, squat roofs of the temple granaries rose.

And froze - tilted, pointing with its golden ram's prow straight at the granaries.

* * *

It became so quiet on the square that I heard my own heart beating.

Then someone in the crowd cried out - one voice, piercing, a woman's:

"The granaries! The god points at the granaries!"

And the square exploded.

"Bread! The god shows where the bread is!"

"The Temple hides grain! The god has said so!"

"The ambassador from the south spoke the truth!"

"The granaries!"

People leapt up from their knees, shouted, pointed with their hands. Warriors by the Avenue closed their spears, holding back the crowd. The commanders with scarabs on their chests looked about in confusion at Khaemuas. Khaemuas went so white that he became the same colour as his linen.

The bearers hastily straightened the barque. The young priest who had stumbled on the step stood white as chalk and looked at his pole with such horror as if it had bitten him.

And Nefer-Ka...

Nefer-Ka did not stir. He stood at the prow of the barque, the censer in his hand, and his face was motionless as the face of a stone statue. Only the smoke from the censer - I noticed - suddenly ceased to rise in an even thread and began to tear, to jerk, as though the hand holding the censer were trembling.

He turned to me. And for the first time in all the time I had known him, I saw in his eyes what I had never seen there.

Not anger. Not fear. Surprise.

He had not expected this. This had not been his design.

I took a step forward. Raised my hand. The square, seeing it, began to quiet - slowly, in waves, from the first rows to the last.

"The god has spoken," I said, as loudly as I could. "The Temple granaries are already sealed with the royal seal. For the good of the Temple." I paused. "In three days, in honour of the festival, the grain from them will be distributed to the people. To all the people. From the hands of Pharaoh and the High Priest. Together." I turned to Nefer-Ka. "Does the High Priest agree?"

Nefer-Ka looked at me. For a long time. The square did not breathe.

Then he slowly bowed his head.

"The will of the god is law for the Temple," he said. In an even, resonant, serene voice. "The Temple agrees."

The square roared so that pigeons took flight from the roofs.

* * *

The young bearer's name was Nakht.

Iti found him in the evening - I had ordered him found. Nakht was hiding in a small cell behind the temple bakery and trembling like a leaf, though the evening was hot. Iti brought him to me in the garden - through the gate of blind Meru, who, as Iti told me afterward, let them through without a riddle, only said: "Today the god has set a riddle himself. Meru is resting."

Nakht was very young - sixteen, no more - thin, with sticking-out ears and so frightened a face that I felt sorry for him before he spoke.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered, falling to his knees. "I did not do it on purpose. I swear. I swear by Maat, I swear by my heart, I swear..."

"Rise, Nakht. I am not angry."

He did not rise. He stayed on his knees, shaking.

"The High Priest ordered it," he whispered in confusion. "In the morning. All the bearers. When a man with a scarab asks about the throne - to draw back. Slowly. All together. So that the people would understand: the god has turned away." Nakht swallowed. "We all knew. We always know. We are always told where to move."

"And you stumbled."

"I did not stumble!" Nakht suddenly cried out - and at once clapped a palm to his mouth, frightened by his own voice. Then whispered: "My Pharaoh. I did not stumble. I was... pulled."

"Pulled?"

"The pole." Nakht raised his eyes to me, full of tears. "It did it itself. It became heavy - suddenly, like a stone. And pulled to the left. I could not hold it. No one could have held it. It pulled that way - toward the granaries - as if the god himself in the shrine had turned and looked there." He sobbed. "No one will believe me. They will say I did it on purpose. Will say I was bribed. They will send me to the southern cataracts, where there are scorpions. Or..."

"Nakht," I said. "Why do you think anyone will say you were bribed?"

Nakht lowered his head.

"Because my family," he whispered, "my mother and sisters last moon received grain on loan. From the royal granaries. By your decree. Without pledging the field." He paused. "Everyone knows this. They will all say I repaid Pharaoh."

Iti, standing by, said quietly:

"My Pharaoh. I believe him."

"Why?"

"Because liars do not weep, my Pharaoh," said Iti. "Liars explain."

I looked a long time at the youth kneeling before me. Then I bent and raised him - by the shoulders, as once, four years ago, I had raised another boy out of the water.

"Nakht," I said. "You will go on carrying the barque. All ten days. And to Luxor, too, you will go. Do you understand? If the High Priest wishes to send you away - say that Pharaoh ordered that you precisely carry the left rear pole. Because the god chose you."

Nakht looked at me as at a madman.

"And if... and if I am pulled again?"

"Then," I said, "let it pull."

* * *

In the evening there was a feast in the palace - the first feast of the festival of Opet, which, by custom, Pharaoh gave for the nobility, the priests and the ambassadors. There were no ambassadors this time - Irikhet had left with the embassy for the south three days before. All had left, except blind Nanakhat: she had stayed in a small house by the river, telling the ambassador that she "had not yet finished speaking with the son of Ra" and would wait - but the hall was full all the same.

Musicians played harps and lutes. Dancers in transparent linen whirled, clicking ivory clappers. Servants carried dishes - roast geese stuffed with figs, fish from the Delta baked in lotus leaves, bread of ten kinds, beer in enormous jars, dates, pomegranates, honey. The hall hummed like a hive.

And in the middle of the hall, between the tables, a dwarf danced.

His name was Djedu. He came up to my waist, with an enormous head, short crooked legs, a broad, mocking, wrinkled face and such clever eyes as are found only in jesters and sages. Djedu served at the temple of Ptah - dwarfs had from ancient times been kept at temples, because it was believed that gods love their dances - but everyone invited him to feasts, because no one in the capital could make people laugh as he did.

He danced, tumbled, pulled faces, mimicked the noble guests - so aptly that they themselves roared with laughter at themselves. He did Paser seasick on a papyrus boat - and Paser, crimson with laughter, threw a date at him. He did Nebamun counting grains of barley one by one - and Nebamun, to everyone's astonishment, smiled. He did Khaemuas jingling bracelets - so that the bracelets on his arms jingled louder than on Khaemuas himself - and he laughed, but somehow stiffly, uncertainly.

And then Djedu rolled up to my throne, clambered up the steps, settled at my feet like a cat and said - loudly enough for the nearest tables to hear:

"Son of Ra! Want a riddle? A temple one! It is set to the god when he is bored!"

"Go on, Djedu."

The dwarf winked.

"'I am carried on shoulders, but I am not a child. I am bowed to, but I am not a king. I am questioned, but others answer for me. And today I answered myself - and everyone was frightened.' Who am I?"

It became quiet in the hall. Too quiet. Someone by the wall coughed. Nefer-Ka, seated in the place of honour at my left hand, slowly set his cup down on the table.

"A god," I said.

"A god!" Djedu shrieked, tumbling down my steps. "The son of Ra has solved it! The son of Ra always solves! Even what the god sets himself!"

The hall laughed - at first uncertainly, then louder. Nefer-Ka did not laugh. He looked at the dwarf - long, coldly, memorizing.

Djedu, it seems, did not notice. Or pretended not to.

* * *

Later, when the feast was in full swing and no one paid attention to the throne, Djedu rolled up to me again - quietly, unnoticed, like a cat. Settled at my feet and spoke in an undertone, not looking at me, as though to himself:

"Son of Ra. Want a tale? Not a temple one. An old one. About a man who left home."

"Tell it."

Djedu scratched his enormous head.

"'There was a man,' he said. 'His name was Sinuhe. He served at the court of Pharaoh. Served well. And once he heard what he should not have heard. That old Pharaoh had died. And that at court there was a plot. And Sinuhe was afraid. So afraid that he fled. Alone. At night. Across the desert. North, to foreign lands, to the Asiatics. Nearly died of thirst. Nomads saved him. And he began to live among them. Married the chieftain's daughter. Had children. Grew rich. Became a great warrior. Defeated in single combat the mightiest champion of that land. Lived there many years. Many. A whole life, almost.'"

Djedu paused.

"'And then he grew old. And every night he dreamed of Egypt. The Nile. Palms. Temples. Every night. And he understood that he could not die in a foreign land. Because he who dies in a foreign land is buried in a sheepskin, and will not find the road into the Duat. He will wander between worlds. Forever. Not remembering who he is and where from.'" The dwarf raised his clever eyes to me. "'And Sinuhe wrote a letter to the new Pharaoh. And Pharaoh answered: "Return. You have done nothing wrong. Your place is here. Your tomb is already ready." And Sinuhe returned. Old. Grey. In foreign clothes, with foreign customs, speaking with a foreign accent. And Pharaoh's children, seeing him, cried: "That is not Sinuhe! That is an Asiatic!" And Pharaoh said: "No. It is he. He was simply far away for a long time."'"

Djedu fell silent.

"A good tale," I said.

"Good," agreed Djedu. "And do you know what it teaches, son of Ra?"

"What?"

"That one can go very far," said the dwarf. "Live a whole foreign life. Forget one's language. Become another. But if you dream of your home - then home still remembers you." He paused. "And one thing more. That one must return not when one is ready. But when one is called."

"And who will call?"

Djedu smiled - broadly, toothlessly, as only jesters can smile.

"The one whose tomb is already ready for you," he said. And, before I could answer, tumbled down the steps and raced into the middle of the hall, where he was already loudly demanding wine and impersonating Nefer-Ka waving a censer - so like that half the hall roared with laughter and the other half looked fearfully askance at the High Priest.

* * *

Nefer-Ka came up to me when the feast was drawing to a close - himself, without retinue, without Bakenren, whom no one had seen in the palace since the day the ambassador named the sign on the sacks. It was said that Bakenren had been "sent to pray" in a distant temple. Various things were said.

"My Pharaoh," said Nefer-Ka, bowing exactly as far as custom required. "A remarkable day."

"Remarkable," I agreed.

"The god spoke himself." Nefer-Ka looked at me - calmly, gently. "Such a thing has not happened for a long time. Many years. Perhaps since the time of the First Ones."

"Perhaps."

"The Temple will distribute the grain as you ordered. In three days." He paused. "And at the new moon, in ten days, the barques will go south. To Luxor. The god must enter his southern home. So it has always been." He inclined his head slightly. "And Pharaoh, naturally, will lead him. As all Pharaohs before you led."

"Naturally," I said.

"In Luxor," Nefer-Ka went on in the same gentle voice, "on the last day of the festival, there is a rite. An ancient one. Very ancient. It is not performed every year. Only in special years. In years of great calamity." He paused. "Pharaoh enters the inner court of the god. Alone. Without guards. As the First Ones entered. And there is revealed to him what was hidden. What will help to bring back the water."

"What exactly?"

Nefer-Ka smiled - with that same smile, condescending, patient.

"If I could say that, my Pharaoh, the rite would not be needed." He bowed again. "Good night. A remarkable day." And already leaving, as though recalling something insignificant, he added over his shoulder: "Ah yes. I am told that in the lower storehouse of the Library old Isiret has found some sodden account of grain. Wrapped in leather. An interesting find. Very interesting." He paused. "A pity that it was sodden. Some things had better not be sodden."

He left. I sat on the throne, and dancers whirled around me in the hall, harps rang, the dwarf Djedu roared with laughter. And I felt the cold rise from under the ribs, on the left - the familiar, accustomed cold - and understood that Nefer-Ka knew. Not everything. But enough.

* * *

Khemu came to me at night - not to the garden, not to the pond, but straight to the palace, through the chambers of Nefret, the senior over the servants, who led her through the corridors, wrapped in a servant's veil, with a basket of linen in her arms. Nefret, as it turned out, could do more than carry gossip: she could also make sure the right people passed unnoticed.

"My Pharaoh," said Khemu as soon as the door closed behind Nefret. She was trembling. "Tonight. I saw it again."

"The columns?"

"The columns." She swallowed. "But this time - closer. Clearer. I saw the court. The inner court of the god in Luxor. I saw the chest of black wood - open. I saw your hands - and the stones in them. And I saw..."

She fell silent.

"What, Khemu?"

"A hand," she whispered. "The one that was raised. I saw it more clearly. Not the face - the face is in shadow. But the hand." She raised her eyes to me, huge, with smeared kohl. "On it was a bracelet, my Pharaoh. Gold. Broad. And when the hand rose - the bracelet jingled."

I was silent for a long time.

"Half the court wears bracelets," I said at last.

"I know," said Khemu. "I know. I say only what I saw." She paused. "And one more thing. Merit asked me to pass it on. Today, when the barque tilted toward the granaries, Merit was standing at the temple gates. She says she saw. She says: the bearer did not stumble. She says: above the barque at that moment someone stood. Tall. Grey. Taller than the barque. He laid his hand on the pole - and the pole tilted."

"Merit saw him?"

"Merit says she did," whispered Khemu. "Merit says she saw him before. Long ago. Many years ago." She paused. "In the year that you ascended the throne."

* * *

I climbed to the palace roof toward morning. Alone. No - not alone: Senusret, of course, was there before me. He stood at the parapet, looking south, to where, many days' journey up the river, beyond the dried fields, beyond the desert, lay Luxor.

"Ten days," he said, without turning.

"Ten days."

"The inner court. Alone. Without guards." Senusret paused. "The blind old woman told me: stay at the gates. Otherwise you will both not return."

"I remember."

"My Pharaoh." He at last turned to me. In the pre-dawn greyness his face was stone, but his voice was not. "I have served the throne longer than you have sat upon it. I have never disobeyed an order. Not once." He paused. "If you order me to stay at the gates - I shall stay. But I want you to know: it will be the first order that I carry out hating myself."

I went to the parapet and stood beside him. We stood shoulder to shoulder and looked south.

"Senusret," I said. "We have ten days. In ten days much can be done."

"For instance?"

"For instance, find out whose hand wears the bracelet. For instance, find out what is being sought at the Rock of the First Ones. For instance, read the Most Ancient Epic - from beginning to end, and not only those lines that are read at coronations." I paused. "For instance, understand why the god today pointed to the granaries - if it was not Nakht."

Senusret was silent a long time.

"And if in ten days we learn nothing?"

"Then I shall go to Luxor," I said. "And enter the inner court. Because if I do not enter - Nefer-Ka will tell the people that Pharaoh was afraid of the god. And a people that today saw the god point to the granaries - that people will believe anything."

Over the eastern hills the rim of the sun appeared - red, wet, enormous. And somewhere below, in the temple garden, the baboons of Thoth cried out, greeting the sunrise. All at once. As they greet it, they say, since the beginning of time.

"My Pharaoh," said Senusret quietly. "May I ask?"

"Ask."

"Are you afraid?"

I looked south for a long time.

"Yes," I said. "But not of the columns. Not of Nefer-Ka. Not of the dark."

"Of what, then?"

"Of what will be revealed to me there," I said. "Of what I perhaps already know. Only do not remember."

Senusret answered nothing. He simply laid his hand on my shoulder - a heavy, calloused, warm hand of an old warrior. As one lays a hand on a son. Or a friend. Or a man one is seeing off on a road, knowing that the road is long.

And we stood so until the sun had risen entirely - over the capital, over the temple, over the sealed granaries, over the golden barque of the god, waiting at the quay for its new moon.
