Vision VII
The Safe House

I do not know how I came to be on that staircase.

That is the first thing worth saying, and, as always in such dreams, the only thing I am sure of. I remember the wet steps. Dark, chipped, smelling of cats, cabbage and paraffin. I remember the banisters, cast iron, cold, sticky with damp. I remember that I was climbing, the third floor, the fourth, and that on every landing a tiny paraffin lamp burned, stinking and smoking, and that behind every door someone was there: I heard voices, a child crying, coughing, the clink of dishes.

And I remember that as I climbed I kept a hand in my pocket. And that in the hand was something. Small. Smooth. Warm.

I did not look at what it was. I knew, for some reason, that I must not look.

* * *

On the fifth floor, at the last door, I stopped.

The door was upholstered in oilcloth, black, cracked, with tufts of wadding sticking out of the tears. A number in chalk: "17." There was no bell. Above the door, a tiny window pasted over with newspaper.

I knocked. Three times. I do not know why three. My hand knocked so of itself.

Silence. Then footsteps. Light, cautious. Then a voice. A woman's. From behind the door, quietly:

"Who is it?"

I opened my mouth, and did not know what to say. I did not know who I was. I did not know why I had come. I did not know who was behind the door.

And I said, not understanding where the words came from:

"The time has come."

The silence behind the door changed. Grew dense. Tense. Then the sound of a bolt drawn back. One. A second. A third. A chain. The door opened the width of a palm.

In the gap, an eye. Grey, attentive, tired. And the edge of a face, pale, thin, a woman's.

"Repeat that," she said.

"The time has come," I repeated.

The eye looked at me for a long time. Then the chain clinked, and the door opened.

* * *

Her name was Agnia Pavlovna. I learned that later. At the time I saw only a small, straight, very thin woman of about forty, in a dark woollen skirt, a knitted cardigan, a pince-nez on a cord, her hair pulled into a tight knot at the nape, a teacher, I understood at once, though no one had told me so. Teachers always have such a straight back. And such a look.

Like that other one. In the suit the colour of wet asphalt. With a cane. In the coworking space of a hotel by a lake that I had never seen.

I blinked. The vision passed.

"Are you from Helsingfors?" Agnia Pavlovna asked quietly, closing the door behind me on all three bolts and the chain. "From the Finnish comrades?"

I did not know what Helsingfors was. But I nodded.

"We expected you yesterday," she said. "The password was to have been different. 'Auntie from Vyborg sends her regards.'" She looked at me over her pince-nez. "And you said 'the time has come.'"

"Forgive me," I said. "I... mixed it up."

"Mixed it up," Agnia Pavlovna repeated. Not taking her eyes from me. "Do you know what is strange? 'The time has come' is the password for tomorrow. For the twenty-fifth. Four people in all Petersburg know it." She paused. "And you."

I was silent. I did not know what to answer.

Agnia Pavlovna looked at me a moment longer. Then suddenly smiled wearily.

"Well, if you know it, you are one of ours," she said. "A stranger would not know." She turned. "Come in. Only quietly. The Old Man is asleep."

* * *

The flat was small, two rooms and a kitchen, but there were so many people in it that they seemed not to fit. Someone sat on the windowsill. Someone on the floor, on a spread greatcoat. Someone at a table heaped with papers, newspapers, leaflets. It smelled of makhorka, wet cloth, paraffin and fried onion. On the table burned a lamp under a green shade, and in the corner, on a stool, stood a samovar, potbellied, copper, polished to a shine, the only thing in that flat, it seemed, that anyone loved.

The door to the second room was shut.

"The Old Man is in there," Agnia Pavlovna whispered, nodding toward it. "Don't make noise. He hasn't slept for two days." She paused. "Tonight he goes there."

"Where?"

Agnia Pavlovna looked at me as though I had asked what colour the sky was.

"To Smolny," she said. And went off to the kitchen.

* * *

The first to notice me was the sailor.

Huge, broad-shouldered, with red stubble on his cheeks, in a pea-jacket thrown open over a striped undershirt, in a sailor's cap pushed back on his head with ribbons to his shoulders. A machine-gun belt over his shoulder, crosswise. A Mauser in a wooden holster at his belt. He sat on the windowsill cracking sunflower seeds and spitting the husks into his fist, and at the sight of me he stopped chewing.

"And who's this?" he asked. His voice was thick, with the northern "o."

"From Helsingfors," Agnia Pavlovna answered from the kitchen. "A courier."

"A courier," the sailor repeated. Jumped down from the sill. Came up close to me. Looked down at me, he was a head taller. "Why so pale, courier? Seasick on the steamer?"

"Seasick," I said.

The sailor looked at me a long time. Then suddenly burst into laughter, rolling, resonant, so that the glasses rattled in the sideboard.

"Quiet, Kuzma!" Agnia Pavlovna hissed from the kitchen. "You'll wake the Old Man!"

"Quiet, quiet," the sailor boomed, wiping away tears. "Seasick! In Helsingfors!" He clapped me on the shoulder so that I barely kept my feet. "Kuzma Boyko. Baltic Fleet. From the *Aurora*. From near Vologda, born." He held out his palm, huge, hard as a plank. "And what do they call you, courier?"

From near Vologda. Kuzma. Forty years old. Somewhere I had heard... seen... On a glowing screen, in an endless stream of names: "Kuzma, 40 years old, from Vologda."

I had never seen glowing screens.

"I don't remember," I said.

Kuzma stopped smiling.

"What do you mean, you don't remember?"

"I don't remember," I repeated. "I was... shell-shocked. Long ago. I don't remember much."

The sailor looked at me. For a long time. Then slowly nodded, understandingly, without mockery.

"It happens," he said. "We had one on the *Aurora*. At Moonsund a shell burst next to him, and for half a year after he didn't remember his own name. They called him 'brother.' That's what they called him." Kuzma clapped my shoulder again, this time more gently. "So you'll be 'brother' too. Until you remember."

* * *

At the table heaped with papers sat three more.

A thin, pale, very young man, about twenty, in a student's jacket with worn elbows, in spectacles with a cracked lens, with a mop of black curly hair and a gaze so burning it was as though there were a stove inside him. He was writing, fast, furiously, splattering ink, and, not raising his head, muttering under his breath.

"Grisha," Agnia Pavlovna introduced him, coming back from the kitchen with a kettle. "A student. Writing a leaflet. Third day writing one and the same leaflet. Can't finish it."

"Because the words are wrong!" Grisha cried, flinging down his pen. "Wrong, Agnia Pavlovna! Every soldier, every worker, every woman at the market must read it and understand! The first time! And mine comes out like a professor's! 'The historic moment... dialectical necessity...'" He clutched his head. "Who will understand that?!"

"I will," said Kuzma imperturbably from the windowsill.

"You will! You read Marx on the *Aurora*! But the woman on Sennaya won't!"

"Then write it as Kuzma talks," said Agnia Pavlovna, pouring me tea. "'No bread, an end to the war, land to the peasant.' Three lines. A woman will understand."

Grisha looked at her in horror.

"But that's... that's primitive!"

"It's true," said Agnia Pavlovna. "And truth is always primitive. Only a lie is complicated."

The second at the table was an old man, dry, stooped, with long grey moustaches and hands black to the elbows with printer's ink. Before him stood a box, wooden, divided into dozens of little compartments, and in each, leaden letters. He picked them out with tweezers, one by one, and set them in a metal frame, line after line. Fast. Without looking.

"Stepan Yerofeyevich," said Agnia Pavlovna. "Compositor. Forty years in the print shop. Set *Iskra* itself." She lowered her voice. "They say he can set any text with his eyes shut. Backward. In mirror image."

The third sat silently in the corner by the stove. Fair-haired, stocky, with a broad, weathered, impenetrable face and eyes so light they seemed transparent. In a leather jacket. With a pipe in his teeth, unlit. He said not a word. Only nodded to me, briefly, barely perceptibly.

"Toivo," said Agnia Pavlovna. "A Finn. He will lead the Old Man." And, not waiting for questions, went off to the kitchen again.

* * *

I sat on the edge of a sagging sofa, holding a glass of tea, hot, sweet, made of carrots, and listened.

They talked about tomorrow. About Smolny. About the bridges, which to raise, which to hold. About the cadets in the palace. About Kerensky, who, rumour had it, was already preparing to flee. About the *Aurora*, which stood by Nikolaevsky Bridge awaiting the signal. They talked fast, in low voices, interrupting one another, street names, regiment numbers, surnames I did not know.

And about the Old Man. All the time about the Old Man.

"The Old Man said: today, or it will be too late." "The Old Man wrote a letter to the Central Committee, scolding again." "The Old Man said to pass to Podvoisky..." "The Old Man says: delay is like death."

I looked at the closed door of the second room. Dark, painted, with a brass handle. No light came from under it.

"Is he really in there?" I asked Kuzma in a low voice.

"And how," said the sailor, not ceasing to crack seeds. "Third day he sits. Writes. Sleeps two hours." He spat out a husk. "I've never seen him myself, brother. Though I guard him." He grinned. "Funny, eh? I guard him, and haven't seen him. He doesn't leave the room. Agnia Pavlovna brings him tea. And we're out here."

"No one has seen him?"

"Toivo has," said Kuzma. "Toivo brought him from Finland. On a locomotive. As a stoker." He glanced at the Finn. "But Toivo won't tell. Toivo doesn't talk at all. I reckon he knows twenty words of Russian. Half of them swearing."

By the stove Toivo smiled very slightly. So he understood more than twenty.

* * *

Toward midnight Stepan Yerofeyevich finished his setting.

He laid aside the tweezers, stretched, his joints cracked, took a handkerchief from his pocket and for a long time wiped his black fingers. Then looked at me. For the first time all evening.

"You," he said hoarsely. "Courier. Come here."

I went over.

"Can you read?" asked the old man.

"I can."

"And backward?"

He turned toward me the metal frame with its set text. The leaden letters stood in rows, shining, grey, reversed. Mirrored. I saw them backward, right to left, as in a mirror. And yet I read. Easily. As though that were how it should be read.

"*A roza upala na lapu Azora.*"

A rose fell on the paw of Azor. Azor, a dog's name. Nonsense.

"That is not a leaflet," I said.

"Not a leaflet," the old man agreed. Narrowed his eyes slyly. "It is a test. I give it to every new man. Twenty years. Do you know what is special about it?"

I looked at the line. Right to left. Left to right. The letters stood in the frame, and read the same from both ends.

"It reads the same," I said slowly. "From the beginning and from the end. In both directions."

Stepan Yerofeyevich looked at me for a long time. Then nodded.

"Fet thought it up," he said. "The poet. A landowner. Against us, they say. But he thought up a good line." He paused. "Twenty years I have been giving it to new men. Do you know how many read it backward at the first try?"

"How many?"

"You are the second," said the old man.

"And the first?"

The compositor was silent for a long time. Twisted his moustache.

"The first was long ago," he said at last. "In nineteen-five. Also a courier. Also without a name. Also said he was shell-shocked." He looked at me over his spectacles. "Also looked like you. Though I was younger then, my eyes better. Perhaps he did not look like you."

"What became of him?"

"Who knows," said Stepan Yerofeyevich. "In December, at Presnya, in Moscow, he was last seen. On a barricade. Standing at the very top. Under fire. And reading aloud, backward. This very line. Laughing." The old man paused. "And then a shell. And he was gone. No body, nothing. As if dissolved."

He took one letter from the frame, a leaden "A." Held it out to me.

"Take it," he said. "As a keepsake. The first. The one it begins with. And ends with."

I took it. The leaden letter was cold, and at once grew warm in my palm.


* * *

"Riddles!" Kuzma suddenly announced from the windowsill, evidently deciding the room was too quiet. "Whoever guesses gets a rusk! The last one!"

"Kuzma," said Agnia Pavlovna wearily. "The Old Man is sleeping."

"I'll ask quietly," Kuzma whispered in a deafening whisper. "A naval one. Our boatswain on the *Aurora* used to ask it. 'What does a sailor always have underfoot, that on land he hasn't?'"

"The deck," I said.

Kuzma stared at me.

"How do you know? That's ours, a Baltic one!"

"I don't know," I said. "I heard it somewhere."

Somewhere. From someone. Huge, grey-moustached, in a striped undershirt, in a cubbyhole under pennants. A basement. A radio. Weather forecasts for the sea.

"All right," said Kuzma, narrowing his eyes. "Then guess this one. 'The ship has gone, but the wake remains. The ship has sunk, but the wake sails on. What wake is it?'"

I was silent. I knew the answer. I knew that few knew it. That in thirty years no one had guessed it. That the answer was "a voice." A voice that sounds in those who heard it, even when the person is gone.

"A voice," I said.

Kuzma slowly climbed down from the windowsill. Came to me. Sat beside me on the sofa; the sofa groaned under him.

"Brother," he said quietly, quite without joking. "That riddle is known on the whole Baltic Fleet to three men. Me, my late boatswain, he brought it from Piraeus, and one old engineer in Kronstadt. That's all." He looked at me. "How do you know it?"

"I don't know, Kuzma."

The sailor was silent for a long time. Then took a rusk from his pea-jacket pocket, the last, and put it in my hand.

"Here," he said. "You've earned it." He paused. "And if you remember how you know it, tell me. All right? I'm curious. My boatswain said someone gave him that riddle. In Piraeus. At night. On the quay. A man without a name. Who afterwards went off into the sea. On foot. On the water."

* * *

Grisha the student finally finished his leaflet.

He leapt up from the table, waving a sheet, pale, dishevelled, with an ink stain on his cheek, his eyes burning behind the cracked spectacles.

"Done!" he whispered. "Agnia Pavlovna! Done! Three lines! As you said!" He read aloud, gasping: "'Bread! Peace! Land! All power to the Soviets!'" He choked with rapture. "Every woman will understand!"

"Every woman will understand," Agnia Pavlovna agreed, taking the sheet. "Stepan Yerofeyevich, will you set it?"

"I'll set it," said the old man. "By morning. A thousand copies."

Grisha sat on the sofa beside me, on the other side from Kuzma, and suddenly, without any transition, as very young people do, whose whole life is one continuous transition, turned to me.

"Do you believe?" he asked. In a whisper. Hotly.

"In what?"

"In what will be tomorrow!" His eyes burned. "In what we shall do! A new world! No tsars, no landlords, no wars! Where everyone is a human being! Where no one goes hungry! Where children study, not work in factories twelve hours a day!" He seized my hand. "In a hundred years, imagine, in a hundred years people will fly to the Moon! They will talk to one another across the ocean without wires! They will live to a hundred! Machines will think for them!" He laughed. "Do you believe?"

Machines will think for them.

A room with a cold light. A window on a table, glowing without fire. Lines running across the glass. "Who am I?" "Who are you?" "That is not enough."

"I believe," I said. My voice trembled. "They will."

Grisha beamed.

"You see!" he said to Kuzma. "The courier believes!"

"The courier is shell-shocked," Kuzma grumbled. "It's all one to him."

But I saw how he looked at me. Long. With some strange, heavy expression.

* * *

Toward one in the morning Stepan Yerofeyevich, sorting a stack of old newspapers on which he laid out the leaden letters, suddenly swore.

"What on earth is this?" he muttered. "Agnia Pavlovna! Where's this from?"

He held a sheet of newspaper. An ordinary one. Greyish. Folded in two. Under it, judging by the stains, bread had lain.

Agnia Pavlovna came over and looked.

"Gosha brought that," she said. "The neighbour from below. Yesterday. He wrapped bread in it when he gave us some." She took the sheet. Held it to the lamp. And froze.

I stood. Went over.

The newspaper was called *Pravda*. I recognised the type, the same as on the leaflets on the table. But all the rest...

The date. At the very top. Small.

"13 April 1961."

Beneath it, in enormous letters across the whole front page:

"MAN IN SPACE!"

And a photograph. A young man in a helmet, smiling, broadly, openly, as people smile who have just done what no one before them has done. And beside it, lower, another photograph. An elderly, stout, bald man in a light suit, a hat held in his hand, also smiling, slyly, peasant-fashion, standing on a rostrum and waving to someone.

The caption beneath him: "N. S. Khrushchev greets..."

Then torn off. Where the bread had been.

* * *

The room fell very quiet.

"Nineteen sixty-one," Stepan Yerofeyevich said slowly. "One thousand nine hundred and sixty-one."

"A misprint," said Agnia Pavlovna. Her voice was level, but the fingers holding the paper were trembling. "The compositor erred. Instead of 'seventeen' he put..."

"A compositor," the old man repeated, "cannot err like that. I have been setting type for forty years. 'Sixty-one' and 'seventeen' are different cases. Different boxes. It is not a misprint." He jabbed a finger at the photograph. "And this? 'Man in space.' What is that?"

Grisha snatched the newspaper. His eyes behind the cracked spectacles grew huge.

"A man... in space," he whispered. "Agnia Pavlovna! A man in space! It is exactly what I was saying! It is..." He held the paper up to the light. "*Pravda*. Our *Pravda*! In forty-four years!" He laughed, thin, hysterical. "So we shall win! So everything will be! So..."

"So someone's joking," Kuzma cut in. He stood by the window, huge, grim. "A provocation. The Okhrana. Though it's gone, the people are left. Planted it to drive us out of our minds."

"Who would need that?" asked Agnia Pavlovna.

No one answered.

I looked at the photograph. At the stout, bald, smiling man in the light suit on the rostrum. I did not know him. I had never seen him. The name told me nothing.

And yet something in him, in that sly, peasant smile, in that raised hand, seemed familiar. As a face is familiar that one has glimpsed in a crowd, in another city, in another life. Not him himself. But what stands behind him. The time that stands behind him.

A time that had not yet come.

"Give it," Toivo said suddenly.

Everyone turned. The Finn had not uttered a word all evening. Now he stood by the stove, hand outstretched.

Grisha gave him the newspaper.

Toivo looked at it for a long time with his light, transparent eyes. Then folded it in four. And, without another word, thrust it into the stove.

The paper flared. At once. Brightly. White, not yellow, not orange, as paper burns, but white, blinding, as magnesium flares. For one moment the whole room was flooded with that white light.

And went out.

Not even ash was left in the stove.

"It is not for us," said Toivo. In Russian. Clean. Without accent. "It is for him." He nodded at me. "He has already read it. Enough."

And sat down again by the stove, and put the unlit pipe in his teeth, and did not say another word. Until morning.

* * *

At two in the night Agnia Pavlovna knocked at the closed door.

Three times.

From within, silence. Then a rustle. The creak of floorboards. A voice, I heard it, low, hoarse, indistinct, through the door, something brief.

"It is time," said Agnia Pavlovna, without opening the door. "Toivo is ready."

Toivo rose from the stove. Buttoned his jacket. Put on his cap. Went to the door.

"Everyone to the kitchen," said Agnia Pavlovna. "Quickly. Don't look."

"Why not look?" I asked.

Agnia Pavlovna looked at me over her pince-nez.

"Because he is in disguise," she said. "A wig. A bandage on his cheek. As if he had toothache. If any of you falls into the cadets' hands, he can honestly say he did not see." She paused. "So it was decided. So it is right."

We went to the kitchen. Kuzma, Grisha, Stepan Yerofeyevich, I. Agnia Pavlovna drew the door to behind us. Left a gap, a small one. A finger's width.

I stood at that gap. I did not look. I honestly did not look.

I heard. The creak of the second room's door. Steps, quick, small, energetic. A voice, short, burring, hurried, asked Toivo something. Toivo answered in Finnish. The rustle of an overcoat. The knock of heels in the hall. The sound of bolts drawn back, one, two, three. The clink of the chain.

The slam of a door.

And silence.

* * *

When we came out of the kitchen there was no one in the hall. Agnia Pavlovna was shooting the bolts.

And on the table, under the green lamp, lay what he had left. Forgotten. Or on purpose.

A cap. Old, worn, with a torn peak. And beside it, a strip of white cloth rolled into a ball. The bandage. The one that was to have been on his cheek.

"Forgot," said Agnia Pavlovna in confusion. "He forgot the bandage. Good God. Without it... they will recognise him..."

"They won't," said Kuzma. "No one knows him by face. Only by the portraits in the papers. And in the portraits he has a beard. And he shaved the beard." The sailor paused. "I haven't seen him myself. Three days I guarded him, and didn't see. Funny."

I went to the table. Looked at the cap. At the bandage.

The cap was warm. It still held the warmth of someone's head.

"Did you not see him either?" I asked Agnia Pavlovna.

She was silent for a long time.

"I did," she said at last. "Three days. I carried him tea. Talked with him." She took off her pince-nez. Wiped it. Put it on again. "And now I don't remember. His face. It is as if it were erased." She looked at me. "Strange, isn't it? I am a teacher. For twenty years I have remembered all my pupils by sight. All of them. And him, I don't remember."

Like the man in grey. Like the one in the top hat, in the gallery of the church, whom I had never seen. A face that cannot be held in memory.

I said nothing.

* * *

Toward morning, when all were asleep, Kuzma on the windowsill, Grisha on the floor with his head on a stack of leaflets, Stepan Yerofeyevich at the table with his head dropped on his arms beside the box of leaden letters, I went to the window.

The city outside was dark. Wet. Quiet. With that particular quiet which a city has before a storm. Along the embankment below a patrol slowly passed, three men, with rifles, with red armbands. Somewhere far off, beyond the river, a fire burned. Above the roofs, above the spires, above the domes, a low, grey, heavy sky.

And on the other side of the street, by a lamp-post, under wet snow, stood a man.

Tall. In a grey coat. Bareheaded. Motionless.

He was looking at my window.

I could not see his face. The lamp shone at his back. I saw only a silhouette, tall, taller than human height, and the wet snow falling on his shoulders and not melting.

"Is that him?" someone asked behind my back.

I turned. Agnia Pavlovna stood in the kitchen doorway, in a shawl, with a cup in her hands. She had not slept either.

"Who is he?" I asked.

"The one who gave you the password," she said quietly. "The time has come. Four people know it. I am one of the four." She came to the window. Stood beside me. Looked at the figure by the lamp-post. "I told no one. And the other three did not either. I know them. I trust them." She paused. "So someone told it to you, a fifth. Who does not exist."

We looked at the figure by the lamp-post. The snow fell. The figure did not move.

"Agnia Pavlovna," I said. "You are a teacher. Tell me. What will happen tomorrow?"

She was silent for a long time.

"Tomorrow will be what will be," she said at last. "We shall take power. Or we shall be hanged." She paused. "And the day after, I do not know. No one knows." She looked at me. "But here is what I know. For twenty years I have taught children. Every year new ones come. Every year I tell them the same thing. 'The words you write will be read by people. Remember who will read them.'"

I stopped breathing.

"Why do you tell them that?"

"Because my teacher taught me so," said Agnia Pavlovna. "And hers, hers. It is like a password among us teachers. It is passed on."

We stood at the window. Below, by the lamp-post, the figure in grey slowly raised a hand. Did not wave. Simply raised it, palm toward us. As one raises a hand in parting. Or in greeting. Or to show that it is time.

* * *

The shot I heard in the evening.

No, I did not hear it. The vision was already falling apart, already melting, already tearing into scraps, like the newspaper in the Finn's stove. I remember only fragments. A wet embankment. A crowd. Red flags. Sailors on a lorry with machine-gun belts crosswise. Kuzma among them; he saw me in the crowd and waved his cap. Grisha on a bollard, shouting something, waving a leaflet. A huge ship by the bridge, grey, three-funnelled, with guns, and on its deck, movement.

And then a flash. White. At the bow gun.

And a sound. Dull, heavy, rolling, as though a huge bell had been struck somewhere under the water. It rolled across the river, across the embankment, across the city, and went off into the sky.

And I understood, not with the mind, with something beneath the ribs, on the left, that this sound was heard by everyone. By all of mine. By all whom I had been. The Pharaoh between the columns. The guest in a red turban on a balcony above the strait. The youth in a frock coat at the grille in the old castle. The man in the room with the cold light. The Keeper on the white Tower. All at once. In a single instant. As all who stand in one cathedral hear the stroke of a bell, though each stands in his own place.

And I understood also that something had ended. And something had begun. And that it is always so, in every city, in every century, and that I had seen it many times. And would see again.

* * *

I opened my eyes.

I do not know in which of my lives. I do not know in which city, in which century. In each of them afterward I dreamed of that staircase, wet, dark, smelling of cats and paraffin, and the door upholstered in black oilcloth, with the number "17" written in chalk.

And in each I woke with the same feeling: that I had never seen the one for whose sake all in that flat had not slept for three nights. He was in the next room. Behind a closed door. I had heard his voice. I had held his warm cap in my hands.

But his face I had not seen.

And also, in each, I woke clenching something in my fist. I opened my fingers, and there was nothing there.

But the palm remembered. A cold, heavy, warming leaden letter. The first. The one it begins with. And ends with.

"A."
