Chapter XXIX
The Road to Hagia Sophia

"Come not from the end from which you came," Akmal repeated, bending a finger. "Bring what you do not have." A second finger. "Ask what you know the answer to." A third. "And do not give your name." A fourth. He looked at his hand with its folded fingers, then at me, and announced in his mixed tongue, half Turkish and half mine, which he had patched together in these days from words overheard from Ferhat: "Four. One easy. Three - oy."

"One easy," I agreed. "I have no name. I could not give it if I wished."

We sat in the courtyard, on a motley carpet under a grapevine - all who considered themselves party to what Ferhat called "the expedition to the dervish" and Hasan Amca "the matchmaking with wisdom." The morning was hot and lazy; from the kitchen came the smell of baking flatbreads; Akmal's doves cooed on the roof. Suleyman sat at the head of the carpet with his beads and said nothing, squinting at the sun. Yusuf was counting something of his own on the abacus. Kemal was drawing with charcoal on a flagstone. Orhan was sharpening his knife. Ferhat translated. And Hasan, who had arrived at breakfast, as always, uninvited, ate flatbread with honey and gave advice.

"Hasan Amca says," Ferhat translated, "that the riddles of dervishes are like trading with Persians. There is never one answer. Always three. One for a fool, one for a clever man and one - the true one." Ferhat paused. "He says, if you solve only the clever answer, the dervish will receive you but will not talk. He will give you tea and keep silent."

"And if I solve the true one?"

Hasan heard the question, licked his fingers and said something which made Suleyman smile into his beard.

"He says," translated Ferhat, "that in sixty years he has never met a man who solved the true answer to a dervish's riddle. Including himself. He went to Ahmet Baba once - some twenty years ago, when a consignment of saffron of his went missing. The dervish gave him tea, was silent, and at parting said: 'Your saffron is in your brother-in-law's cellar.'" Ferhat laughed. "And there it was."

"So he helped after all."

"Hasan Amca says that was a stupid answer," said Ferhat. "About the saffron. The true one the dervish never told him." Ferhat listened on. "And to the question 'what is the true one?' the dervish answered: 'That you seek not the saffron but a reason to quarrel with your brother-in-law.'" Ferhat snorted. "And since then Hasan Amca has not quarrelled with his brother-in-law. Twenty years."

Hasan nodded with dignity and reached for the next flatbread.

* * *

"Come not from the end from which you came," said Yusuf, not raising his head from the abacus. He said it in Turkish, drily, but I understood. Then he added something more.

"Uncle says," translated Ferhat, "that it is all simple here. From which end did you come into the city? On the first day?"

I thought.

"By a street. On foot. I was walking along a street - and suddenly understood that I was walking. I do not remember from where. I remember only the street. The paving. The houses."

"From land," said Ferhat. "You came from land. So to the dervish you must go - from water."

"From the sea?"

Yusuf clicked a bead, as if totting up. Suleyman nodded.

"Mustafa," said Ferhat. "Mustafa will take us. We round the cape - where the old palace of the Sultans is, where the walls go down straight into the water - and land at the old sea gates. From there to Hagia Sophia - half an hour on foot uphill." He smiled. "That is how the Greeks entered the city. From the sea. Through the sea gates. For a thousand years. So it is the right end."

"One solved," said Akmal, straightening a finger. "Two - oy."

* * *

"Bring what you do not have," said Orhan.

All turned to him. Orhan had not uttered a word all morning - sharpening his knife and scowling. Now he put down the knife, looked at me from under his brows and said something more - gruffly, more briefly than Yusuf.

"He says," translated Ferhat with evident surprise, "that you have no name. So bring a name."

"Where am I to get one?"

Orhan shrugged - as if to say, that is your concern - and took up his knife again.

"Perhaps take the name Grandfather gave?" suggested Ferhat. "Misafir. Guest. It is, after all, now your name."

"But the fourth rule is - do not give your name," I said. "If I bring it - I shall have to give it."

Silence hung over the carpet. Hasan stopped chewing. Yusuf stopped counting.

"Oy," said Akmal.

And then Kemal, who sat at the edge of the carpet, put down his charcoal, came to me and without a word thrust his hand into the pocket of my cloak. I had no time to stop him. He rummaged there - among the note in Persian, the drawing of two doors, the warm coins, the fragment of the nazar - and drew out a sheet folded in four.

A blank sheet. The very one. Which had lain in my pocket since the first morning, with the stones, the coins and the note. Without a single letter. Without a single mark. I had grown so used to it that I had almost forgotten it was there.

Kemal unfolded the sheet. Raised it over his head, showing everyone - clean, white, empty. And said one word:

"Isim."

Name.

"He says," translated Ferhat slowly, "that this is your name. A blank sheet. You do not have it - but you have it. You will bring it - and not give it. Because nothing is written on it."

Suleyman slowly set down his beads and looked a long time at his younger grandson.

Then said something - quietly, but so that Ferhat flinched.

"Grandfather says," translated Ferhat, "that Kemal sometimes solves riddles before they are set. He says: 'The boy was born on the night when ships burned on the Bosphorus, and the midwife said that he would see what is written on the reverse.'" Ferhat paused. "I never heard that. About the ships."

Kemal, paying no heed, neatly folded the sheet in four again and put it into my hand. Then returned to his place and took up his charcoal once more.

"Two," said Akmal, straightening the second finger. His voice was quiet, almost reverent. "One - oy."

* * *

The last rule - "Ask what you know the answer to" - we did not solve at breakfast. Ferhat went through the options: ask what your name is - but the answer would be "I don't know." Ask where you are from - the same answer. Ask what two times two is - Hasan said that for such a question the dervish would drive you out with a stick, and was probably right.

"You will ask on the road," Suleyman said at last. In Turkish, briefly, but I understood him. And added something which Ferhat translated thus: "The road itself will tell you. The road is the best teacher of riddles."

Suleyman did not come with us. His knees, he said - his knees no longer loved the hills of Sultanahmet. But at the gate he detained me, laid a hand on my shoulder and looked long into my face - silently, with the same serious, appraising look with which he had looked at my stones on the first day.

Then he took from his wrist the red thread with half of the split nazar - the one he had worn since the night the guest came to me - and tied it on my wrist. Tight. With two knots.

"Grandfather says," Ferhat translated quietly, "that you must not take it off. Until you return." Ferhat paused. "If you return."

"He said that? 'If'?"

Ferhat looked away.

"He said so."

* * *

And then Hodja Nureddin appeared at the gate.

Small, dry, in his enormous white turban, with his beads, out of breath - he had evidently been running, for the turban had slipped to one side and his pointed little beard was ruffled. He saw us - Ferhat, Kemal, Akmal, Orhan, Hasan and me, gathered at the gate - and threw up his hands.

"He says," translated Ferhat, hiding a smile, "that he heard we are going to Ahmet Baba. From Hasan Amca. Who has told the whole quarter." Ferhat listened on. The Hodja spoke fast, hotly, waving his beads. "He says that Ahmet Baba is not a real dervish. That he studied among the Franks. That he reads books of unbelievers. That he argues with the Quran. That in his lodge lives a cat who knows how to open doors. And that..."

"A cat?" I repeated.

"So he says," Ferhat shrugged. "The cat opens doors. By herself. With her paw."

Hodja Nureddin finished his speech, caught his breath, straightened his turban - and suddenly turned to me and spoke in my language. Broken, laboriously assembled, but understandable.

"Ahmet Baba - clever," he said. "Too clever. Clever - dangerous. Clever tells truth. Truth - not always medicine. Sometimes - poison." He paused, looking at me with sharp, mocking eyes in which now there was no mockery. "You remember Nasreddin? Key under the lamp?"

"I remember."

"Ahmet Baba will tell you where the key is," said the Hodja. "But will not tell what is behind the door." He jabbed a dry finger at me. "You ask yourself: do you want to know what is behind the door? Or do you want to stand on the threshold? On the threshold - warm. No draught."

He turned to go. Then stopped. Rummaged in the folds of his robe and took out a small pouch - canvas, tied with a red thread. Thrust it into my hand.

"Salt," he said. "And bread. A crumb. If Ahmet Baba offers you to stay - do not stay. Eat the bread, throw the salt over your shoulder - and go." He paused. "I do not like him. But he is my friend. Forty years. We have argued forty years." And already leaving, over his shoulder, in Turkish, he added something.

"What did he say?" I asked Ferhat.

"He said," translated Ferhat, "'Tell him that I am right. He will understand.'"

* * *

Mustafa waited for us at the quay - huge, bald, with his gold tooth and curled moustaches - and, seeing the procession coming down to the water, burst out laughing so that his boat rocked.

"He says," translated Ferhat, "that for the spirit who came to him in a dream the boat is free. And for six - it is free too, because Hasan Amca said that last time he failed to deliver the mastic."

"What mastic?" I asked.

"None," said Ferhat. "Hasan Amca never did buy the mastic. But Mustafa is sure he owes. " He shrugged. "In our port, Misafir, no one remembers any more who owes whom. Everyone simply carries one another free, just in case."

We settled in the caique. The boat sank until the water came almost to the gunwales. Mustafa leaned on the oars - and we set off.

Not across the Golden Horn, as last time, to the Phanar. But along the shore, eastward, to where the bay opens into the Bosphorus, and the Bosphorus into the sea. Past the Galata Tower on the far shore - round, stone, with a pointed roof like an astrologer's cap. Past ships at anchor - tall sailing vessels, pot-bellied steamers smoking like samovars, tiny fishing boats from which fishermen waved to Mustafa and shouted something. Past quays, warehouses, shipyards.

And then we rounded the cape.

* * *

I had seen this cape from the balcony - from afar, in haze, fleetingly: a green hill above the water, cypresses, some walls. Now, close up, from the water, it was different.

The walls rose straight out of the sea. Enormous, ancient, built of grey stone and red brick - in bands, like a layer cake - with towers, square and round, with battlements notched like old teeth. Waves beat on the foot of the walls, and spray flew up to the very embrasures. And above the walls, on the hill, among cypresses and plane trees, rose roofs, domes, pavilions crowned with golden spires - the old palace of the Sultans, where, as Ferhat had told me, only shadows lived, because the Sultans had long ago moved to the other shore, to a new palace which they keep building and never finish.

"Here there used to be a Greek acropolis," Ferhat told us as Mustafa guided the boat along the walls, so close that I could have touched the stone with my hand. "And earlier still - before the Greeks - they say a little town stood here. A fishing one. It was founded by a man named Byzas. He asked the oracle where to build a city. The oracle said: 'Opposite the blind.'" Ferhat laughed. "Byzas thought long about what that meant. And then he sailed here and saw on the other shore, in Asia, a city built earlier. In a bad place - flat, open, without a harbour. And understood: those who built a city there and not here are blind. Because they did not see this cape. And built his own - opposite the blind."

"A good story," I said.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, "that ever since, this city sees what others do not. " He paused. "And that this is why everyone wants it. Greeks. Latins. We. All. Each thinks that if he takes it - he will gain sight."

Mustafa suddenly stopped rowing. Pointed his hand at the wall. There, right by the water, in the masonry, was an arch. Walled up with stone. Old, almost indistinguishable under grown weed and shells.

"The sea gates," said Ferhat. "One of the old ones. They were walled up when the Turks came." He paused. "And those over there - open. Farther on. There we shall land."

Kemal, sitting in the prow, suddenly leaned over the side and lowered his hand into the water - right by the walled-up arch. Held it there. Drew it out. And looked at me.

On his palm lay a shell. Small, white, spiral. Coiled - in a circle, from the edge to the centre.

He held it out to me. I took it. Kemal said nothing.

* * *

We landed at the open sea gates - a low, dark, damp arch in the wall, through which fishermen hauled their catch into the city. We climbed uphill - through narrow, steep, winding lanes, past old wooden houses hanging over the pavement, past fountains, past little mosques, past cats sprawled on warm stones - and the higher we climbed, the quieter it grew around us, as it always did in Sultanahmet.

Hasan fell behind at the second turn. At the third Akmal went back for him, took him by the hand and hauled him uphill as a tug hauls a barge. Hasan puffed, panted, cursed hills, dervishes and his own curiosity, but came.

And then the street ended. And before us - across the whole width of the sky - rose Hagia Sophia.

* * *

I had seen it before. From afar - from the balcony, from the hill, from the boat. I had passed by it on the day we went to the widow with the cats - and Ferhat had told me of the faces of saints showing through the lime.

But I had never stood before it so. Right at its foot. Looking up.

It was not merely large. It was enormous - as a mountain is enormous, as the sea, as the sky: not by measure, but by the fact that beside it everything else - houses, people, trees, even the minarets at the corners - became small, toylike, unreal. Its walls - dark red, faded to the colour of clotted blood, buttressed outside by massive supports as though someone feared it would fall - rose to the semi-domes, and the semi-domes to the main dome. And that dome, enormous, flat, grey as lead, lay upon it like a lid on a gigantic cauldron - and it seemed that it did not lie but floated. Hung in the air. By itself.

"They say," Ferhat said quietly, "that when it was built, the emperor entered, looked up and said: 'Solomon, I have surpassed thee.'" He paused. "Thirteen hundred years ago."

We went in.

* * *

Inside it was dark - after the dazzling sun so dark that at first I saw nothing but patches of light falling from somewhere above. Then my eyes adjusted. And I saw.

Space. Not a hall, not a temple - space. Enormous, empty, filled with light pouring from forty windows around the base of the dome - so that it seemed the dome rested on nothing, that it hung on those forty rays like a golden net. Beneath the dome - a floor covered with carpets on which people prayed - tiny as ants under this unthinkable height. Columns - green, red, mottled, of marble I had never seen - rose to the arches. On the walls hung enormous round shields with golden inscriptions in script - not that script, not mine, but Arabic - the names of Allah, of the Prophet, of the caliphs.

And between the shields, beneath a layer of lime, beneath grey whitewash - gold showed through.

I did not see it at once. Only when my eyes had fully adjusted. Through the lime, in one of the semi-domes, at a place where the whitewash had flaked from damp, a piece of golden ground was visible. And on it - an eye. Enormous, dark, stern. Looking down. At those who prayed. At us. At me.

"There," whispered Ferhat. "Do you see? Faces. They show through."

I looked at this eye showing through the whitewash - and it seemed to me that it looked at me as the eye at the top of the Egyptian obelisk in the hippodrome had looked: calmly, patiently, without surprise. As one looks at one who is many centuries late but has come all the same.

* * *

We were found by the old kayyim - the custodian of the mosque. A small, bent old man in a dark robe, with a long grey beard and a broom in his hand, on which he seemed not so much to sweep as to lean, as on a staff. He came up to us quietly, shuffling on the carpets, and said something to Ferhat.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, "whether we are the ones going to Ahmet Baba." Ferhat raised his brows in surprise. "He says Ahmet Baba sent a boy to him this morning. Said: 'If six come from the sea, and with them a stranger without a name - show them the column.'"

"What column?"

The old kayyim beckoned us with his hand and led us - across the whole enormous hall, between the praying, to the north-west corner, where a column stood by the wall. Ordinary at first glance, faced in bronze plates up to the height of a man. And in the bronze, at chest height - a hole. Small, round, the size of a big thumb. Its edges were polished to a shine by countless touches.

"The weeping column," said Ferhat, having heard the old man. "The column of Saint Gregory. The Greeks said a saint lives in it. The Turks say that here the Prophet Khidr once touched the stone." Ferhat listened on. "He says: the stone inside is always damp. Even in the fiercest heat. The column weeps. No one knows why. If you put your thumb in the hole and turn your hand in a circle - a full circle, without taking the thumb out - and if the thumb comes out damp - the wish will be granted."

"In a circle," I said.

"In a circle," nodded Ferhat. "He says Ahmet Baba ordered that you do this. Before you go to him."

I went to the column. Put my thumb into the opening. Inside it was cold. And - yes - damp. The stone was wet, as though it truly wept.

I turned my hand. Slowly. In a circle. My palm slid over the polished bronze, over the traces of the thousands and thousands of hands that had gone this circle before me.

When the circle closed, I drew out my thumb.

It was damp.

The old kayyim looked at my thumb, then at me. And said something - quietly, briefly.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, "what you wished."

I thought. I had wished nothing. I had simply turned my hand in a circle.

"Nothing," I said.

The old man heard it. And suddenly smiled - toothlessly, brightly, as if I had said something very right.

"He says," translated Ferhat, "that this is the first time in the fifty years he has served here that anyone has answered truly. Everyone wishes. Wealth, love, health, a son. And you - nothing." Ferhat listened on, and his face grew more and more serious. "He says: only those answer so who already have everything. Or who do not yet know what they want." Ferhat paused. "He says: Ahmet Baba told him - if the stranger wishes nothing, it means it is he."

* * *

Then the kayyim led us upstairs.

A narrow, dark, sloping ramp - without steps, paved with stone slabs - climbed in zigzags inside the wall, from turn to turn, higher and higher. It was cool and dark here, it smelled of dust and stone, and our steps echoed hollowly. Hasan cursed the ramp so inventively that Orhan, walking behind, for the first time that day laughed aloud.

"He says," translated Ferhat, choking with laughter, "that in old times empresses rode up this ramp to the galleries on mules. And that if empresses could, so can he. But only if there is a mule."

There was no mule. Hasan made it himself.

The gallery stretched along the whole hall - broad, long, with columns and arches, with a marble floor worn by centuries. From here, above, the enormous space under the dome could be seen entire - and the people on the carpets below seemed quite tiny, like scattered grains.

The kayyim stopped by a marble balustrade - a low stone barrier separating the gallery from the abyss. Pointed at it with his broom.

I bent. On the smooth marble, polished by the elbows of thousands of people who had leaned on it for a thousand years, signs were cut. Shallow, worn, almost invisible. Angular. Sharp. Like sticks and twigs.

"Runes," said Ferhat, having heard the kayyim. "Varangian. Northern. He says that a thousand years ago northerners served here in the emperor's guard. Giants with axes, with red beards, from beyond the cold sea. One of them, they say, was bored on duty - and carved his name here. With a dagger." Ferhat paused. "No one knows what is written. The kayyim says - some say: 'Halfdan was here.' Others - 'Halfdan carved these runes.'"

"Halfdan was here," I repeated.

A man who was bored on duty a thousand years ago. Who wanted someone to remember that he had been here.

And then the kayyim pointed his broom a little farther. A half-step to the right of the runes.

There were signs there too. Quite tiny, quite worn, almost invisible - I should not have seen them had the light from the window not fallen on the marble so obliquely. But they were there.

Long. Flowing. Curving. Running one into another without breaks.

The script.

One line. Short. Cut beside the Varangian runes - as though someone else, at another time, had stood in this very place, leaned on this very marble, been bored or been longing - and also wished that someone would remember he had been here.

Kemal knelt by the balustrade. Ran a finger over the line. Slowly. From beginning to end.

Then raised his eyes to me.

"Sen," he said quietly.

I knew this word. Ferhat had taught it to me in the first days. It meant "you."

I did not ask what he meant. I feared the answer.

* * *

From the gallery, from behind a column, in the depth, a sound came. Footsteps. Light, unhurried. Someone was walking toward us from the far end of the gallery, where it was quite dark.

Orhan instantly straightened, his hand on his knife. Ferhat stepped toward me. Akmal hid behind Hasan, which was not difficult.

From the shadow between the columns a man came out.

Tall. In a grey frock coat of European cut, buttoned to the last button. With a cane in his hand. With a top hat which he held in the other hand, pressed to his chest, as one holds a hat on entering a strange house.

His face I did not remember.

I looked straight at him - from five paces, in the light falling from the gallery window - and saw that he had a face. Eyes, nose, mouth. But as soon as I tried to hold in memory even one feature - it slipped away. Like water through fingers. Like a face in a mirror when the glass mists over.

"Bonjour," he said. In French. Softly, politely, with impeccable pronunciation. "You are going to the dervish, monsieur."

Not a question.

"I am," I answered in French. The language came by itself, as then, at Kyra Theodora's.

"Do not go," said the man in grey. As softly. As politely. "He will tell you what you need not know. Yet." He paused. "You are not ready. You are like a letter opened before its time. If you read it - you will not understand. And if you understand - you will not be able to forget."

"Who are you?"

The man in grey inclined his head slightly - as one inclines it, wondering whether to answer.

"One who seeks the same as you, monsieur," he said. "Only from the other end."

He turned to Kemal, who was still kneeling by the balustrade. Looked at him. Long.

Kemal did not look away.

"A remarkable boy," said the man in grey. "Sees the reverse." He put on his top hat. "Take care of him, monsieur. Such ones see us too."

And left - unhurriedly, tapping his cane on the marble floor - into the depth of the gallery, whence he had come. His steps grew quieter, quieter - and died away entirely.

Orhan, coming to himself, rushed after him with a knife in his hand. Came back in a minute - pale, out of breath.

"Yok," he said.

No one.

"It is a dead end there," whispered Ferhat, translating after the kayyim, who stood pressed against a column, crossing himself - in the Greek way, right to left, I noticed, though he was a Muslim. "The gallery ends in a wall. There is nowhere to go."

* * *

The kayyim saw us downstairs, to the exit - silently, shuffling, leaning on his broom. At the very doors he stopped. Looked at me. And began to speak - slowly, quietly, as if telling a tale to a child at bedtime. Ferhat translated.

"When they built this church," said the old man, "long ago, under the Greek emperor - the workmen once went off to dinner. They left on the site a boy. The master's son. To guard the tools." He paused. "And while the boy sat alone, a man came up to him. Bright. Tall. In white. Asked: where is everyone? The boy said: gone to dinner. The man said: run, call them. Tell them to come back quickly. The boy said: I cannot, I was told to guard. The man said: run, I shall guard in your place until you return." The kayyim paused. "The boy ran. Told his father, the father told the emperor. The emperor understood that it was an angel. Understood that the angel had promised to guard until the boy returned. And the emperor said: the boy shall never come back here again. Sent him far, far away, over the sea, to the edge of the earth. For ever."

"Why?" I asked.

"So that the angel would guard for ever," said the kayyim. "He had promised - until his return. And the boy will not return. So the angel is here for ever." The old man looked up, under the dome, to where through the lime a golden eye showed. "Thirteen hundred years. The angel waits for the boy. And the boy does not return."

He paused.

"And sometimes," he added very quietly, and Ferhat translated in a whisper, "sometimes I think: and what will happen if the boy returns? The angel will then be free. Will go." The kayyim looked at me. "Who will guard then?"

He did not wait for an answer. Turned and went back into the enormous hall, shuffling on the carpets, leaning on his broom - a small old man beneath the endless dome.

* * *

The lodge of Ahmet Baba proved to be quite near - behind Hagia Sophia, in a lane descending to an old cemetery where among cypresses stood leaning stone gravestones crowned with turbans hewn from stone. A low wall. A wooden gate once painted green, now peeled to grey. Above the gate - no signboard, only a small copper plaque with one word in script. Arabic.

"'Patience,'" Ferhat translated.

The sun was already setting. The sky above the dome of Hagia Sophia turned pink, then lilac. From the minarets the evening call floated - one voice, a second, a third, until the whole city was singing, calling to one another.

I knocked at the gate.

For a long time no one opened. Then the gate opened - exactly a hand's breadth. In the gap appeared the face of a boy - about ten, shaven-headed, in the felt cap of a dervish, with huge curious eyes. And at his feet, peeping out from behind his knees - a cat. Tricolour, fluffy, with green eyes. She looked at me. Then raised a paw and pushed the gate - and it opened another hand's breadth.

Hodja Nureddin had not lied.

The boy said something. Ferhat translated:

"He asks: from which end did you come?"

"From the sea," I said.

The boy nodded. Asked again.

"What did you bring?"

I took the blank sheet from my pocket. Unfolded it. Showed it. The boy looked a long time at the clean paper. Then nodded again - gravely, like an adult.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, "what your name is."

I was silent.

The boy waited. The cat at his feet waited. The sun was setting.

I was silent.

The boy smiled - for the first time - and asked the last. Ferhat translated it quite softly:

"He asks: what do you wish to ask the sheikh? Remember - only what you know the answer to."

I stood at the gate, on the threshold. The sun was setting behind the dome of Hagia Sophia. On my wrist was a red thread with half of a split nazar. In one pocket - a blank sheet, a note, coins. In the other - the Hodja's pouch with salt and bread. In my hand - a white spiral shell which Kemal had drawn from the water by the walled-up sea gates.

And I suddenly understood what to ask. Understood as I understood all that mattered in this city: not with the head but with something under the ribs, on the left.

The riddle of the jeweller Karapet. "He who holds both stands in the doorway. Where he looks - there is home."

"I wish to ask," I said slowly, "where my home is."

Ferhat translated. The boy listened. Looked at me long, with his head tilted, like a bird.

"And do you know the answer?" Ferhat translated his question.

"I do," I said. "Where I am looking."

The boy was silent a long time. Then bent, took the cat in his arms, pressed her to his chest. And said something - one phrase.

"He says," Ferhat's voice trembled, "'The sheikh will receive you. Tomorrow. At dawn. Come alone.'" Ferhat paused. "And one more thing. He says: 'The sheikh said: if the stranger answers "where I am looking" - tell him: then look carefully. Because now you are looking back.'"

The gate closed.

* * *

We descended to the sea in silence, in the dusk, by the same narrow lanes. Hasan no longer cursed the hills. Akmal did not run ahead. Orhan walked behind, not letting go of his knife. Kemal - beside me, so close that his shoulder touched my elbow.

At the walled-up sea gates, where Mustafa waited with his boat, Ferhat stopped me.

"Misafir," he said quietly. "That man. In the gallery. In grey."

"Yes."

"He said: 'One who seeks the same as you. Only from the other end.'" Ferhat looked at the dark water, at the reflection of the first stars in it. "Do you remember the fountain at Kyra Theodora's? The inscription that is read from both ends?"

"I remember."

"If you go from one end, and he from the other," Ferhat said slowly, "then one day you will meet. In the middle." He paused. "I do not know whether that is good or bad."

I did not answer. I looked at the dark dome of Hagia Sophia above us, on its hill, in the last light of sunset. Somewhere beneath it, in the wall, a priest waited who would one day finish the liturgy. Somewhere beneath the Golden Gate a marble king slept. Somewhere on the gallery, beside the rune of a northern giant, a line was cut which a twelve-year-old boy had read with one word: "You." And somewhere beneath the dome an angel guarded tools, awaiting a boy who would never return.

And if he returned - who would guard then?

Mustafa leaned on the oars. The boat pushed off from the wall. And when we were already far away, almost at the cape, I turned - and it seemed to me that on the gallery of Hagia Sophia, in one of the upper windows, a tall figure stood. Grey. Looking after us.

Or the last light was simply falling so.
