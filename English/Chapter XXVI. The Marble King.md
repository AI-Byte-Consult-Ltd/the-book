Chapter XXVI
The Marble King

The note was brought by the old woman with the candles.

I did not recognize her at once - the first time, by the well in Sultanahmet, I had seen her sitting on the ground, in black, with a rosary with a cross at the end and with a kerchief on which thin wax candles were laid out. Now she stood at the gate of Suleyman's house - straight, severe, in the same black but in a clean, starched apron and a white collar, like a maid from a good house - and held in her hand a letter folded in four, on thick paper with a gilt edge.

Akmal, who had opened the gate, stared at her with such amazement as though he had seen a ghost. Then he turned and yelled across the whole courtyard:

"Grandfather! The Greek woman! The one who swore! She's come!"

The old woman, hearing this, pursed her lips and said something in Greek - judging by the tone, anything but a blessing.

Suleyman came out to the gate himself. They talked - in the same mixture of Turkish and Greek as the first time - and the old man, having listened, was silent a long while, running his beads. Then he took the letter from her and brought it to me.

"She says," Ferhat translated, who had come up behind, "that her name is Despina. That she serves in the house of a certain lady. In the Phanar. On the other shore of the Golden Horn." Ferhat paused. "The lady wishes to see 'the young man in the red turban who asked about the old columns.' Today. Before sunset."

I unfolded the letter. The paper was costly, thick, slightly yellowed, with a gilt edge, and in the corner - embossed, barely noticeable - a crest: a double-headed eagle holding in its claws something like a sword and an orb. Beneath the crest were several lines, written in Greek in an elegant, old-fashioned, slightly trembling hand. And below - a translation, hastily, schoolgirlishly scrawled in Turkish, evidently by Despina herself.

"What does it say?" I asked.

Ferhat looked at the letter and slowly read:

"'Kyra Theodora of the house of Palaiologos-Melissenos invites the guest to share with her coffee and an old story. Come while I still remember it. I am ninety-one years old.'" Ferhat raised his eyes to me. "Palaiologoi. Why, they were Greek emperors. The last. Before us."

Suleyman, hearing the name, suddenly laughed quietly - drily, like a crackling branch.

"Grandfather says," translated Ferhat, "that in the Phanar every second Greek calls himself a descendant of the Palaiologoi. As every second beggar among us calls himself a descendant of the Prophet." Ferhat paused, listening. "But this one, says Grandfather, may perhaps be the real thing. He knows her. Long ago. Forty years ago he sold her silk for her granddaughter's wedding. She haggled over every cubit like Hasan Amca himself, but paid in gold. Old gold. The kind that nobody mints any more."

* * *

We crossed the Golden Horn in a caique - a narrow, long, light boat, painted blue and red, with a sharply raised prow. The oarsman, to my surprise, proved to be Mustafa - the very boatman who, according to Hasan, had told the man in the grey frock coat that he had seen no stranger in velvet shoes.

Mustafa was huge, bald as a melon, with black moustaches curled upward and with arms as thick as my thigh. Seeing me, he broke into a smile, baring a gold tooth, pressed a hand to his heart and said something long and flowery.

"He says," Ferhat translated, choking with laughter, "that for a friend of Hasan Amca his boat is free. And that he has never in his life seen you. And never will. And if anyone asks - you are probably a spirit. Or an angel. Or simply came to him in a dream, because he drank a lot yesterday."

"Tell him thank you," I said. "And that he came to me in a dream too."

Mustafa roared so that the boat rocked, and took up the oars.

Everyone came with us - that is, everyone who had managed to prove to Suleyman that we could not do without him. Ferhat - to translate. Kemal - silently, he simply got into the boat, and no one dared put him off. Akmal - because he declared that if he were not taken he would swim after us himself, and no one doubted that he would. Orhan - with a knife at his belt, "because Greeks," as he explained through his teeth. And Hasan Amca, who happened to be passing the gate just at the moment we were getting into the boat, and announced that he had urgent business in the Phanar with a dealer in mastic.

"What business?" Ferhat asked suspiciously.

"Urgent," Hasan answered imperturbably, settling in the stern so that the boat sank a hand's breadth. "Very urgent. When we get back, I'll do it."

* * *

The Golden Horn that day looked like molten gold - and I understood where the bay got its name. The sun stood low over the western hills, and the water reflected it so brightly that it hurt to look. Around us darted dozens of similar caiques - with passengers, with cargo, with fruit sellers who shouted to each other across the water, offering each other melons. Barges laden with timber drifted heavily past. At the shores stood shipyards, where on the slips the skeletons of unfinished ships showed black, and from there came the knocking of axes and the shouts of foremen.

And on the hills on both shores - the city. Domes, minarets, tiled roofs, cypresses, dark and sharp as candles. And walls. I did not see them at once - ancient, enormous, half-ruined walls of thin brick and grey stone, with towers, running along the shore, along the water, here intact, there crumbled, overgrown with grass and bushes, with houses clinging to them like swallows' nests.

"The Greek walls," said Ferhat, noticing my glance. "Old. Very old. A thousand years. When Sultan Mehmed took the city, his ships could not enter the Golden Horn - the Greeks had barred the entrance with an enormous chain. Iron. From shore to shore. Then the Sultan ordered the ships hauled overland. Over the hill. On logs smeared with fat. Seventy ships in one night." Ferhat pointed to the hill on the left. "There. In the morning the Greeks woke up - and the Sultan's ships were already in the bay, behind their backs."

"And the city fell," I said.

"And the city fell," Ferhat nodded. "A month later." He paused. "Grandfather says the Greeks still cannot forgive it. Not the Turks. The chain."

Mustafa at the oars said something and roared with laughter. Hasan in the stern roared too.

"Mustafa says," translated Ferhat, "that his great-great-grandfather hauled those ships. Over the hill. All night. And ruptured his back. And ever since, all the men in their family have been oarsmen. So as never to haul ships overland again."

Kemal, sitting in the prow of the boat, had been silently looking at the ancient walls all this time. Then he suddenly turned to me and pointed - not at the walls but under the water. To where beneath the golden surface, in the dark depth, something was visible. Huge, round, black links. One after another. Going down into the depth.

"The chain," Ferhat whispered. "What is left of it. They say it was never raised. It lies on the bottom. Four hundred years."

We passed over it in silence.

* * *

The Phanar rose from the water up the hillside - in narrow, steep, cobbled lanes where, it seemed, two people could not pass without brushing shoulders. The houses here were different from Suleyman's: not wooden but stone, tall, gloomy, with narrow windows barred with grilles, with heavy oak doors bound in iron. Houses built by people who did not trust the street.

"Rich Greeks lived here," Ferhat told us as we climbed, panting, after Despina, who, in spite of her years, walked so fast that Hasan had fallen behind at the second turn. "The richest. The noblest. They served the Sultans. As translators. Dragomans. As rulers of distant lands - there, beyond the Danube, in Wallachia, in Moldavia. The Sultans appointed them hospodars. They lived there like princes. Then returned here. Built houses." He pointed to a gloomy mansion behind a grille. "Grandfather says they had a saying: 'Our grandfathers were kings. Our fathers were interpreters of kings. And we are interpreters of interpreters.'"

Orhan, striding behind with his hand on his knife, muttered something.

"He says," translated Ferhat, "that the Greeks still think the city is theirs. And that someday they will take it back."

"And do they think so?"

Ferhat shrugged.

"Ask the lady," he said. "I think we have arrived."

* * *

The house of Kyra Theodora stood at the very top of the hill, above the Golden Horn - old, stone, three-storeyed, with peeling plaster, with wooden balconies blackened by time, with a garden behind a high wall from which hung branches of fig and pomegranate. Above the gate, in a stone niche, was carved the same crest as on the paper: a double-headed eagle. One of the eagle's heads was broken off.

Despina led us through the garden - overgrown, run wild, but still beautiful: roses, lemons, huge bushes of jasmine, a marble bench almost drowned in ivy, and in the middle - a fountain. Old, marble, with a round basin into which water ran in a thin stream from a lion's mouth. Along the rim of the basin ran an inscription. In Greek letters.

By the fountain, in a deep wicker chair, wrapped in shawls, sat an old woman.

She was so old that she seemed unreal - as though carved from yellowed ivory and forgotten to be painted. Tiny, shrivelled, with a face covered by a thousand fine wrinkles, like old parchment. Grey hair, gathered in a high coiffure in the fashion of half a century ago, held with a tortoiseshell comb. On her fingers - rings too heavy for those transparent, bird-like hands: rubies, emeralds, an enormous sapphire. On her knees sat a peacock - a real one, alive, with a tail hanging to the very ground - and she stroked its blue neck as one strokes a cat.

And her eyes were young. Dark, shining, mocking. The eyes of a girl who has not yet decided whether to fall in love or to laugh.

She looked at us. At each in turn - Ferhat, Kemal, Akmal, Orhan, breathless Hasan, who at last appeared in the gateway. Then - at me. And looked for a long time.

"So," she said at last. In French. In an old-fashioned, slightly bookish French, such as was spoken half a century ago in good houses. "You have come after all. Sit down, young man. The others may drink coffee in the arbour. Despina will feed them." She looked at Orhan. "And tell your young friend with the knife that in this house the last time anyone was cut was in the year fourteen hundred and fifty-three. And it was not we who did the cutting."

* * *

I understood French. I do not know why myself - I had never thought of it, but the words came to me clearly, easily, as though I had spoken this language all my life. Ferhat, hearing me answer her, raised his brows in amazement - and withdrew to the arbour, leading the others away.

We two were left. And the peacock.

"Did Despina tell you?" asked Kyra Theodora. "How she saw you by the well?"

"No."

"She came home white as this marble. She said: 'Kyra, I saw him. The one from your story. In the red turban. By the well above the underground water. He asked what lies under the water.'" The old woman stroked the peacock. "I said to her: 'Despina, you are seventy years old and you believe in fairy tales.' And then I did not sleep for three nights." She paused. "And then a man in grey came to me."

My hands went cold.

"A man in grey?"

"A week ago. Tall. Polite. He spoke French better than I - and I, young man, have spoken French since I first spoke at all." She narrowed her eyes. "He asked about a family relic. About what has been kept in our house since that night. Since the twenty-eighth of May, fourteen hundred and fifty-three. Offered money. A great deal of money." Kyra Theodora smiled. "I told him that the Palaiologoi do not sell what does not belong to them. And what does belong to them - was sold long ago to the Genoese. He smiled and left." She paused. "Young man, for ninety-one years I have looked at people's faces. He had a face. But I did not remember it. Not a single feature. As though I had looked into water."

"The boatman Mustafa said the same," I said. "About the man who sought me in the port."

"Then he is seeking you." Kyra Theodora nodded, not in the least surprised. "And what I have. So it is connected." She raised a hand laden with rings and rang a little silver bell. "Despina! Coffee. And the casket."

* * *

The coffee was black, thick, bitter, in tiny porcelain cups painted with golden birds - so thin that light shone through the porcelain. Despina set the tray on the marble bench, beside it a small casket of dark wood inlaid with mother-of-pearl, and withdrew three paces, standing by the fountain like a sentry.

"Do you know what the Palaiologoi are, young man?" asked Kyra Theodora, sipping her coffee.

"The last rulers of this city. So Ferhat said."

"The last basileis." She set down her cup. "For two hundred years they ruled what was left of a great empire. An empire which for a thousand years was the centre of the world. When in your - wherever it may be - country people lived in dugouts and prayed to trees, here, in this city, there were a million people. Marble palaces. Libraries. A hippodrome for a hundred thousand spectators. Aqueducts. Hospitals. A university. Gold. Silk." She smiled. "And at the end there remained - one city. One set of walls. And fifty thousand people behind them. And the last emperor, Constantine. The Eleventh. Who knew that the city would fall - and did not leave."

"Why?"

"Because he was the last," said Kyra Theodora simply. "The last does not leave. The last remains so that someone will remember there was a first." She paused. "On the twenty-ninth of May, at dawn, the Turks broke through the walls at the Gate of Saint Romanus. Constantine tore off his purple boots and all the imperial insignia, so that no one would recognize him, took a sword and threw himself into the very thick. As a simple soldier. No one ever saw him again. Neither living nor dead. The body was never found."

"And what became of him?"

Kyra Theodora looked at me with her young, shining eyes.

"That, young man," she said, "is the most interesting part."

* * *

"The Greeks say," she began, lowering her voice, "that Constantine did not die. When the Turks were already near, when he was surrounded and swords were raised over him, an angel came down from heaven. Took him. And carried him under the earth. Under the Golden Gate - the oldest, the greatest gate of the city, through which emperors once entered the city in triumph. The Turks themselves walled it up, long ago - they say because they feared this legend." She paused. "The angel laid him in a cave under the gate. And turned him to marble. The Marble King. He lies there to this day. Sleeping. With a sword in his hand."

"And waits?"

"And waits," the old woman nodded. "One day the angel will return. Wake him. Give back his sword. And the Marble King will come out from under the Golden Gate - and take the city back. And drive the Turks to the Red Apple. To the very edge of the earth, from where they came."

"Do you believe it?"

Kyra Theodora laughed - drily, ringingly, as old little bells ring.

"Young man, I am ninety-one. I have outlived four Sultans, three Patriarchs and two husbands. I know how the world is made. Marble kings do not wake." She sipped her coffee. "But I know something else. There is another legend. It is told less often. About Saint Sophia."

"Tell it."

"On the night when the Turks burst into the city, a liturgy was being served in Saint Sophia. The last. The church was full - women, children, old men, all who could not fight. And the priest stood at the altar with the holy gifts in his hands." She paused. "The Turks broke down the doors. Rushed in. And then the wall behind the altar opened. By itself. And the priest entered it - with the holy gifts, not having finished the liturgy. And the wall closed behind him."

"Is he there still?"

"Still," nodded Kyra Theodora. "In the wall. Waiting. They say he will come out when the city is ours again. And finish the liturgy from the very place where he broke off. From the very word."

Two legends. A king turned to marble under a gate. A priest gone into a wall. Neither died. Both wait. Both will return.

"When this rose blooms in a foreign land - I shall return."

I did not know where these words had come to me from. What rose? What land? I had never heard them. And yet they came - and stood beside the old woman's legends like one more candle in a row.

* * *

Kyra Theodora opened the casket.

Inside, on faded purple velvet, lay three things.

A small icon - the Mother of God with the Child, dark, almost black with age, in a silver frame, bent as though it had been struck.

A ring - massive, golden, with a seal: a double-headed eagle, as on the crest above the gate.

And a coin.

One. Small, golden, worn.

With an incomprehensible sign on one side. And interlaced lines on the other.

I stopped breathing.

I knew this coin. In my pocket lay exactly such. Three - left after I gave several to Hatice Hanim. And one more - warm, her coin, which she gave me "because you have the same."

Slowly, feeling my fingers shake, I took one of mine from my pocket. Laid it beside.

Identical. To the last stroke.

Kyra Theodora looked a long while at the two coins lying side by side on the purple velvet. Then she raised her eyes to me. And in them there was no longer any mockery.

"So Despina is right," she said quietly. "And I, an old fool, did not sleep three nights for nothing."

* * *

She told me everything - slowly, in French, with long pauses, as though lifting each word from the bottom of a deep well.

Her ancestor - very distant, in the twelfth or thirteenth generation, she had lost count - had been one of those who stood on the walls on the last night. Manuel Melissenos, a relative of the emperor, commander of a detachment at the Blachernae Palace. On the night of the twenty-eighth to the twenty-ninth of May, when the whole city was praying in Saint Sophia and Turkish drums thundered beneath the walls, a man climbed up to him on the wall.

"Young," said Kyra Theodora. "A stranger. Neither Greek, nor Latin, nor Turk. In strange clothes. Without weapons. Without a name - he said so: 'I do not remember my name.' He told Manuel that the city would fall at dawn. That this could not be stopped. That so it must be." The old woman paused. "Manuel wanted to arrest him as a spy. But the man said to him: 'Do not be afraid. The city will not die. It will change its name. So it is with cities - and with people. The name changes, and the city remains. Like me.' And gave him this."

She touched the coin with the tip of a transparent finger.

"And said: 'Keep it. When another comes with the same - give him what you saw this night.'"

"What did he see?"

"That is not written in the family chronicle," said Kyra Theodora. "Manuel survived. Was taken prisoner. Ransomed. Lived another thirty years. Wrote down everything - about that night, about the wall, about the man without a name. But what he saw - he did not write. Wrote only: 'I saw how he went away. This cannot be described in words. Let the one who comes see for himself.'" She paused. "The chronicle burned in a fire in the Phanar a hundred years ago. Only that phrase remained - my great-grandfather knew it by heart. And the coin."

"And what am I to do with it?"

Kyra Theodora took her coin from the velvet. Then took my hand - with dry fingers, light as fallen leaves. Laid the coin on my palm. And closed my fingers around it.

"Take it," she said. "Five hundred years my family has kept it. Waited. My grandmothers told of it to their granddaughters, like a fairy tale. I myself told it to my granddaughter. She laughed." Her voice wavered. "She died last year. Of cholera. I have no one left to tell."

The coin in my palm was warm. Like the one that Hatice Hanim had given me.

* * *

When I was already rising to leave, Kyra Theodora stopped me - with a gesture light as the wave of a fan.

"One moment. Look at the fountain."

I looked. Along the rim of the marble basin, all round, in a circle, ran an inscription - Greek letters, weathered but still distinguishable.

"Read it," said the old woman.

"I do not read Greek."

"Try," she said with a sly smile. "It is a riddle. It was set in Saint Sophia a thousand years ago. Just such an inscription was on its fountain - where people washed before entering the church. My ancestor ordered it carved here when this house was built. I shall tell you what it says: 'Wash your sins, not only your face.'" She paused. "And now the riddle. What is special about this inscription?"

I looked at the letters. They ran in a circle. Without beginning and without end. I walked round the fountain, following them with my eyes, trying to understand where the line begins.

And suddenly understood - not knowing Greek, not knowing a single letter. Understood the way I had understood the signs on the ledger at Yusuf's.

"It reads the same," I said slowly. "From the beginning and from the end. In both directions. In a circle. One can begin anywhere - and arrive at the same place."

Kyra Theodora clapped her hands - drily, ringingly, like a child. The peacock on her knees stirred in displeasure and spread its tail - enormous, shimmering blue, green, gold, with a hundred eyes.

"Bravo!" said the old woman. "Bravo, young man! Of my guests in sixty years not one has solved it. Not one. Not even the Patriarch." She leaned toward me. "And do you know what my great-grandfather said of this inscription? He said: 'It is not a riddle. It is a description. Some people are made so. Their life reads the same from the beginning and from the end. Because it has neither beginning nor end. Only a circle.'"

* * *

We returned across the Golden Horn already at dusk. The water darkened, from gold became copper, then lilac, then black. On the shores lights were kindled. Mustafa rowed silently, powerfully, rhythmically, and the boat slid over the sunken chain as over the back of a sleeping serpent.

Everyone was silent. Even Akmal, who all evening had been feeding the peacock figs and for this had been drenched in Despina's contempt, sat quietly in the stern, looking at the water. Hasan dozed, leaning against the side - his business with the mastic dealer, it seemed, had never taken place. Orhan sharpened his knife, but without his usual fury, almost thoughtfully.

Kemal sat beside me. Then, without a word, he held out his hand palm up - and looked at me.

I understood. I took Kyra Theodora's coin from my pocket and laid it on his palm.

Kemal held it a long time. Then nodded - gravely, once - and gave it back to me.

"Warm," he said in Turkish. I understood without translation.

"Ferhat," I said, looking at the dark water. "Did you hear what she told? About the Marble King?"

"I heard," Ferhat nodded. "Despina told it in the arbour. To everyone. Three times." He paused. "Grandfather knows that legend. He says we Turks have a similar one. About the Red Apple."

"Which?"

"Kyzyl Elma. The Red Apple." Ferhat looked at the water. "It is the place we always go toward. Far away. At the edge of the world. No one knows where it is. When we took this city - we thought the Red Apple was here. Then we thought - in Rome. Then - in Vienna." He paused. "Grandfather says the Red Apple is always where one has not yet reached. And when you reach it - it turns out to be farther." Ferhat smiled. "The Greeks wait for their king to return. We seek an apple that cannot be reached. Grandfather says it is one and the same tale. Only told from two ends."

From two ends. Like the inscription on the fountain.

"And you?" I asked. "What do you believe?"

Ferhat was silent a long while.

"I believe that Father will return," he said at last. Quietly. For the first time - not merrily, not lightly. "Like the Marble King. Someday. When someone wakes him."

Mustafa at the oars suddenly began to sing - in a low, hoarse voice, something slow, drawn-out, boatmen's. Hasan woke and joined in. Akmal picked it up. Even Orhan, after hesitating, began humming the tune under his breath.

And I sat in the boat over the sunken chain, between two shores of one city that had many names, and clutched a warm coin in my pocket.

Five hundred years. Someone, five hundred years ago, climbed a wall to a man named Manuel and told him that the city would change its name but remain. "Like me."

I did not remember this. I could not remember it.

But when the caique nosed against the quay at our shore and I stepped onto the wet boards, it seemed to me for one moment that behind my back, over there, on that shore, at the top of the hill, in the dark window of an old house with a one-headed eagle over its gate, someone raised a candle.

And held it until we passed out of sight.
