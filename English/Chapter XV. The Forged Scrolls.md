Chapter XV
The Forged Scrolls

Nebamun came at dawn, when I was still sitting by the window, having never closed my eyes after the night at the temple pond - and in his hands was not his usual little box with counting tablets, but a scroll. One. Thick, tightly rolled, sealed with a clay seal bearing the impression of the sacred Eye.

"My Pharaoh," he said, bowing with that dry, unhurried precision with which he did absolutely everything. "Forgive me for troubling you at such an hour. But it seems to me that this scroll had better be read before the court wakes."

"What is it?"

"A petition, my Pharaoh. From the Temple. Delivered yesterday after sunset, by Bakenren's messenger." The keeper of the treasury laid the scroll on the little table before me, but did not take his hand from the seal, as though holding down the lid over a boiling pot. "The Temple requests from the royal granaries three thousand hekats of barley and fifteen hundred hekats of emmer. For the Opet festival. For sacrifices to the god, for the bread of the procession, for distribution to the people in Luxor."

"Three thousand hekats," I repeated. "The Temple asks grain of the throne?"

"That is precisely what troubles me, my Pharaoh," said Nebamun. "The Temple has not asked the throne for grain in forty years. The Temple itself fed half the Delta. The Temple, a moon and a half ago, was buying fields from peasants for a sack of barley. And now - it asks. And appends," he at last broke the seal and unrolled the scroll on the table, "inventories of its granaries. All empty. Or almost empty. The Temple writes that the drought has exhausted its stores too, that it has given all to the people and now, for the sake of the piety of the festival, is compelled to turn humbly to the mercy of the Pharaoh."

I looked at the even columns of signs. The names of granaries - temple ones, in Memphis, in Heliopolis, in the capital, in a dozen small towns of the Delta. Figures. The seals of scribes. All neat, clean, flawless.

"And what do you think of it, Nebamun?"

The keeper of the treasury was silent. Then said - evenly, with no expression at all, as one reads aloud the total of a column:

"I think, my Pharaoh, that in forty years I have seen many false scrolls. False scrolls are always flawless. True ones - never. In a true scroll there is always a blot, a smudge, a corrected figure, a beer stain. People who count honestly grow tired and err. People who lie take pains."

He tapped the scroll with a dry finger.

"And this scroll, my Pharaoh, has taken very, very great pains."

* * *

By the time the sun rose above the eastern hills, a strange company had gathered in my chambers.

Nebamun sat on a stool straight as a stick, with a tablet on his knees. The scribe of the council Ani - old, hard of hearing, with a reed brush behind his ear - had been summoned because he read others' handwriting better than anyone in the palace, though each line had to be repeated to him twice. And Iti - I had sent for him to the Library myself, before I had time to think why exactly I needed him. The youth came out of breath, with an ink stain on his cheek, with hair tousled from sleep - and, seeing Nebamun, froze on the threshold like a rabbit before a kite.

"Come in," I said. "Can you read the handwriting of the temple scribes?"

"I can, my Pharaoh," answered Iti, stammering slightly. "In the Library are kept copies of the temple inventories for the last twenty floods. I... I copied them. All. Last spring, when old Amenemope ordered the storeroom put in order."

"All?" Nebamun raised an eyebrow.

"All, esteemed Nebamun. Four hundred and twelve scrolls." Iti blushed. "I remember the hand of each who wrote them. Not because I tried. Simply... it sticks."

Nebamun looked at me. I looked at Nebamun. The keeper of the treasury for the first time all morning permitted himself something like a smile.

"Sit down, boy," he said. "It seems that you now stand worth more than all three thousand hekats."

Ani, tilting his head to one side, whispered loudly:

"What does he stand? Repeat for the record?"

"He is worth a great deal, esteemed Ani," Nebamun said patiently.

"Ah!" Ani nodded. "All the young are worth a great deal. Then they cheapen. In his years I myself was worth a whole bull. And now - half a jug of beer." And, pleased with his own joke, he began to scratch on his potsherd.

Iti, unable to help it, snorted into his fist - and at once looked at me in fright. I smiled at him, and he blushed still more.

* * *

We spread the Temple's scroll on the floor - it was so long that it took up half the room - and Iti knelt beside it, bending so low that he almost touched the papyrus with his nose.

For a long time he was silent. Ran a finger along the lines, not touching them. Moved his lips.

"This," he said at last, pointing to the inventory of the granaries of Memphis, "was written by Pentu. A junior scribe of the temple of Ptah. His 'k' always has a tail to the left, like a cat looking back. You see? And this - Heliopolis - also Pentu. And the capital - Pentu. And Bubastis. And Sais." Iti raised his head, bewildered. "My Pharaoh, all this was written by one man."

"Is that strange?"

"Very, my Pharaoh. Granary inventories are written on the spot. The scribe of Memphis writes in Memphis, the scribe of Heliopolis in Heliopolis. Their hands are different, I know them all. And here - one hand. Twelve granaries, twelve cities, from Memphis to Sais - and one hand. As if someone sat in one room and wrote them all in a row. In one night."

"Perhaps the Temple ordered them copied out fair?" I suggested. "For a petition to the throne."

"Perhaps," Iti agreed. "But then..."

He fell silent. Licked the tip of a finger - I had no time to stop him - and carefully touched the edge of a sign at the very bottom of the inventory.

The ink smeared.

Iti held his finger up to the light. A black smudge remained on the skin.

"Then," he said quietly, "why is the seal at the bottom old? The seal - of last flood's inventory. I have seen it. Here it is written that the inventory was drawn up on New Year's Day, on the first day of the month of Thoth, before two witnesses. That was more than two moons ago. And the ink is fresh. Ink two moons old does not smear, my Pharaoh. It eats into the papyrus. But this..." he showed his finger, "this was written the day before yesterday."

The room fell silent.

"Repeat for..." Ani began, but Nebamun laid a dry hand on his shoulder, and the old scribe fell silent.

* * *

"That is not all," said Nebamun. He was already standing over the scroll, bent, and his eyes behind narrowed lids ran along the columns of figures as fingers run over the beads of an abacus. "Boy, read me the inventory of Memphis. All of it. Aloud."

Iti read. Nebamun listened, moving his lips soundlessly.

"Stop," he said. "Again. The line about receipts."

"'On the third day of the second month of the season of Akhet,'" Iti read, "'there was delivered by river to the granary of the temple of Ptah in Memphis forty barges of barley from the Upper nomes. Twelve thousand hekats. The barley was distributed to the people within one moon, by the mercy of the High Priest.'"

"Forty barges," Nebamun repeated. "By river. On the third day of the second month of Akhet." He turned to me. "My Pharaoh, do you remember what the esteemed Merira said at the council?"

I remembered. I remembered the tattooed arms, the thunderous voice: "My barques stand in the Nile up to their bellies, like ducks on ice."

"Merira said," I answered slowly, "that cargo barges with grain would ground on the first shoal. That bread can be carried only on asses."

"Merira said that of the present season, my Pharaoh," Nebamun nodded. "And the third day of the second month of Akhet is precisely the day when the nilometer showed the lowest water in forty floods. The Keeper of the Gauge sends me a copy of his tablet every morning - I keep them all. On that day, my Pharaoh, not even a fishing boat could have passed along the Nile. And the Temple writes that forty barges with grain passed."

"So there was no grain?"

"So," said Nebamun, "there were no barges. And the grain..." He straightened. "The grain, my Pharaoh, perhaps was. Only it did not come by river, and did not go anywhere. Lay where it lay. And on papyrus it was first brought in, and then distributed to the people. So that on papyrus the granaries should be empty."

I sat looking at the endless ribbon of flawless signs spread on my floor. Twelve granaries. Twelve cities. One hand. Fresh ink under old seals. Forty barges that had passed along a river along which not even a boat could pass.

"The Temple is hiding grain," I said aloud.

"The Temple is hiding grain, my Pharaoh," Nebamun confirmed. "And now asks for more. From the royal granaries. Three thousand hekats. Which, if you give them, will empty our granaries by a third. And then by spring all will starve - except the Temple."

"And then," Iti added quietly, not raising his eyes from the scroll, "people will go not to the Pharaoh but to those who have bread."

We all looked at him. He was abashed.

"So said the nomarch Ankhtifi," he muttered. "I recorded the audience. 'If the throne cannot protect - he who has bread will protect.'"

* * *

Ani had long been nodding over his potsherd, Nebamun had gone for copies of the nilometer tablets, and Iti still sat on the floor over the Temple's scroll, running a finger along the lines - as though hoping to find something more there.

"Iti," I said. "Come here. Rest."

He rose, came over, sat on the edge of the bench - on the very edge, as though afraid to take up more room than was proper.

"You noticed what no one in the palace would have noticed," I said. "Neither Nebamun, nor Ani, nor I."

"I merely copied scrolls, my Pharaoh," he said. "Many scrolls. When you copy a great many - you begin to see. Not letters. Hands." He paused. "Every scribe writes as he breathes. Hurries - letters run. Fears - letters tremble. Lies - letters..." he sought a word, "become too beautiful. As with Pentu here. Pentu usually writes carelessly. He is lazy. And here - as if each letter were drawn for a tombstone."

"Because he was afraid," I said.

"Or because someone stood behind his back," said Iti. And at once blushed, as though he had said too much.

I looked at him. At the ink stain on his cheek. At the tousled hair. At the thin, stained fingers nervously clasped on his knees.

"May I ask, my Pharaoh?" he suddenly said, not raising his eyes. "Do you... do you know the riddles of the scribes?"

"The riddles of the scribes?"

"In the school of scribes the elders set them to the younger. " Iti brightened, and fear in his face for the first time that morning yielded to something boyish. "Our signs, you see... they are themselves like riddles. A mouth is drawn - and it reads as the sound 'r.' Water is drawn - and it reads 'n.' A beetle is drawn - and it means 'to become.' One can write a word so that only he will read it who knows how to look." He took a potsherd that lay by Ani and a reed brush. "Here. Read."

He drew three signs. An owl. A ripple of water. And a little seated man with a hand to his mouth.

I looked. Owl - "m." Water - "n." The little man at the mouth - that is the sign set at the end of words about food, about speech, about... I suddenly understood.

"Mn," I said slowly. "'Menes.'"

"Almost!" Iti beamed. "Only it is not 'Menes.' It is 'men' - 'to remain,' 'to abide.' The little man with the hand to the mouth is for words about speech, but here he stands because..." he suddenly faltered, blushed. "Because it is the name that remains when it is spoken. So we were taught: the name of the Pharaoh is what remains even when the Pharaoh himself is no more. Because it is pronounced. While the name is pronounced - the man abides."

"And if the name is pronounced by no one?" I asked.

Iti was silent.

"Then he goes into the Duat forever, my Pharaoh," he said quietly. "So the priests say. That is why names are carved in stone. That is why for enemies they are chiselled away."

I remembered myself, nameless. He who does not remember his name - no one pronounces it either.

"Set another," I said.

Iti thought, then drew: a feather. And a heart.

"Which is heavier?" he asked.

"The heart," I said. "A feather weighs nothing."

"Wrong, my Pharaoh." Iti smiled - shyly, but with evident pleasure at having caught the Pharaoh himself. "The feather of Maat weighs exactly as much as truth. And the heart - as much as a lie. If a man lived truthfully - the heart is lighter than the feather. If he lied - heavier." He looked at the scroll on the floor. "Pentu's heart now is probably heavier than a mountain."

"And the heart of the one who stood behind him?"

"I do not know, my Pharaoh," said Iti. "Perhaps he has no heart. Perhaps he left it in a jar, as is done with the dead. So that it should not be weighed."

* * *

When Nebamun returned with the nilometer tablets, Iti was again sitting over the scroll, and I was pretending to read a report from Ptahhotep. The keeper of the treasury laid the tablets on the table, spread them like dice - one to one, by day - and silently pointed to the one lying under the third day of the second month of Akhet. The figure was the lowest of all.

"Now we need one thing, my Pharaoh," he said. "To see with our own eyes at least one of these granaries. If it is empty - I am an old fool and beg the Temple's pardon. If it is full..."

"If it is full," I said, "then the Temple is a thief."

"If it is full," Nebamun corrected me with impeccable precision, "then there is an error in the Temple's inventories. Let someone else pronounce the word 'thief.' I am the keeper of the treasury. I speak only in figures."

He was gathering the tablets back into his little box, and suddenly, without raising his head, said in precisely the same tone in which he had reported figures:

"My Pharaoh. Permit an old servant a word - not about grain."

"Speak."

"At court they whisper," said Nebamun, carefully laying tablet to tablet, "that in recent days the Pharaoh sends too often for the junior keeper of the Library. That the boy carries the Pharaoh notes which no one reads. That the Pharaoh smiles when the boy enters." He closed the box. "I, of course, attach no importance to gossip. A court without gossip is like the Nile without crocodiles: both calmer and duller. But if it pleases the Pharaoh that the boy go on carrying notes which no one reads - let him carry them not by day and not through the main corridor. There the walls have ears. And some of the ears have bracelets."

I felt my ears burn. Iti, bent over the scroll two paces from us, froze - his back turned to stone, and even from the back of his head one could see how he had flushed to the roots of his hair.

"I thank you, Nebamun," I said as evenly as I could.

"Not at all, my Pharaoh." The keeper of the treasury rose, made his dry, exact bow - and already at the door added, just as imperturbably: "By the way, the boy counts splendidly. If the Library should ever grow too close for him, I have room in the treasury. There, at least, the walls are thicker."

He went. Iti did not raise his head for a long time. And when at last he raised it - he looked not at me but at the window, and said quietly, as though to himself:

"I shall go through the garden, my Pharaoh. There are no walls there."

* * *

"No," said Senusret.

"I did not ask permission," I said.

"I know, my Pharaoh. That is why I say 'no' in advance." The commander of the army stood before me, arms folded on his chest, fully armed, as though he already knew where I meant to go. "Last time you went to the temple pond in another's dress and your own sandals. The guard who let you out now stands at the southern outposts with Djehuty and, they say, thanks the gods every night that he is alive. This time you mean to go to a temple granary. At night. To Bakenren's men."

"I need to see it with my own eyes."

"Let my men see it."

"Your men the Temple knows by sight. Me - no. No one expects the Pharaoh in a granary at night."

Senusret looked at me for a long time. Then sighed heavily - as fathers sigh when they understand that their son's stubbornness is stronger than their own.

"Very well," he said. "But this time I go with you. And Inu. And Bata. And this..." he glanced at Iti standing by the doors, "this scribe, since without him no one will tell which ink is which. And sandals, my Pharaoh." He pointed to my feet. "The sandals you will put on are mine."

Senusret's sandals proved two fingers too large for me, coarse, trodden down, smelling of leather and road. Bata, seeing them on me, snorted and at once received a cuff from Inu.

* * *

The temple granary, which the inventory described as "empty after distribution to the people," stood on the northern edge of the capital, beyond a canal - where the city passed into fields, and the fields, parched and cracked, passed into desert. We went there on foot, in the dark, five of us - I, Senusret, Inu, Bata and Iti - in plain dress, as day labourers go who are returning from work. The moon was no longer copper, as on the night of the pond, but white, waning, and its light was exactly enough not to stumble.

The granary proved huge - I had not expected such. A long low building of mud brick, with a flat roof on which in a row stood dome-shaped storehouses, like gigantic beehives. On each - a round opening at the top where grain is poured in, and a small door below where it is raked out. All the doors were sealed with clay. On each seal - the sacred Eye.

At the gates, on a mat, leaning against the wall, slept a watchman. Around him stood three empty beer jugs. A fourth, half drunk, he clutched in his hand.

"Djedhor," whispered Iti. "I know him. He used to be a scribe at the Temple. They drove him out for drunkenness. Now he guards."

"And how he guards," Bata grunted.

We had almost passed him when Djedhor suddenly opened one eye.

"Who goes?" he asked in a slurred tongue. "Thieves? If thieves - come in, take, I do not care, I am drunk. If priests - I am not drunk, I am praying. Lying down. So it is nearer to the god of the earth."

Senusret stepped toward him, but I held him by the arm.

"We are day labourers, father," I said. "Looking for work. We heard that men are needed at the Temple - to load grain."

Djedhor burst out laughing - loudly, drunkenly, to tears.

"Load!" he squeezed out at last. "Load! Son, there is nothing here to load. Here all is empty. So it is written." He slapped his palm on the air, as on an invisible scroll. "Bakenren himself told me: Djedhor, if anyone asks - all is empty. All distributed. To the people. By mercy." He hiccuped. "So I say: empty."

"And in truth?" Iti asked quietly.

Djedhor turned a cloudy eye on him - and suddenly squinted.

"And I know you," he said. "You are that boy from the Library. Who copied my old inventories. You even laughed at my handwriting." He wagged a finger. "I remember everything. I am drunk, but I am not a fool." He hiccuped again and lowered his voice to a loud whisper: "Want to know what is in truth? Go and look. I saw nothing. I am asleep." And closed his eye, embracing the jug like a beloved woman.

* * *

Senusret cut the seal off the nearest door with the tip of his knife - carefully, so that afterward, if need be, it could be pressed back. Inu brought forward a lamp which until then he had hidden under his cloak. Bata pulled the door.

And from the storehouse, rustling like water, barley poured out.

It streamed to the ground, onto our feet, golden in the lamplight - dry, large, clean, smelling of summer and field. Bata leapt back. Inu swore - for the first time in all the time I had known him. Senusret stood motionless, watching a golden heap grow at his feet.

"Empty," said Bata. And laughed - nervously, almost hysterically. "Empty! Distributed to the people!"

We opened a second storehouse. Emmer. A third - barley. A fourth - wheat, select, such as there was not even in the royal granaries. A fifth, sixth, seventh. All full. To the very vault.

Iti stood in the midst of it all, holding in his palm a handful of grain, and could not utter a word.

"How much is here?" I asked hoarsely.

Iti looked at the storehouses. Counted the domes. Moved his lips soundlessly.

"If all are full alike, my Pharaoh..." He swallowed. "There is here as much as the Temple asks of you for the festival. Only three times over."

"In one granary," said Senusret.

"In one," Iti confirmed. "And in the inventory there are twelve."

And then Inu, who was going round the storehouses with the lamp, quietly called to us from the far end.

By the very wall, apart from the rest, stood sacks. Dozens of sacks, tightly tied, laid in a row as if prepared for dispatch. On each - a mark in paint. Not the sacred Eye. Another sign: a triangle, point down, and beneath it three wavy strokes.

"What is it?" I asked.

Iti bent, peered - and paled.

"It is not a temple sign, my Pharaoh," he whispered. "It is a sign... So on the maps in the Library the southern cataracts are marked. The triangle - a mountain. Three strokes - running water. It means... 'south, beyond the cataracts.'"

In the granary it became so quiet that I heard outside, at the gate, drunken Djedhor snoring.

"South," Senusret slowly repeated. "Beyond the cataracts. To where the southerners come from. To where on the prisoner a temple scarab was found."

No one answered. No one needed to answer.

* * *

We heard steps just as we were closing the last door.

Light, hurried, shuffling - and the flicker of a lamp in the doorway. Inu instantly put out his own, Senusret pushed me behind the nearest dome, Bata and Iti pressed to the wall.

Into the granary came a man. Young, thin, in the dress of a temple scribe, with a writing box under his arm and a lamp in a trembling hand. He looked about - fearfully, like a thief - passed us to a table in the corner, set down the lamp, opened the box, took out a clean scroll, an inkwell, a brush. And sat down to write.

He wrote quickly, biting his lip, glancing back at the door every now and then. Wrote, looking into another scroll - old, worn - and transferring something from it, changing, drawing each letter with that very painstakingness of which Iti had spoken - as for a tombstone.

Iti, pressed to the wall two paces from me, soundlessly moved his lips. I read one word from them.

"Pentu."

Senusret came out from behind the dome first. The scribe jerked up his head, saw an enormous dark figure in the lamplight - and dropped his brush. Ink splashed on the scroll, on the table, on his knees.

"Do not shout," said Senusret quietly. "No one means to kill you. For now."

Pentu did not shout. He slid from the bench to the floor, to his knees, and covered his face with his hands. His shoulders shook.

I came up. Crouched before him. Inu lit the lamp again, and the light fell on my face.

Pentu raised his head - and recognised me. I saw recognition pass over his face like a wave: first incomprehension, then horror, then something like relief - such as comes to people who have too long awaited being caught, and now at last are caught.

"My... my Pharaoh," he rasped and pressed his forehead to the floor.

"Rise, Pentu," I said. "And tell me who stands behind your back when you write."

* * *

He did not tell at once - first he wept, then hiccuped with fear, then Bata thrust a flask of beer at him, and he drank, his teeth chattering against the neck. And then he began to speak - quickly, disjointedly, as though afraid that if he stopped, he would no longer be able to begin.

Bakenren had summoned him three days ago. Gave him the old inventories - real, with real figures - and ordered him to rewrite them anew. All twelve. So that the granaries should be empty. To affix to each inventory the old seal - the seals Bakenren had brought himself, in a casket. To write of distributions to the people, of forty barges from the Upper nomes. The forty barges Bakenren had invented himself. Pentu had told him that by the river now not even a boat could pass. Bakenren had answered: "The Pharaoh does not look at the river. The Pharaoh looks at scrolls."

"And the sacks?" I asked. "With the southern mark?"

Pentu shook harder.

"I do not know, my Pharaoh. I swear. They are brought at night. By people I do not know. Loaded on asses and led away. Where - I do not know. I do not ask. Whoever asks - he..." He broke off.

"He what?"

"He goes to serve in the temple at the southern cataracts, my Pharaoh," Pentu whispered. "It is hot there. And scorpions. And from there no letters are written."

"Why did you agree?"

Pentu was silent for a long time.

"I have a mother, my Pharaoh," he said at last. "And three sisters. My father died in the last famine. Bakenren said: if I rewrote the inventories, my sisters would eat temple bread until the next flood. If I refused - their field..." He faltered. "We have a small field. Near Bubastis. Bakenren said it might turn up in the inventory of those bought out by the Temple. Backdated. He said: 'I know how to write backdated, Pentu. Now you do too.'"

Senusret let out a low sound - half growl, half groan.

I looked at this thin, ink-smeared youth kneeling before me in the golden barley which the Temple had "distributed to the people" - and thought of a tale I had heard once in childhood. Or had not heard - but for some reason knew.

"Pentu," I said. "Do you know the tale of the eloquent peasant?"

He raised to me tear-stained, astonished eyes.

"I know it, my Pharaoh. Everyone knows it. We were taught to write by it."

"Tell it."

He did not understand - no one understood - but began to tell. First stammering, then more evenly, as though the very familiar story calmed him.

"'There lived a peasant, and his name was Khunanup. He loaded asses with salt, reeds, leopard skins, stones and herbs from the oasis and went to the city to exchange them for bread for his wife and children. And on the road he met a man named Nemtynakht, a servant of the great steward. And Nemtynakht envied his asses. He spread his cloak on the narrow road - so that on one side was the cloak and on the other a field of barley. And said: "Do not step on my cloak." The peasant turned aside. And his ass bit off an ear of barley from the field. And Nemtynakht said: "Your ass has eaten my barley. I take your ass." And took the asses. And beat the peasant.'"

"And what did the peasant do?" Bata asked, not noticing himself how he had become absorbed.

"'He went to the great steward,'" Pentu continued. "'And began to speak. He spoke of truth. Of Maat. Of how he who holds the scales must not incline. Of how a lie comes out, as silt from water. He spoke so beautifully that the steward went to the Pharaoh and said: "There is a peasant, he speaks better than all scribes."'" Pentu sobbed. "'And the Pharaoh commanded: "Let him speak more. Do not answer him. Let him speak and you write it down." And the peasant came nine times. Nine times he spoke. And was not listened to - on purpose. And he went on speaking. And the ninth time he said: "I shall complain of you to Anubis." And went away, ready to die.'"

"And?" asked Iti, though, of course, he knew.

"'And the steward brought him back. And read him all that was written down in the nine times. And returned his asses. And gave him all the property of Nemtynakht. Because truth spoken nine times weighs more than a cloak on a road.'"

Pentu fell silent.

"Bakenren spread a cloak on the road," I said. "For you. For your sisters. For the whole Valley. And you turned aside. And your ass bit off an ear."

"Yes, my Pharaoh," whispered Pentu.

"I shall not take your ass, Pentu," I said. "And I shall not send you to the southern cataracts. You will return to the temple. In the morning. As if nothing had been. You will finish writing this inventory - as Bakenren ordered. You will give it to him. And you will go on copying all he orders, from now. Only every inventory - every one, real and false - you will copy twice. The second copy you will give to Iti. Through the garden. At night. Do you understand?"

Pentu looked at me as though I spoke a foreign tongue.

"And my sisters? And the field?"

"Your family's field from today is in the inventory of royal lands," I said. "Not temple ones. Backdated." I smiled slightly. "I too know how to write backdated, Pentu. Ask Nebamun."

Pentu covered his face with his hands and wept again - but now these were different tears.

* * *

We returned to the palace after midnight. Senusret led me to the doors of my chambers, took back his sandals from me, put them on and silently went away - only at the turn of the corridor did he look back and say:

"You let him go, my Pharaoh. He may run to Bakenren and tell all."

"He may," I agreed.

"And then?"

"And then, Senusret, I shall know that I erred in a man. That too is knowledge."

He looked at me for a long time. Then shook his head - whether in reproof or in respect - and went away.

But I did not go to sleep. I went to the Library - not knowing why. Perhaps because it was quiet there. Perhaps because Iti had gone there too, to his cubbyhole by the storeroom, and for some reason I did not wish this night to end without him.

In the Library one lamp burned. Old Amenemope sat at the table, leaning on his staff of black wood, and was reading something, moving a finger along the lines so slowly as though each word had to be not read but excavated. Iti sat opposite him on the floor, hugging his knees, and listened.

Seeing me, both leapt up. I waved a hand.

"Sit. I came to listen. What are you at?"

"The Duat, my Pharaoh," said Amenemope. "The boy asked me where those go whose names no one pronounces. And I do not know. I only read him what is written. And much is written, and various."

I sat down on the bench by the window. The night beyond the window was dark and warm; somewhere in the garden an owl cried.

"Read," I said. "I am interested too."

* * *

Amenemope read long - and not so much read as told, now and then glancing into the scroll, as if checking memory against papyrus.

He told that a man has not one soul but several. That there is the ka - the double, the force of life, which is born together with a man and stays to live in the tomb after his death, and to it must be brought bread and beer, otherwise it will starve. That there is the ba - a bird with a human head, which by day flies out of the tomb and flies over the world of the living, and by night returns to the body. That there is the akh - light, radiance, into which a righteous man is transformed when he has passed all trials. And there is the name - ren - which too is a part of a man, and the shadow - shut - which too is a part of a man, and the heart - ib - which remembers all that a man has done.

"And the heart is weighed," said Iti. "On scales, against the feather of Maat. In the Hall of Two Truths."

"Of Two Truths," I repeated. "Why two?"

Amenemope raised faded eyes to me.

"Because truth, my Pharaoh, is always double. One - that which the living see. The other - that which the gods see. And they do not always coincide. Therefore the hall is called the Hall of Two Truths. There the dead man stands before forty-two judges and says to them: 'I did not steal. I did not lie. I did not kill. I did not take bread from the hungry. I did not falsify the scales.' Forty-two oaths. And if even one is false - the heart drags down. And then it is devoured by Ammit - a beast with the head of a crocodile, the body of a lion and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus. And the man dies a second time. Now for ever."

"And if all are true?" asked Iti.

"Then he goes to the Fields of Iaru," said Amenemope. "To the Fields of Reeds. There all is as here, only better. Barley taller than a man. The river always full. No drought." He smiled. "And no Temple that hides grain."

Iti snorted. I too.

"And the stars?" I asked. "I have heard that Pharaohs after death go to the stars."

"They go, my Pharaoh. To the Imperishable Ones." Amenemope jabbed his staff at the window, at the northern part of the sky. "You see those stars that circle around one point and never set beyond the horizon? All the other stars sink in the west, die, are born again in the east. But these - never. They are ever awake. The ancients wrote that the Pharaoh after death becomes one of them. That is why in the most ancient tombs there are narrow shafts aimed straight at them - so that the soul of the Pharaoh should know where to fly."

"And that one in the south? The brightest?"

"Sopdet, my Pharaoh." The old man's voice warmed. "The star of Isis. She vanishes from the sky for seventy days - exactly as long as embalming lasts. And then returns - at dawn, just before sunrise. And when she returns - the flood begins. Every year. From the beginning of time. She is the herald. She promises that the water will come."

"Did she return this year?" I asked quietly.

Amenemope was silent for a long time.

"She returned, my Pharaoh," he said at last. "Three years in a row she has returned. On her day. In her place." He lowered his staff. "And the water - did not come."

* * *

Iti sat a long time silent, hugging his knees. Then suddenly asked - quietly, almost in a whisper, as though himself afraid of his question:

"Venerable Amenemope. Can a man return from the Duat?"

The old man looked at him.

"Ra returns every morning," he said. "Every night he passes through twelve gates, fights Apep, dies - and every morning is born again. Osiris returned - Isis gathered him in parts, and he came alive to become king of the dead. Sopdet returns every year. So - yes. The gods return."

"And men?"

"Men..." Amenemope stroked the scroll with his palm. "There is one place in the Most Ancient Epic. No one likes to read it. Because no one understands it." He paused. "It says there that the First Ones, who descended from the Heavens, did not die as we do. They went away - and returned. In another body. In another time. Not remembering themselves. And each time they had to learn anew who they were. And when they learned - they remembered everything. All the previous times."

My throat went dry.

"And what happened when they remembered?"

"That is not written there, my Pharaoh. There is only one line. Incomprehensible." The old man narrowed his eyes, as though recalling. "'And when he remembers his first name, he will return what was taken. And the two Eyes will become one.'"

The two Eyes. Merit at the pond. The white leads. The black keeps.

"Who is 'he'?" I asked.

"I do not know, my Pharaoh." Amenemope slowly rose, leaning on his staff. "I only keep scrolls. Perhaps the High Priest knows. He read that place three times in one moon." The old man moved toward the exit, shuffling. At the very door he stopped. "Boy, see the Pharaoh out. For, I see, he walks at night in another's sandals. One may lose one's way."

He went. The lamp crackled. The owl in the garden cried again.

Iti sat on the floor, still hugging his knees, and looked at me. Long. Silently.

"My Pharaoh," he said at last. "And if a man does not remember that he has already died? If he thinks that he lives for the first time? How will he know that he has returned?"

I did not know what to answer. I looked at this youth with an ink stain on his cheek, sitting in a patch of light among thousands of scrolls - and it suddenly seemed to me that I had seen him before. Not yesterday. Not at the audience. Long ago. In another place. In another light. As though we had sat so once already, and he had already asked me this, and I already had not known the answer.

"Perhaps," I said slowly, "someone will tell him. Someone who remembers for him."

Iti lowered his eyes.

"I would tell," he whispered barely audibly. "If I remembered."

The lamp flickered - and went out, burnt to the bottom. And in the darkness of the Library, smelling of cedar, dust and old papyrus, we sat a long time silent, not lighting another - a Pharaoh who did not remember his first name, and a junior keeper of scrolls who promised to remember it.
