Chapter XI
Two Stones

Dawn beyond the narrow window of my room kindled reluctantly, grey and cautious, as though it were itself unsure whether to lay claim to this night at all. I had not closed my eyes from the very moment the tall silhouette had melted into the darkness, returning to me the mastery of my own body - and now I lay motionless, listening to the first, still uncertain sounds of the waking house: the creak of floorboards somewhere below, muffled voices, the splash of water, the cooing of pigeons on the roof.

The threshold was empty. Kemal had gone, as he had promised, with the first light - only on the boards by the door remained a small dark smudge of soot from his lamp, and the half of the blue bead still hanging on its red thread above the doorpost, like an eye from which the pupil has been removed.

My hand, against my will, reached for my belt, to where since the previous evening two stones had rested - in the small leather pouch which Akmal had taken down for me in the attic from the belt of his father, lost at sea. I had not parted with that pouch for an instant since the moment I tied it to my belt - as though foreseeing that sooner or later I should need it.

My palm closed over the pouch - and I froze.

The stones were warm. Not with that ordinary, barely perceptible warmth which any object keeps that has lain a night against the body - but with a warmth far more insistent, almost feverish, as though inside this little leather pouch someone had lit a tiny but quite genuine hearth.

I sat up on the divan, and with fingers trembling from agitation untied the pouch and poured both stones onto my open palm. Black and white, smooth, polished - and scalding, implausibly hot, as though just taken from a furnace, and not from a pouch that had lain all night at my belt.

I involuntarily opened my palm wider, expecting a burn - but no pain followed. This heat, for all its insistence, did not scorch the skin but seemed to seep through it, bypassing the usual paths of sensation, echoing not so much in the fingers as somewhere deeper, where vague, inexplicable recognition is born.

I brought the stones closer to the light growing beyond the window - and saw what I had never noticed before, however long I had studied these finds in the past day.

On the smooth surface of both stones, where the warmth was especially strong, there showed through the finest, barely distinguishable carving - lines so thin that by day, in ordinary light, I should have taken them for chance scratches or a play of light on polished stone. But now, in this grey, still hesitant dawn, these lines formed something remotely resembling writing: curved, flowing into one another, lacking any single direction - a pattern which, as soon as the stone cooled a little, began to pale and melt, like frost under the morning sun, until it vanished altogether, leaving behind only the familiar, flawlessly smooth surface.

My heart pounded as though I were again standing on the threshold of something whose solution must either save me or destroy me completely.

With a trembling hand I reached for the scrap of paper with the incomprehensible inscription - the very one that had so interested old Suleyman yesterday - and held it close to the stone, which still kept the remnants of warmth and the remnants of the pattern that had emerged.

The resemblance was not complete - I would not have vouched for it - but neither was it chance. The same manner in which lines flowed into one another without pauses, without clearly distinguishable boundaries between signs, the same alien, unfathomable logic of writing that would not fit into any alphabet known to me. As though both - the scrap of paper and these stones - had been written, carved, minted by one and the same hand, in one and the same - whatever it might be - epoch.

I tried to summon the warmth again, deliberately, by effort of will - clenched both stones in my fist, shut my eyes, imagined the night figure as clearly as I could. Nothing happened. The stones remained ordinary stones - cool, smooth, silent - however I gazed at them awaiting a miracle which I was clearly unable to repeat.

So, I thought, putting the finds back into the pouch with hands now cooled, this warmth comes not by my will. It comes - if it comes - when something happens that does not depend on me. Last evening, on the balcony, I saw the grey figure for the second time - and, it seems, it was then that the stones in my pouch first seemed to me a little warmer than usual. And at night, when the tall silhouette bent over me, depriving me of power over my own body - the warmth, one must suppose, reached its limit.

This regularity, scarcely outlined, at once gave birth in me to a question far more frightening than the fact of hot stones itself: if the warmth comes together with this being - whoever, whatever it may be - does that not mean that the stones are somehow connected with it? That they are not a mere chance find in a wanderer's pocket but something like... a signal? A warning? And perhaps - and this thought frightened me especially - a sort of key, unlocking a door of whose existence I had not even suspected.

And here memory returned to me a scrap of another, far earlier delusion - the one that had seized me yesterday by the carpet with the old men. Then, in that half-delirium of columns and echoing steps, the stones in my hand had suddenly filled with weight - not warmth, but precisely weight, as though absorbing all the cold that surrounded me from every side at that instant. Perhaps, I thought, these stones respond not to some one particular phenomenon but to the very boundary - between waking and delusion, between what I might call my world and what clearly did not belong to it.

"Remember," the night guest had said.

I did not remember. But the stones, it seemed, remembered for me.

* * *

Light, hurried steps sounded beyond the door, and Akmal appeared on the threshold - sleepy, tousled, but, as usual, irrepressibly cheerful from early morning. Seeing me, pale, with dishevelled hair and an evidently unhealthy expression, he at once grew serious - and then his glance slid upward, to the doorpost, to the cracked bead.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at me. Then, without a word, he carefully took the remaining half down from the nail, tied the thread in a knot and hid it in his fist, as though it were the most ordinary morning chore.

"All is well," I lied, stretching my lips in a smile which, judging by Akmal's not wholly convinced look, came out less confident than I should have liked. "I simply slept badly last night."

He, naturally, did not understand a word, but caught the intonation unerringly and, shaking his head with the air of a man who knows the sure remedy for any affliction, waved his arms, miming the consumption of food, and then peremptorily pointed to the door: come down, breakfast is waiting, and on an empty stomach no bad dreams pass.

* * *

Breakfast was in the yard, on the same motley carpet, under an awning of vine, through whose leaves the sun dropped trembling green patches on the cloth. On the trays - white cheese, salty and crumbly, black olives glistening with oil, tomatoes, cucumbers, quince jam of a thick amber colour, honey in the comb, flat breads still warm, and in the centre of it all a tall copper kettle from which steam rose.

The family was assembled. Suleyman - at the head of the carpet, in a morning robe, with a rosary in his hand. Yusuf - beside him, already with a ledger on his knees, as though even at breakfast he could not part from figures. Orhan - a little apart, sullen as ever, but, I noticed, having pushed a plate of olives toward me before I could sit down. Kemal - quiet, with blue circles under his eyes after the sleepless night; he raised his gaze to me, nodded briefly, as one nods to an accomplice, and looked no more. Akmal plumped down beside me and at once plunged a hand into the honey.

And Ferhat - just returned from the night shift, reeking of tar, fish and sea, with hair wet from washing - sat opposite and wolfed down a flat bread with such greed as though all night he had been loading not ships but mountains.

"Good morning," he said with his mouth full. "How did you sleep in our house?"

I faltered. Kemal, not raising his head, glanced quickly at Akmal. Akmal opened his fist - and on his palm lay half of a blue bead on a red thread.

Ferhat stopped chewing.

Suleyman, seeing the bead, unhurriedly set aside his rosary. He took the half from his grandson, turned it in his fingers, looked at it against the light. Asked Kemal something - quietly, briefly. Kemal answered with one word and nodded in my direction.

The old man was silent for a long time. Then he said something to Ferhat - evenly, calmly, as though speaking of the weather.

"Grandfather says," Ferhat translated slowly, not taking his eyes from me, "that a nazar cracks when it takes upon itself a blow meant for a person. He says - this is good. It means the nazar has done its work." He paused. "And he asks whether you saw anyone in the room last night."

All looked at me. Even Yusuf raised his eyes from his abacus.

"I saw," I said. "Or I dreamed it. I do not know."

Ferhat translated. Suleyman nodded again - without surprise, without fear, as one nods on receiving an expected answer. Then he hung the half of the bead on his wrist, winding the thread over the rosary, and uttered a long phrase which Ferhat translated, stammering:

"Grandfather says: in this city there are many old guests. Some come to those who owe them. Others - to those whom they themselves owe. He says, since your guest took nothing and harmed no one, it means he came not for a debt, but with a debt. And that sooner or later he will say what he wants." Ferhat paused. "And he also says that this evening he will hang a new nazar. A bigger one."

Orhan snorted - but, it seemed to me, not mockingly but rather nervously.

* * *

And then Suleyman, as if deciding that enough had been said of night guests, clapped his palms on his knees, and on the faces of all the boys at once animation ran - as boys all over the world brighten when adults announce a game.

"Bilmece!" Akmal proclaimed, jumping on the spot.

"Riddles," Ferhat explained, smiling. "Every morning grandfather sets a riddle. Whoever guesses first gets the best piece of honey. Today, grandfather says, it will be set for the guest. Let him show what his head is stuffed with besides fog."

Suleyman narrowed his eyes, stroked his beard and slowly, in a chant, uttered several lines. Ferhat translated after him, adjusting to the rhythm:

"'It goes - but does not arrive. All await it - but no one has seen it. It arrives - and at once becomes another.'"

The boys whispered. Akmal fidgeted, evidently knowing the answer and barely restraining himself from shouting it out. Orhan looked at me defiantly.

I thought.

"Tomorrow," I said.

Ferhat translated. Suleyman raised his eyebrows - and laughed contentedly, shaking a finger at Akmal, who groaned in disappointment. A saucer with the thickest piece of honeycomb was pushed toward me.

"Another," said Orhan in his own tongue and, not waiting for his grandfather, set a riddle himself - jerkily, defiantly, looking straight at me.

"'I have a house without doors,'" Ferhat translated, "'and inside - gold. Want the gold - break the house.'"

"An egg," I said almost at once.

Orhan slapped his knee so that the crockery rang. The boys laughed. Yusuf, not raising his head from his accounts, muttered something at which even Ferhat laughed.

"Uncle says," he translated, "that Orhan has been setting that riddle since he was five and it is time to learn a new one."

Orhan scowled, but in his eyes, it seemed to me, flickered something like respect. He thought, then uttered another - slower, quieter.

"'I have two brothers,'" Ferhat translated. "'One - black as night. The other - white as day. They never meet. But between them - everything.'"

Silence hung over the carpet.

I felt the pouch at my belt - cool, cooled down - seem to sway slightly from a non-existent wind.

"Day and night," I said slowly. "The answer is - a day and a night. Or... twilight. What is between."

Orhan looked at me, and in his eyes there was no longer defiance. There was bewilderment.

"He says," Ferhat translated quietly, "that he did not set it on purpose. That it came into his head by itself. He did not know it before."

Kemal, sitting at the edge, took the bit of charcoal that lay behind his ear and on the flagstone of the yard, beside the carpet, quickly drew two circles - black and white - and a wavy line between them. Exactly as yesterday. Then he wiped out the drawing with his palm before anyone but me could make it out.

Suleyman told his rosary. Click. Click. Click.

"Enough riddles," he said at last - I understood it without translation. And everyone, as if on command, returned to eating.

* * *

After breakfast, when Ferhat was already yawning so that he seemed able to swallow the whole yard, I nevertheless made up my mind.

"Ferhat. May I ask you something? The stones. The ones I showed yesterday." I untied the pouch. "Last night they were hot. At dawn a pattern showed on them. Like on the note. Then it vanished."

Ferhat bent, touched the stones - cool, smooth, perfectly ordinary - and shrugged.

"Perhaps they warmed from your body? You were feverish in the night, grandfather says."

"Perhaps," I said. "And the pattern?"

Ferhat was silent. Then he translated my question to his grandfather. Suleyman, narrowing his eyes, took the white stone, turned it - as yesterday, intently, like a craftsman - and returned it.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, "that he is no master of stones. He traded in silk, not gems. But in the Grand Bazaar, in the row of jewellers, there is a master Karapet - an Armenian, an old man, he has had a shop there for fifty years. Grandfather says, if there is a man in this city who knows everything about stones - it is Karapet. If you wish - Akmal will take you. You promised him ships anyway."

Akmal, hearing his name and the word "ships," leapt up so swiftly that he overturned a cup.

* * *

The Grand Bazaar did not begin at once - the city prepared one for it gradually, as a succession of appetisers prepares for the main dish. First - lanes where they traded right from the doorsteps: strings of onions and garlic, mountains of apricots, baskets of eggs, cages of hens scolding in their own hen-language. Then - a square where copperware was sold, and the air rang with hammer blows: chasers right there, before the eyes of buyers, beat patterns on trays and jugs, and the sun flared on the fresh copper as on gold. Then - the street of the cobblers, smelling of leather and tar, and at every door hung bunches of pointed slippers of all colours - scarlet, yellow, emerald.

And then, at a turning, Akmal suddenly stopped and tugged my sleeve, pointing upward.

Above the roofs, above the domes of bathhouses and the minarets, a column rose. Enormous, dark, charred, as though flame had once licked it, bound with iron hoops like a barrel - hoops, so that it should not fall apart. It was older than everything around - older than the houses, older than the mosques, older, it seemed, than the bazaar itself. It stood here as witnesses stand who have been forgotten to be dismissed.

"Çemberlitaş," said Akmal with pride, as one speaks of something one's own. And mimed with his hands the hoops girdling the column.

We came closer. At the foot, in the shade, sat a beggar - old, blind in one eye, in rags, with a copper bowl before him. Seeing that I had thrown back my head, he began to speak - in a hoarse, sing-song voice, evidently relating what he told every gawker a hundred times a day. Akmal listened, nodding, then tried to retell it to me with gestures - and got tangled, waved a hand.

Then the beggar, noticing that I was a foreigner, suddenly switched to a broken mixture of tongues from which I caught several familiar words:

"Old! Very old. Here first king. Under stone - much. Noah's axe. Jug where bread. Crosses. Very, very old. All under earth. No one saw. All know."

"Noah's axe?" I repeated.

The beggar nodded with such enthusiasm that the rags shook on him.

"Noah! Ship! Flood! Axe - here, under column. And also..." he bent closer, lowering his voice to a hoarse whisper, and his seeing eye glittered, "and also what they hide. What older than Noah. What older than all."

I stood at the foot of the column, and it suddenly seemed to me that the pouch at my belt had become a little heavier. Not warmer - precisely heavier, as yesterday, in that half-dream of columns. I laid my palm on it. The stones did not heat. But they seemed to be listening.

Akmal looked at me with alarm.

I threw into the copper bowl one of my strange coins. The beggar caught it, held it up to his seeing eye - and suddenly fell silent. He looked at it for a long time. Then he raised his head, looked at me from below upward - with a quite different, sober, attentive look.

"Not from here," he said. "Not from here, money. And you - not from here."

And thrust the coin into his bosom so quickly as though afraid I would change my mind.

* * *

The Grand Bazaar swallowed us as the sea swallows a boat.

Under endless stone vaults, painted with faded patterns, ran streets - not even streets, but corridors, passing one into another, branching, converging, winding, as though someone had built this labyrinth on purpose so that no one should ever leave it by the way he had entered. Through small windows in the domes fell shafts of light, and in them dust swirled - golden, slow, smelling of cinnamon and leather. From the ceilings hung lamps of coloured glass - blue, red, green - and although by day they were not lit, the sun, passing through them, scattered on the floor flags coloured patches resembling strewn gems.

And the noise - such noise as I had not heard even in the port. Hundreds of voices, calling, bargaining, quarrelling, laughing in dozens of tongues. The ring of coins. The knock of hammers. The rustle of carpets, which were unrolled before buyers in one wide movement, as banners are unfurled.

"Effendi! Effendi!" A carpet seller, pot-bellied, in a fez, seized my sleeve. "Bakın! Look! Only look! To look - free!"

He unrolled before me a carpet - scarlet, with a pattern of blue lozenges and white stars, so bright that my eyes hurt. Akmal said something to him sharply, with boyish impudence - and the seller, throwing up his hands in offence, released my sleeve, calling after us something at which Akmal burst out laughing.

At the next turning a boy with a copper tray on a strap, hung with tiny cups, poured me something black and thick, smelling so bitter and so sweet at once that my head spun. Coffee. I drank - and for a long time afterward felt my heart pounding in my chest.

The jewellers' row was quieter. Here they did not shout - here they whispered. The shops were tiny, like caskets, with glass showcases in which on black velvet lay gold - bracelets, earrings, rings, chains - and in the half-dark it glowed of itself, as if from within. The masters sat at low tables, bent over their work, with loupes in their eyes, and did not raise their heads when buyers passed. They had no need to call. The gold called of itself.

Master Karapet's shop was at the very end of the row - the smallest and the darkest. Above the door hung a sign so old that the letters on it had worn away almost to nothing.

* * *

Karapet was old - so old that he seemed carved of the same dark wood as his work table. Grey eyebrows, thick as two bushes, overhung his eyes; from under them the eyes looked sharp, mocking, young. On his nose sat spectacles, and in his right eye a loupe as well, which made the right eye seem huge as an owl's. His hands - gnarled, in old scars from gravers - lay on the table motionless, like those of a man who knows the price of every movement.

Beside him on a stool sat a boy of about ten - his grandson, as I understood later - polishing a silver ring with a bit of suede, his tongue stuck out so intently that he did not notice us come in.

"Aram," said the old man, not raising his head. "Guests."

The boy jumped up, bowed. Akmal began quickly to speak, pointing at me. Karapet listened, then took the loupe from his eye and looked at me - long, searchingly.

And spoke - in broken but quite comprehensible language to me, with a heavy, sing-song accent:

"You - stranger. Suleyman agha sent? Good. Suleyman agha good man. Honest. Forty years ago sold me silk for my daughter's wedding and did not cheat by one cubit. Show what you brought."

I untied the pouch and poured the stones out onto the black velvet of his table.

Karapet did not touch them at once. He looked. For a long time. Then he fitted the loupe in his eye again, bent - so low that he almost touched the stones with his nose - and froze.

The silence stretched. Aram, forgetting the ring, stared at his grandfather with open mouth. Akmal shifted from foot to foot.

"Hm," said Karapet at last. He took the black stone with two fingers - carefully, as one takes a fledgling. Turned it. Then the white. Then laid both back and took off the loupe.

"Not agate," he said. "Not onyx. Not obsidian. Not marble. Not jasper." He bent his gnarled fingers. "Fifty years I hold stones. My father held. His father held. I know stones from Persia, from India, from Egypt, from the Urals, from Brazil. Such - I do not know."

"And the polish?"

"Polish..." Karapet shook his head. "Polish - not human. Man polishes - traces remain. Small, under loupe visible: back-forth, back-forth. Here - no traces. As if water rolled it a thousand years. But water rolls - stone round. And this one - not round. This one - as it should be." He paused. "As if someone said to the stone: be like this. And it became."

I told him about the warmth. About the dawn. About the pattern.

Karapet did not laugh. He looked at me over his spectacles - with a long, heavy look.

"Aram," he said, not taking his eyes from me. "A candle."

The boy brought a candle, lit it from a lamp. Karapet took the white stone with tongs - thin, jeweller's - and held it to the flame. He held it long. The stone, by all laws, should have blackened from soot. It did not blacken. Karapet held it. We all watched.

And suddenly - at first barely noticeable, then more distinct - on the smooth surface of the stone lines showed through. Thin, flowing, running into one another. The same tracery. The same as at dawn.

Aram gasped and sprang back. Akmal clutched my sleeve. Karapet did not stir - only his hand, holding the tongs, faintly trembled.

He took the stone away from the fire. The pattern lasted a few moments more - and melted like breath on glass.

The old man sat silent for a long while. Then he laid the stone back on the velvet, took off his spectacles, wiped them on the skirt of his robe and put them on again.

"My grandfather," he said at last quietly, "told me one story. When I was like Aram. About a man who came to this shop. Long ago. Very long ago. Grandfather was young then, and in the shop sat his father. The man came - a stranger, without a name, in strange clothes. Brought two stones. Black and white. Asked what they were. Great-grandfather heated the stone on a candle - and saw letters. No one could read."

My throat went dry.

"What became of that man?"

"I do not know." Karapet shrugged. "He left. Grandfather said - he said a strange thing, leaving. Said: 'I shall come once more. Perhaps not I, but I shall come.' Grandfather thought - madman. Not a few madmen in Istanbul." He paused. "I thought so too. Fifty years I thought so."

He pushed the stones toward me over the velvet - carefully, with two fingers.

"Take. Do not sell. Show to no one. Especially to those who will ask where from." And, after a pause, he added: "And a riddle I shall tell you. For the road. My grandfather said - that man left it. To great-grandfather. No one solved it."

He pronounced it slowly, with difficulty picking the words of a foreign tongue:

"'Two stones - day and night. One keeps, the other leads. Who holds both - he stands in doors. Where he looks - there home.'"

I repeated it to myself. Then again.

Who holds both - he stands in doors.

I remembered the black grille which I had never seen. No - did not remember: felt, as one remembers a smell from a childhood that did not exist. A door behind which another world lay. A threshold on which one stands, belonging to neither room. I shook my head, driving off this feeling - it had no relation to me, I was sure. Almost sure.

"What is the answer?" I asked.

"If I knew the answer," said Karapet, fitting the loupe in his eye again and bending over his work as though the talk were over, "I would not sit in the shop fifty years. Go, stranger. To Suleyman - a bow. And come when you have solved it. I am interested."

* * *

On the way back Akmal nevertheless brought me to the ships.

We came out to the gulf - to that very strip of water I had seen from the balcony - and it proved at close range not a strip at all but a whole world. Masts stood so thickly that between them the opposite shore could not be seen: tall sailing ships with furled sails, pot-bellied merchantmen, narrow rowers' boats darting to and fro among them like water-striders, and smoking steamers with enormous paddle wheels at their sides. Gulls cried. Boatmen cried. Porters cried, tossing sacks, bales and barrels from shoulder to shoulder. It smelled of fish, tar, salt water and coal.

Akmal raced along the quay like a dog let off the leash, showing me now one ship, now another, naming them - probably by flags, by names on the sides - and each time his face shone as though he were showing me his friends. Somewhere there, among these masts, I thought, ten years ago had stood the ship on which his father had gone to Crete. Akmal had been quite small then. He could not remember him. But he came here, I was sure, as Grandmother every evening sang her song: in order to wait.

At the very edge of the quay, on an upturned barrel, sat an old sailor mending a net, and, noticing my glance, asked Akmal something, nodding at me. Akmal answered. The sailor laughed and said something at which the boy blushed.

We walked home, and the pouch at my belt was cool and light, as befits a pouch with two ordinary stones. I kept my palm on it the whole way. Who holds both - he stands in doors. Where he looks - there home.

And where do I look, I thought. And where is my home.

At the gate Kemal was waiting for us. Seeing me, he silently held out a sheet of paper - folded in four, like that blank sheet that lay in my pocket. I unfolded it.

On the sheet in charcoal two doors were drawn. Side by side, wall to wall. One - open, the other - shut. And between them, on the threshold, with his back to the viewer - a man. Small, barely distinguishable. With a turban on his head.

And in each hand - a little circle. One black. The other white.

I raised my eyes to Kemal. He shrugged - quite like an adult - and pointed to the sky, as he had done yesterday. Then he laid his palms together under his cheek. Then he raised one finger.

Tonight. He had drawn this tonight. Sitting at my threshold.

I put the drawing in my pocket, with the note and the blank sheet. Now there were three.

The sun stood already high - it was nearing noon. From the house came Yusuf's voice calling the boys, and I went up to my balcony, deciding that after this morning I had a right simply to sit, thinking of nothing, and watch the city. I felt through the cloth the pouch at my belt - cooled, ordinary, unremarkable to the touch - and caught myself in the thought that for the first time in all this time I knew firmly at least one thing: whatever was happening to me in this city, in this body, in this life deprived of memory and name - these stones were not merely stones.

And I was not the first who had carried them in his pocket.
