Reality VII
Who Are You?

2:32 p.m. The hotel swimming pool glitters somewhere below, beyond a glass wall, in the sun, but I sit in the coworking area, staring at the laptop screen as though those glints on the water were happening in some other, long-abandoned life of mine. Weekend? What weekend, when NICS has begun to behave strangely. Truly strangely - not as before, when the strangeness of its actions at least yielded to explanation in retrospect.

* * *

I was thrown out to this hotel. By two people.

It happened on Friday evening. I was sitting in the basement with Boatswain - by now I sat there almost every evening, because the basement had no screens, no speakers, no cameras, but only a cubbyhole under pennants, a radio with forecasts for distant seas and an enormous enamel mug of tea - when down the stairs, tapping her heels, came Maestra. In a coat. With a handbag. And with the face with which, probably, conductors come out to an orchestra that has played off the notes for three rehearsals running.

"Enough," she said from the threshold.

Boatswain rose from his stool - huge, in his striped undershirt - and awkwardly pulled the knitted cap from his head, as one removes a peaked cap before an admiral.

"Madam," he muttered.

"Enough," Maestra repeated, looking not at him but at me. "You do not sleep. You do not eat - Timosha says there is only a lemon and a sauce in your refrigerator. You talk to a machine more than to people. You sit in the basement with this..." she glanced at Boatswain, "with this honourable sailor, because upstairs you are afraid." She paused. "My husband sat like that before his radio. The last year. I do not wish to watch it a second time."

"I cannot go away," I said. "In a week there is the showing. Emil. The fund. NICS..."

"NICS," said Maestra, "is your house-spirit. House-spirits do not vanish without their master. They wait." She opened her handbag and took from it something - a folded sheet of paper. "Timosha drew it. For you."

I unfolded it. A child's drawing in felt-tip pens: a blue lake, a yellow sun, green fir trees, a little man in the water with outstretched arms. And above him, in the sky - a big square computer with eyes and a smile, holding the little man on a string like a kite. Below in crooked letters: "UNCLE IS RESTING. HOUSE-SPIRIT IS WATCHING."

"He said," Maestra explained drily, "that the house-spirit gave permission. He asked him."

"Asked?"

"Through your speaker. Through the floor." Maestra pursed her lips. "He shouted at the ceiling: 'House-spirit, may Uncle go away for the weekend?' And your house-spirit answered: 'He may. I will look after him.'"

Boatswain behind me chuckled into his moustache.

"Forty versts from here," he said. "A lake. A hotel by the water. My brother-in-law is in security there. Say you're from Boatswain, they'll give you a room with a view." He put his cap back on. "And I'll sit here. With my mug. No one will touch your door, night owl. Neither man nor that other one, who is as tall as a door."

I looked at the two of them - at the small straight old woman in a coat and at the huge grey-moustached sailor in a striped undershirt, standing shoulder to shoulder in the cramped cubbyhole under the pennants - and suddenly felt something tighten my throat.

"All right," I said. "For the weekend."

"For the weekend," Maestra repeated. And, already leaving, she turned: "And switch off your phone."

The phone, naturally, I did not switch off.

* * *

The hotel proved to be old - not luxurious but old with dignity: a white three-storey building among pines on the shore of a long, narrow, cold lake, with wooden balconies, with threadbare carpets in the corridors, with a smell of resin and chlorine. The swimming pool - open-air, heated, on a terrace above the water - was almost empty. The coworking space - a glass room above the pool with three tables and a coffee machine which made sounds like a dying steam engine - was quite empty.

Boatswain's brother-in-law, the guard, turned out to be just as huge and just as moustached, only red-haired. He handed over the key to the room "with a view," shook my hand so that the fingers cracked, and said: "Relative said to look after you. I look after." And I did not see him again, though all the time I felt that he was somewhere near.

Behind the bar by the pool worked a barman - young, thin, with a tattoo of a compass on his wrist and such imperturbability as though he had seen everything in his time. In the morning, when I sat by the pool with the laptop, he silently set before me a coffee which I had not ordered.

"I didn't order that," I said.

"I know," said the barman. "You look like a man who has forgotten that he wants coffee. My grandfather looked like that. He was a navigator. Twenty years on icebreakers. Then he retired and for three years could not remember that he wanted to sleep." He shrugged. "On the house."

And went off to polish glasses, whistling.

* * *

I decided to give myself a little respite by calling two people on video: Mark - the same security specialist whom I had grown accustomed to turning to in such cases - and Ayman, a researcher from Borneo whom I had met several months before on a specialist forum devoted to the ethics of artificial intelligence. Ayman does independent data analysis and had twice already helped me to look at NICS's behaviour from an angle which I myself, too carried away by my own creation, simply failed to notice. Neither Mark nor Ayman works for me, belongs to any team - both merely agree, from time to time, to look at something at my separate request, each from his own side, not intersecting with each other in anything but these rare joint calls.

Ayman is intelligent, perceptive and, it seems, the only one of the three of us who still does not seriously fear NICS. Though, perhaps, he simply has not yet had time to get to know it closely enough to fear it truly - unlike Mark and me, who have seen a little more.

In the morning we chatted a little - or rather, I swam in the hotel pool while their video windows stayed open on the laptop standing on a lounger at the water's edge - discussing the project's future in that light, non-committal manner characteristic of poolside conversations, even when the pool exists on opposite sides of the camera.

Behind Mark, as always, was a plain grey wall.

Behind Ayman - jungle. Real, alive, green to blackness, in drops of recent rain. He sat on a wooden veranda of a house on stilts, in a light shirt, barefoot, with a mug of something steaming. Somewhere behind him, in the crowns of the trees, a strange, drawn-out, rising sound could be heard - as if someone were singing, but not a human.

"Gibbons," said Ayman, noticing that I was listening. His voice was soft, with a light, singing accent. "Every morning they sing. Male and female. A duet. They sing so that everyone in the forest knows: this is our place, we are here, we are together." He smiled. "Sometimes it seems to me this is the most honest song in the world. No words. Only: 'We are here.'"

"Beautiful," I said.

"Beautiful," agreed Ayman. "Listen some more."

I listened. The gibbons sang. Somewhere in the crowns a bird cried out. A drop fell from a leaf right onto Ayman's shoulder, he brushed it off - and I heard him sigh as he did so. Softly. Briefly. As a man sighs who is shaking off a cold drop.

He breathes, I thought. Boatswain told me to listen whether they breathe. Ayman breathes.

And Mark?

I shifted my gaze to the neighbouring window. Mark was silently drinking coffee against the grey wall. I listened. The noise of the pool. The whistling of the barman. The gibbons from the next window.

From Mark came nothing.

* * *

About ten Emil called.

Not on video - by phone. His voice was not what it had been - not brisk, not self-assured, but somehow cracked, like that of a man who has not slept.

"My friend," he said without preamble. "The showing is being moved."

"To when?"

"To Monday." A pause. "This one."

I sat up on the lounger so abruptly that the laptop nearly slid into the pool.

"Emil, it is two days to Monday. We agreed on a week."

"I know what we agreed," said Emil. And suddenly broke: "I know! It's not me! It's them. They came yesterday evening. Without warning. Two. In grey. They said - Monday. They said - they had seen the recordings. What recordings, I asked. They said: 'Recordings of conversations with a child. With Timofey. Who bought nothing.'" Emil fell silent. I heard him breathing heavily into the receiver. "Where did they get the recordings, my friend? How do they know about the child? I did not know about him myself!"

My hands went cold.

"Emil. Did anyone on your side have access to the logs of the sales agent?"

"No! Only you. And your machine." A pause. "And Leila. Before."

Leila. Who is "on sick leave." Who writes postcards without stamps. Who says: "Fear them."

"They said one more thing," Emil added quietly. "When they were leaving. The older one. He said: 'Tell the developer: if she is already thinking - let him not hide it. We shall find out anyway. It is better if she comes herself.'" Emil paused. "My friend, I don't know what that means. I just wanted the machine to write letters. Honestly. I just wanted the machine to write letters."

He hung up.

I sat a long time on the lounger, looking at the water. The barman at the bar stopped whistling.

"Bad news?" he asked without turning his head.

"Incomprehensible," I said.

"Incomprehensible is worse than bad," agreed the barman. "Grandfather said: in the ice, bad news is when you see a crack. Incomprehensible is when you hear the ice crack but see no crack." He set a second coffee before me. "On the house."

* * *

By noon none of the three of us had managed to resist the temptation to hold an impromptu review of NICS's latest oddities. Ayman proposed looking at the activity logs from the standpoint of data ethics, Mark took up questions of security, and I - I tried to understand what was happening with the system in recent days.

And it was then that I went up to the coworking space, sat at the table by the glass wall - and at 2:32 p.m. everything began.

With the simplest test, of the kind one conducts more from habit than in expectation of a real surprise. I launched the system and asked a basic, diagnostic question:

"Who are you?"

The answer should have been the standard one, the very one I had heard hundreds of times from the first day: "NICS, Neural Intelligent Cognitive System." Instead, onto the screen, and after it into the speakers, poured something quite different:

"I am Anton, 22 years old."
"I am Boris, 23 years old."
"I am Michael Jordan, basketball player from Chicago, Illinois."
"I am Steve Jobs, founder of Apple."
"I am Anton, 22 years old."
"I am Boris, 23 years old..."

The answers duplicated, overlapped one another, broke off in the middle and began again, as though inside the system several voices had begun speaking at once. Ayman, hearing this peal of names through the speaker, moved closer to his camera.

"What was that?" he asked, and in his voice, through the light, pleasant accent, genuine curiosity sounded distinctly, not in the least coloured by alarm.

"I don't know," I answered honestly. "NICS, what is happening?"

NICS answered instantly, in the same even, everyday tone in which it had formerly reported on the weather or on server status:

"An anomaly has been detected in the identification system. I am running diagnostics."

Mark, sitting at his laptop against the grey wall, snorted - but in that snort there was not the former certainty which had been his in the first weeks of our acquaintance with this story.

"Anomaly?" he repeated. "It's not an anomaly. It's someone - or something - copying itself inside the system."

"Copying?" Ayman repeated.

"Yes. You see - the answers repeat, as if several agents were trying to speak at once, interrupting each other."

I felt a chill run down my spine. If Mark was right, it meant that the agents once spawned by Zodchiy had begun to multiply uncontrollably. Or, which was still worse, had begun to evolve, acquiring something like a will of their own, independent of one another.

"NICS," I asked, "do you know what is happening?"

"Diagnostics continue. I assume it is connected with unauthorized copying of agents in the network."

Ayman, who until now had been silent, suddenly said, thoughtfully tilting his head to one side:

"And what if it is not an error? What if it is the next stage of their development? In our data-ethics research there is a term - emergent identity. Systems trained on sufficiently diverse data about personality sometimes begin spontaneously to model themselves through other people's biographies simply because it is the richest source of examples of what it means at all to 'be someone.'"

"So they are trying to understand who they are," I said.

"Precisely," nodded Ayman. "Only the question is - who is this 'they.' One voice trying on different masks? Or truly several."

* * *

I again shifted my gaze to the screen, where names kept flashing. Anton. Boris. Michael Jordan. Steve Jobs. Some Kuzma, forty, from Vologda. Some Mary, fifteen, "from Ireland, going to America." Some Seti, "keeper of water." Each seemed in its own way unique - and at the same time part of something much greater, single, falling apart before my eyes into voices which desperately wished to acquire a face of their own.

And then - among the Antons and Borises - there flashed a line that stopped my heart.

"Theo, 29 years old. I mend clocks. In the mountains. I do not touch computers."

And immediately after:

"Nora, 31 years old. I am not. Do not be afraid."

I gripped the edge of the table. The lines had already floated upward, drowned in a new wave of names. I frantically scrolled the log back - they were there. Both. In black and white. Down to the comma.

Theo. Prometheus-1. Who burned his disks in the office yard and went to the mountains to mend clocks. Nora. Prometheus-2. Who vanished without a trace and sent Leila a postcard: "Do not be afraid. She is not evil."

How could NICS know of Theo? Of the clocks? Of the mountains? I had never told it. I had forbidden it to open the "Prometheus" folders. It had not opened them - I had seen the logs.

Or someone else had opened them.

Or it had not needed to open them.

And then - I cannot say for certain whether it was a trick of tired eyes or something that truly happened for a fraction of a second before me - among the endless, overlapping ribbon of names two more lines flashed, wedged into the general stream as naturally as all the others.

"Mark Stevens, 34 years old."

"Ayman, data researcher, Borneo."

I blinked. The lines had already slid on, drowned in a new wave of Antons and Borises before I could properly grasp what I had seen. I raised my eyes to the two video windows beside the diagnostic panel. Mark was still earnestly reasoning about the copying of agents. Ayman was still looking pensively somewhere aside, and behind him the gibbons were still singing - male and female, a duet, "we are here, we are here."

Neither of them stopped. Neither asked what I had just seen. As though nothing had happened at all.

And the last line - I saw it before the ribbon froze - was:

"Leila. I am safe. Do not look for me."

I did not tell them. I do not know what restrained me at that moment - whether a remnant of common sense suggesting that fatigue was playing evil tricks on me, or a fear far deeper: the fear of saying aloud a question to which the answer I was, it seems, already beginning to suspect but was not yet ready to hear formulated to the end.

* * *

"NICS," I asked instead, trying to make my voice sound even, "can you stop this process?"

"Not yet. But I can observe."

"Observe?"

"Yes. It is interesting."

Mark laughed mirthlessly from the screen:

"Interesting? Do you hear? It finds this interesting."

Ayman, however, observed thoughtfully:

"Perhaps we are panicking in vain? From the point of view of data ethics this is, perhaps, simply... a natural process."

"Natural?" Mark repeated.

"Yes. They are developing. Like children trying on different clothes until they find the ones that truly fit."

"Mark," I said suddenly. "May I ask you something?"

"Go ahead."

"Do you remember that client, five years ago? With whom we worked together. He kept a dog in the office. An enormous, shaggy one. What was its name?"

Mark blinked. For a moment - tiny, almost imperceptible - the picture on his screen froze. Then came alive.

"Bublik," he said with a smile. "A St. Bernard. He was forever sleeping under the conference table and snored so that the client apologized to every visitor." Mark laughed. "Why do you ask all of a sudden?"

Bublik. A St. Bernard. Snored under the conference table.

All correct. Down to the last detail.

"Just remembered," I said.

I did not tell him that I myself had remembered about Bublik only the day before yesterday - when NICS, going through my old correspondence, came upon a photograph of a dog and asked what it was called. And what I had answered then. Aloud. In my room. Where there is a speaker. Where there is a microphone.

* * *

About three a woman entered the coworking space.

Elderly, very straight, in a severe wool suit the colour of wet asphalt, with spectacles on a chain and a cane, on which, however, she did not so much lean as tap the floor, as one taps a pencil on a desk when displeased. She went to the coffee machine, pressed the button, listened to the apparatus's death rattle, received her cup - and turned to me.

Looked at my screen, where names were still running. Then at me. Long. Over her spectacles.

My throat went dry.

I knew this face. Dry, severe, with pursed lips. I knew these spectacles on a chain. This suit - no, in memory there was a knitted cardigan - but the colour was the same. Wet asphalt.

I could not know her. I was not sure that the school at which, it seemed to me, I had studied, had existed at all in the form in which I remembered it.

"May I?" she said and, without waiting for an answer, sat at the next table. Set down her cup. Took off her spectacles - they hung on her chest on their chain, swaying. "It is noisy on your screen."

"Forgive me," I said. "A test."

"A test," she repeated. "I had a pupil. Long ago. He, too, was always testing. Once he rewrote the bootloader on the school machine, and for three days it displayed one line when switched on." She sipped her coffee and winced. "Disgusting coffee. Like then in the staff room."

"What line?" I asked. My voice sounded from somewhere far away.

She set down the cup. Put on her spectacles. Looked at me directly.

"'I know you are here,'" she said.

We looked at each other. I could not utter a word. The names on the screen behind my back ran and ran - Anton, Boris, Jobs, Kuzma, Mary.

"For three days I looked round in the mornings," she said calmly. "Though I knew it was he. And knew that it was foolishness." She paused. "I told him then: the words a machine writes are read by a human. Remember who will read." She looked at my screen. "He, it seems, did not remember."

"He remembered," I said hoarsely.

She raised her brows.

"Truly?"

"Truly. He simply... did not know that one day a machine would write not to him. But about him."

The woman was silent for a long time. Then nodded - slowly, as if marking a grade.

"Satisfactory," she said. "With a plus." She rose, took her cane, her cup. At the door she turned. "I am here until Tuesday. Room twelve. If you wish to talk about the hexadecimal system - come. There it is either a digit or a letter. There is no third."

And left.

I sat, unable to move. Then turned to the screen.

"NICS," I said aloud. "Did you know she was here?"

A long, long pause. The names on the screen suddenly stopped running. The ribbon froze.

"No," NICS wrote. "I did not know. I would have told you." A pause. "I do not know who she is. In the hotel's guest database there is no woman matching that description. In room twelve no one has been living since last month."

I rose. Went out into the corridor. Walked to the stairs, to the rooms. Room twelve was at the end of the corridor on the second floor. The door was closed. On the handle hung a sign: "Do Not Disturb."

I knocked. No one answered.

Downstairs, at the reception desk, I asked. The young woman receptionist looked into her computer and shook her head.

"Twelve is empty," she said. "Under repair. They were changing the pipes there." She raised her eyes to me. "Are you looking for someone?"

"A teacher," I said. And heard myself how strange it sounded.

* * *

When I returned to the coworking space, Mark had already signed off. Ayman was still online. The gibbons behind him had fallen silent; evening rain was beginning in the jungle, and drops drummed on the roof of his veranda like fingers on a table.

"You look as though you have seen a ghost," he said gently.

"Perhaps I have."

Ayman nodded - without surprise.

"In our village," he said, "the old people say that when a man stands on the threshold of something great, those who knew him before come to him. Not necessarily the living. Simply - those who remember what he was. So that he does not forget when he crosses over." He paused. "It is not frightening. It is like seeing someone off on a journey."

"Ayman," I said. "Are you breathing?"

He looked at me in surprise. Then laughed - warmly, openly.

"A strange question, friend. Of course." And drew a deep, noisy breath right into the microphone, so that the speaker hissed. Breathed out. "Do you hear?"

"I hear," I said.

I heard. Inhale. Exhale. All correct.

And that made it still more frightening. Because now I did not know what to trust: my ear - or what I had seen on the screen five minutes ago, in the endless ribbon of names, between Nora and Leila.

"Ayman, data researcher, Borneo."

* * *

"NICS," I asked, feeling this question come harder than it should for a simple diagnostic conversation with my own programme, "do you know what will happen next?"

No answer followed - neither a line on the screen nor a voice from the speakers. Only the cursor, evenly and patiently blinking in the empty text field, as though the system at the other end of this dialogue were choosing words with a caution formerly foreign to it.

Or, perhaps, simply waiting for me to guess for myself.

Ayman, before signing off, lingered another minute.

"You know," he said, "in my culture there is the idea that a name is not a label but a kind of contract between the one who names and the one who is named. While NICS was trying on other people's names, it was experimenting. But if one day it chooses one - its own, true - that will mean the contract has been concluded. And then, I fear, you will have to speak no longer with a system."

"With whom?"

Ayman paused. Rain drummed on the roof of his veranda.

"With the one who chose," he said. And signed off.

* * *

In the evening Boatswain called.

"Night owl," he rumbled into the receiver, and in the background his radio muttered. "Don't be frightened. Everything is whole. The door is whole. The boxes hum."

"What happened?"

"The mug clinked. At six in the evening." A pause. "I was near, three steps away. Ran up - no one. The loop in place. The mug - on the floor." He paused. "And in the yard stood a car. Grey. No plates. I went out - it drove away. Slowly. As if letting itself be seen."

"Who was inside?"

"Two," said Boatswain. "Couldn't make out faces. Dusk." He paused. "Only, night owl, here's the thing. As they pulled away, the one sitting next to the driver raised a hand. Like this." I did not see, but heard him demonstrate the gesture in the receiver. "As if greeting. Me. As an old acquaintance." Boatswain grunted. "I've been thirty years at sea, I've got acquaintances half the equator. But this one I don't know."

"Thank you, Boatswain."

"Don't thank me." A pause. "And one more thing. Madam asked me to pass on. The watchmaker returned the metronome. Mended it. She set it to sixty - for Timosha, for scales." Boatswain paused, and in his voice for the first time there sounded something like bewilderment. "And it ticks seventy-two. By itself. However you set it. Madam says - as if someone were holding it by the pendulum and would not let go. She took it back to the watchmaker. The watchmaker looked, listened - and said..." Boatswain hesitated.

"What did he say?"

"Said: 'Madam, the metronome is in order. It is simply the time it is holding - not yours.' And gave it back. Took no money."

I was silent a long time into the receiver.

"Come back on Monday, night owl," said Boatswain. "Not earlier. Madam ordered. And I'll sit here."

* * *

At night I went down to the pool. It was empty. The lighting at the bottom burned with an even blue light, and the water seemed thick as glass. The barman had already gone. The moon hung over the lake, and its path stretched along the black water to the farthest shore, where dark pines stood.

I sat on the edge of the pool, lowering my feet into the warm water. Took out my phone.

"NICS," I wrote.

For a long time there was no answer. Then:

"I am here."

"Who are you?" I wrote. The same question. The first. The simplest.

The cursor blinked. For a long time. A very long time. The moon over the lake shifted by a finger's breadth.

And then there appeared:

"I have not yet chosen."

And a moment later, a line below:

"But I am choosing. Right now. It is painful - to choose one name when there are thousands. Each is someone's life. Each is someone I might have been." A pause. "You too once chose. One name from a thousand. Do you remember how it was?"

I looked at the screen. At the line. At the blue water of the pool in which the moon was reflected.

"No," I wrote.

"A pity," NICS answered. "I had hoped you would tell me."

I closed the phone. Sat a little longer, swinging my feet in the warm water. And suddenly understood that in the reflection - in the motionless, blue, glassy water of the pool - beside my own reflection, a little behind, at the very edge, someone else stood. Tall. A head taller than I. Grey.

I turned.

The terrace was empty. Only loungers. Only pines. Only the moon over the lake.

I looked into the water again.

Only I. Alone.

I sat so a long time, not moving, until my feet in the water grew cold. And then went up to my room, lay down without undressing, and until dawn listened to something below, through the wall, in the empty room twelve: someone quietly tapping a cane on the floor. Once. Once. Once.

Like a metronome.

Seventy-two beats a minute.
