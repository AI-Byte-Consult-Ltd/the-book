Vision I
Luxor

We were walking — this I remember with perfect clarity, though everything else about that day grows hazier each time I try to recall it, as if my memory had agreed to keep one single detail in exchange for the whole. We were walking.

But before that — before that, there was the river.

I remember it the way one remembers the face of a dying man: not whole, but feature by feature, each more painful than the last. The Nile, which at this season ought to have been full and heavy, turbid with the silt that brings life to the fields, flowed in a narrow, weary ribbon down the middle of its immeasurably wide bed. Its banks lay bare and cracked on either side, like old ribs — grey shoals strewn with abandoned boats bleached by the sun, and herons standing knee-deep in the mud, too emaciated to take wing at our approach.

And down this dying river sailed a god.

A golden barque, shining so brightly that it hurt to look at it, glided ahead, drawn on ropes by two dozen oarsmen in the smaller boats. On the barque, beneath a canopy of the finest linen, in a closed shrine like a carved ark, stood a statue — I did not see it, no one saw it but the initiated — yet I knew it was there, as one knows of a heart one cannot see. After it came the barque of the queen of the gods, and after hers the barque of their son, and only then, a little apart, as is fitting for a mortal, though a living god — mine.

A festival. I remember that it was a festival. I remember the music — it drifted from the banks, from somewhere behind the palms, where crowds, in spite of the hunger, in spite of the drought, had gathered to watch the god sail to his southern home. Tambourines. The rattle of sistra, jingling like scattered silver. Flutes spinning out something high and thin, almost plaintive. And above it all, the singing of women, long and drawn out, now merging into a single voice, now breaking apart into hundreds.

"My Pharaoh," said the helmsman of my barque, an old man with a face like sycamore bark, leaning toward me so that the rowers should not hear. "Allow me to speak a word that, perhaps, ought not to be spoken."

"Speak," I answered. I remember that I answered precisely so. I remember my own voice — even, calm, as a Pharaoh's voice should be at a festival.

"I have steered barques on this river since the day my beard began to grow," said the helmsman. "And never once have I seen a god travel south on water so shallow. Twice this morning the god's barque ran aground on a shoal. Twice the rowers hauled it free with their hands, waist-deep in the silt." He fell silent for a moment, looking at the golden radiance ahead. "The old men among us say: if the god does not wish to go, he should not be dragged."

"And if the god wishes it, but the river will not let him?" I asked.

The helmsman looked at me for a long time, and in his faded eyes there flickered something I did not then understand, and which now, years and lives later, I understand all too well: pity.

"Then, my Pharaoh," he said quietly, "one must ask oneself whose will it is — the god's, or that of those who hold the ropes."

He did not say another word until we reached Luxor.

* * *

I remember the landing. I remember how the barque nosed against the stone steps that led down to the water — steps which in good years went almost entirely under, and which now stood exposed to their very foundations, covered in green slime and dried shells. I remember the crowd on the bank — thousands of faces turned toward me, thousands of hands reaching upward. Hungry faces. Wasted hands. People cried out, threw themselves on their faces, held out their children to me — so that the Pharaoh's shadow might fall on them and bring good fortune.

I remember that I walked through that crowd along a living corridor cleared before me by the guard — and that this guard was not the usual one. The faces were strangers'. The shields were the same, the spears the same, but the faces were strangers'. I remember that I noticed this and paid it no heed — or paid it heed, but decided that in the bustle of the festival the detachments had got mixed up, as everything gets mixed up at a festival.

From the landing to the temple ran an avenue of sphinxes — long and straight as a drawn bowstring, receding into the distance between two endless rows of stone beasts with the bodies of lions and the heads of rams. They lay on their plinths with their paws drawn in, and gazed at one another across the road — for thousands of years they had gazed, unblinking, as sentinels gaze who need neither sleep nor food nor to ask questions. Between the forepaws of each stood a small figure — a king, guarded by the beast — and in the slanting evening light it seemed that the beasts were pressing those small kings to their breasts, as mothers press their children.

The crowd pressed in on both sides of the avenue, behind the backs of the sphinxes, and at one moment a woman broke through the guard. Thin, in a sun-faded dress, with a child in her arms — a boy of about three, motionless, with closed eyes and dry, cracked lips. A guardsman swung the shaft of his spear at her, but I raised my hand, and he froze.

"My Pharaoh," the woman whispered, falling to her knees directly on the scorching flagstones. "He has not opened his eyes for three days. The healer said that only a god could help. Or you."

I remember how I looked at the child. I remember his lashes — long, still — and the thin vein in his neck, beating so faintly that it was barely visible. I knew that I was not a god. I knew it as clearly as I knew that I must do what was expected of me. I bent down and touched his forehead with my palm.

The forehead was hot. The child did not open his eyes.

"Take him to the priests of the House of Life," I said to the woman. "Tell them the Pharaoh commanded that he be treated as the children of the palace are treated. Tell them I shall ask after him tomorrow."

Tomorrow. I remember how I spoke that word — and I remember that even then, at that very instant, something within me said, quietly and distinctly: there will be no tomorrow. I drove the thought away. The woman crawled back into the crowd, pressing the child to her breast and repeating my name like a prayer. I never learned what became of the boy.

Somewhere in the crowd, at the foot of one of the sphinxes, sat a blind singer with a small harp on his knees. He plucked the strings and sang, in an aged, cracked, yet strangely pure voice, a song that seemed to be known to everyone around, for people took up individual lines:

"They came down from the Heavens when the earth was empty,
They kindled fire, and the water burst into flame,
They left us bread, and the Nile, and death —
And promised to return, that they might wipe all away."

I remember how I stopped for a moment upon hearing the last line. I remember how the singer, as if sensing my gaze, turned his sightless face toward me and smiled — toothlessly, serenely, as old men smile who have long ceased to be afraid. I remember that I wanted to ask him where the song came from. And I remember that the guard swept me onward, and the question remained unasked.

Beside me walked a man I knew. An old warrior, stocky, his face scored with scars — the commander of my army. His name in this dream slips away from me like a fish from the hand, though I know that I knew it, that I spoke it hundreds of times, that I trusted him more than anyone at court. He walked at my right hand, and his palm lay on the hilt of his sword — not on the belt, as is proper at a festival, but on the very hilt.

"I do not like this guard, my Pharaoh," he said under his breath, without turning his head. "Two of the commanders I do not know at all. Their detachments were supposed to have remained in the capital."

"A festival," I answered. "At a festival everything gets muddled."

"At a festival," he said, "men muddle what they wish to muddle."

I remember those words. I remember how they settled over me like cold — and how I drove that cold away, because a Pharaoh cannot be afraid in the sight of thousands.

At the temple gates we were met. The enormous pylons, two stone wings, rose before us into the sky, painted with scenes of the victories of long-dead kings, and before them, on either side of the entrance, sat stone giants — my predecessors, or ancestors, or those who were called ancestors — gazing over our heads at the river with that expression of eternal, unruffled calm which belongs only to stone. Before the pylons rose an obelisk — slender, sharp, covered with signs from top to bottom — and its shadow, long as a spear, lay across the square, its point aimed directly at the gates.

At the gates stood priests. Many priests. White robes, shaven heads gleaming in the sunset light, incense smoking in bronze bowls on long handles. And at their head — small, rotund, with a head smooth as polished stone — a priest whose face I remember better than I would wish. He bowed before me lower than all the others had bowed — so low that it resembled mockery.

"Peace be upon you, my Pharaoh," he said, in a voice soft and oily as wax. "The High Priest awaits you in the inner court. The god has arrived at his southern home, and the hour has come when the Pharaoh must appear before him — alone, as the First Ones appeared."

"Alone?" repeated the commander of the army, and steel rang in his voice.

"Only the initiated, honoured sir," replied the rotund priest, without so much as glancing at him. "Such is the custom. For thousands of years. Warriors have no place in the inner court of the god — neither armed nor unarmed."

"My Pharaoh…" The commander turned to me, and for the first time I saw in his face what I had never seen there before: fear. Not for himself. For me. "Allow me to go with you. At least as far as the colonnade. At least as far as the first court."

I remember how long I looked at him. I remember that I wanted to say "yes." I remember that behind him, in the crowd, someone was watching me — an old man leaning on a staff, grey-haired, heavy-set — and shaking his head, slowly, from side to side, as though trying to tell me something without words. I do not know who he was. In this dream I do not remember his name. But I remember his eyes, and I remember that they held the same thing as the eyes of the commander of the army.

"Wait for me here," I said. "At the gates. If I do not come out before moonrise…"

I did not finish. I do not remember why I did not finish. I remember only that the commander of the army clenched the hilt of his sword until his knuckles whitened, and bowed his head — heavily, slowly, as one bows one's head before a sentence.

"I shall be here, my Pharaoh," he said. "Until moonrise. And after."

* * *

Beyond the gates lay the first court, and in it were people whose faces I remember only in snatches, like flashes of lightning above a night river.

I remember a young man in costly garments, with bracelets on both arms, standing by a column with the air of one who does not belong here and knows it. As I passed, his bracelets chimed — he hastily pressed his arm to his side to muffle the sound, and looked away. He did not bow. Or he bowed, but too late. I remember only the chime — thin, melodious, out of place, like laughter at a funeral.

I remember a girl in the robes of a junior priestess — very young, with enormous dark eyes ringed in black paint. She stood among the singers who held the sistra, and she was not singing. She was looking at me. When our eyes met, she shook her head almost imperceptibly — once, twice — and her lips soundlessly formed a single word. I did not catch it. But I knew what word it was. I knew it as one knows in dreams things that cannot possibly be known.

"Do not go."

The senior priestess beside her, noticing this, jerked her sharply by the arm, and the girl was led away somewhere into the depths, behind the columns, before I could stop. I remember that I wanted to stop. I remember that I did not.

And I remember a young man. Young, slender, dark-eyed, in the plain linen dress of a scribe. He was trying to make his way to me through the crowd of priests — squeezing between the white robes, working his elbows, and his face was twisted with the despair known only to those who are late, and know they are late. He was shouting something — I saw his lips moving — but in the din of sistra, tambourines and singing I could make out no sound. Two priests intercepted him, took him by the arms and drew him back, gently, almost tenderly, as one draws a child away from a fire. He turned to me once more — and tears stood in his eyes.

I remember that look. Of everything that happened that day, I think I remember it most clearly of all. I do not know why. I do not even know who he was. But somehow, recalling that court, I feel neither fear nor cold — but a sharp, almost unbearable sorrow, as though in that moment something had been taken from me that I had never had time to call my own.

* * *

The High Priest awaited me in the inner court, by the altar, where incense smoked in bronze bowls — thick, sweet, enough to make the head spin.

He was tall, straight as the obelisk at the gates, and no longer young, though age seemed to have no power over his bearing. His robe was whiter than all the others, and on his chest hung a broad collar bearing the image of the sacred Eye, which seemed to look at me together with his own eyes.

"My Pharaoh," he said, and bowed exactly as far as custom required — not a finger's breadth lower. "The god has arrived at his southern home. I thank you for having escorted him."

"I escort the god every year," I answered. "As my predecessors did."

"As the First Ones did," he corrected gently. "Yes. But this year everything is different. This year the river did not wish to let the god go south. You saw it yourself, my Pharaoh. Twice the barque ran aground."

"I saw."

"The gods speak to us in signs," continued the High Priest, slowly circling the altar, the incense smoke curling about him as if alive. "Drought is a sign. A shallow river is a sign. Famine is a sign. The drought will not relent, my Pharaoh, until the scales are set right anew. For too long one pan has outweighed the other."

"And whose pan, in your opinion, has outweighed?" I asked, though the answer was clear to me in advance, as the answer is clear to a question asked not for the sake of an answer, but to hear whether the other will dare to utter it aloud.

He did not dare. Instead of answering he smiled — with that indulgent, patient smile known only to those who are certain that time works for them and not for you.

"The First Ones, who descended from the Heavens," he said instead, "left us not only the Epic. They left us something else. That which the Pharaoh holds in his hand when he stands before the god in his southern home. That which no Pharaoh has held in his hands for many generations — for none of them was worthy."

He made a sign, and from behind a column came a priest — the same one, rotund and oily — bearing before him a casket of black wood, bound at the corners with gold. He set the casket on the edge of the altar and withdrew.

The High Priest lifted the lid.

Inside, on a cushion of faded red linen, lay two stones. One black, one white. Smooth as river pebbles, yet perfect — so perfect that they seemed polished not by human hands but by time itself, for hundreds upon hundreds of years. They did not gleam. They seemed to drink in the light of the torches without reflecting it.

"Take them, my Pharaoh," said the High Priest. "So the First Ones did. Take them — and the gods will see who you are."

I remember how I stretched out my hand. I remember how I touched them — and how something passed through my fingers, from fingertips to wrist, that I can call neither warmth nor cold nor pain — something like recognition, as though I had held them before, long ago, in a hand that was mine and yet was not. I remember that I closed my palm around them and did not wish to let them go.

And I remember the High Priest's face at that moment — no, at that instant, for in those days they knew no minutes, but my memory, it seems, muddles the times. He was looking at my palm clenched around the stones, and on his face was an expression I had never seen before in all the years: not triumph, not gloating, but hunger. A naked, ancient hunger, such as belongs to one who has waited too long.

"Come, my Pharaoh," he said. "There is something you must see with your own eyes before we continue this conversation."

And we went.

* * *

I do not remember how long that journey lasted — an instant, an eternity — but I remember distinctly the gigantic columns of Luxor rising before us one after another like a petrified forest, each trunk broader than the arms of ten men could encircle. Their capitals, open like papyrus buds frozen in the eternal blossoming of stone, were lost somewhere in the darkening heights, from which not a single ray of sunset light fell upon us any longer — up there, only night held sway, having descended sooner than it should have descended upon the earth beneath our feet.

Evening had already fallen on Luxor, but in the west the sky still held a narrow band of dull copper light — that peculiar shade seen only here, above the Nile, when the day does not die at once but reluctantly, clinging to the horizon with its last glimmers, as though even the sun were loath to leave this land.

I was the Pharaoh. I knew it as firmly as I knew the weight of the double crown upon my head — and yet in that knowledge there was not a drop of what might be called power. I was surrounded on all sides: priests walked to the left, to the right, behind me, and only ahead, where we were heading, was it empty — frighteningly, deliberately empty, as it is empty before the place to which you are led without being asked for your consent.

The music had been left behind. Sistra, tambourines, flutes, the singing of women — all of it had fallen silent, cut off by the walls as a thread is cut with a knife. Here, between the columns, it was quiet. So quiet that I could hear the shuffle of the priests' bare feet on the stone flags, the crackle of the torches, and my own breathing — uneven, alien, as though it were not I who breathed, but someone within me.

Between the columns the air was different — thicker, heavier, smelling of torch resin and of something else for which I could not then find a name, and which now, in remembering, I do not dare to name again.

"Tell me, High Priest," I said, and my voice sounded unexpectedly loud in that silence, rebounding from the columns and returning to me as an echo. "What is it that I must see?"

"The truth, my Pharaoh," he answered, without turning. He walked ahead, a little to one side, and the torch in the hand of the priest beside him cast trembling russet glints on his white robe. "The truth that the First Ones knew and their descendants forgot. That the Pharaoh is only a vessel. That the power he holds in his hand never belonged to him. That he merely keeps it — until the appointed time."

Until the appointed time. I remember how those words answered within me with a strange, inexplicable echo — as though I had heard them before, as though I should hear them again, in another place, at another time, in another tongue.

"And when the appointed time runs out?" I asked.

The High Priest at last turned. His face in the torchlight was calm — frighteningly calm.

"Then the power returns to the place from which it came, my Pharaoh. To those who are worthy to keep it."

The priests' steps, until then resounding and confident, grew shorter. I did not understand why at once — and when I understood, it was already too late to understand anything at all: they were not walking beside me, they were encircling me, closing in tighter with every ten paces, as water closes behind the stern of a boat which that same water will in the end swallow up.

I remember that I looked back. I remember the gates far behind, beyond the forest of columns — a tiny rectangle of sunset sky in which, it seemed to me, stood a stocky silhouette with a sword at its belt. He was waiting. He had promised to wait until moonrise. The moon had not yet risen.

The High Priest was the first to halt — between two columns so enormous that in their shade vanished not only my shadow but, it seemed, all that remained of the evening light which had still been clinging to the horizon a moment before.

"Here," he said quietly, almost tenderly.

I wanted to ask — what is here, why here — but I did not manage to utter a word. Whether someone behind my back turned too sharply for a man in a peaceful procession, or the flame of a torch held by one of the priests on my right wavered, casting upon the column's stone a shadow all too like a raised hand, or whether it merely seemed so to me — I cannot say for certain even now, years and, it seems, lives later.

I remember cold. Not the evening cold that descends upon the Valley with the darkness, but another — sharp, concentrated somewhere on the left, beneath the ribs — and the two stones, black and white, suddenly grown heavier in my palm, as if they had absorbed all this cold at once, to keep it from reaching my heart.

I remember that they glowed. Very faintly. Like embers beneath ash.

I do not remember whether anyone struck me. I do not remember whether I cried out. I do not even remember whether I fell — or whether it simply grew dark faster than it should have grown dark even here, among the gigantic columns that so securely hide the sky from the earth.

I remember only one thing — the last, before everything vanished. Far away, beyond the columns, at the gates, someone cried out. A deep, hoarse, desperate cry of a warrior who has realised he is too late. I recognised that voice. I knew that voice. I could not recall the name of the one to whom it belonged — but I knew that he was crying out mine.

And then — darkness. Complete, soundless, without edge or bottom — a darkness into which not even the echo of footsteps reaches, footsteps that only a moment before had seemed to me the most important sound in the world.

* * *

I opened my eyes — and found that I was standing in the middle of a sunlit street, with a scrap of paper in my hand, two paces from old men playing backgammon on a motley carpet.

My heart pounded as though I had truly just been running — or had just died. I raised my free hand to my left side. No wound, no pain. Nothing but the memory of cold, too distinct to be merely a dream — and a question to which I had no answer: had what happened between those columns already happened to me, or had I just seen how it would happen?

I opened my other hand — the one that had been clenched in a fist, I know not since when. Two stones, black and white, lay in it — cool, smooth, silent. Perfectly ordinary. The same as I had found them in my pocket that morning.

I did not know where they had come from. But looking at them, for the first time in all that long, strange morning, I was afraid not that I did not remember the answer — but that somewhere deep within myself, it seemed, I already knew it.
