Vision VI
The Valley and the Tower

I do not know how many years I lived in the Tower.

That is the first thing worth saying, because time there did not pass as it does elsewhere. It did not flow like a river, nor tick like a clock. It grew. Like grass. Slowly, imperceptibly, stalk by stalk. Spring came to the Valley, and I watched it from the window. Summer filled with gold, and I watched. Autumn fell away, winter covered everything in white, and then spring again. I did not count. I did not remember that I ought to count. I was the Keeper of the Tower, and that was all I knew about myself.

The Tower stood on a crag at the entrance to the Valley. Tall, white, built of a stone that glowed pink at sunset and gold at dawn. Seven tiers. A spiral staircase within, from the cellar to the topmost platform, three hundred and thirty-three steps; I knew this because I counted them every morning as I climbed. On the topmost platform stood a bowl. Enormous, bronze, turned green by the rains, on three bronze legs shaped like dragon's claws. In the bowl lay firewood. Dry, resinous, laid in a lattice. It had never been lit.

Never.

"It is for the last resort," old Bran told me on the first day. Or on the day I remember as the first. "If the Greyness comes. You light it, and the whole Valley will see. And all will understand."

"Understand what?"

Old Bran gave me a long, sorrowful look from beneath his grey brows.

"That it is time," he said.

* * *

And the Valley...

I do not know how to describe the Valley to one who has not seen it. I tried later, many times, in different lives, in different languages. The words would not hold. They slipped off it as water slips off a lily leaf.

It lay below, at the foot of the Tower, between two ranges of mountains, not high, not menacing, but old, rounded, wooded to their very summits, as though these mountains had long since wearied of being mountains and decided to become hills. The Valley was long, a day's journey from the Tower to the far end, where the mountains came together again and where, it was said, the wasteland began, and so green that the green seemed not a colour but a sound. High, clear, unceasing.

Meadows. Boundless, rolling, in flowers, blue, yellow, white, lilac, over which the wind drove waves as over a sea. Groves: birch, light and ringing; oak, dark and solemn; apple, in blossom in spring and in fruit in autumn. A river, not wide, transparent to the very bottom, with pebbles on the bed visible as through glass, wound through the whole Valley, and along it stood mills with slow wheels, and houses.

The houses of the Valley people were low, round, half sunk into the hills, with grass-grown roofs, so that from afar they could not be told from knolls. Only the smoke from the chimneys, blue, thin, smelling of applewood, betrayed that people lived there. Round windows. Round doors, painted blue, green, yellow. Gardens. Apiaries. Vegetable plots. Sheep on the slopes, white, fluffy, like clouds that had come down to graze.

The people of the Valley were short, sturdy, sunburnt, with clear eyes and slow speech. They sang when they worked. Sang when they ate. Sang when they buried their old. They knew no war, not one, ever, in all their memory. They knew no locks; the doors of their houses were never fastened, because there was no one to fasten them against. They knew no money; they traded what they had: honey for wool, wool for bread, bread for a song.

They knew me. The Keeper of the Tower. And every morning one of them would climb the path to the foot of the crag and leave a basket at the door. Bread. Cheese. Apples. Honey. A jug of milk. Sometimes flowers. Sometimes a child's drawing in charcoal on a piece of birch bark: the Tower, and on it a little man waving his hand.

Every morning I waved to them from the window.

* * *

"Keeper!"

Her name was Mella. She climbed the path every morning, the miller's daughter from the lower bend, red-haired, freckled, in a blue dress with a white apron, with a basket on her elbow and a song on her lips. Her age I did not know. As old as girls are who have not yet decided whether to become grown. She set the basket by the door, knocked, three times, always three, and called from below, her head thrown back:

"Keeper! Are you alive up there?"

And I called down to her from the window of the seventh tier:

"Alive, Mella!"

"And what can you see?"

That was our custom. Every morning. She asked what could be seen. And I told her. All that I saw from a height no one in the Valley had seen, because no one but me climbed the Tower.

"I can see that old Gwen's sheep on the far hill have run off into the grove," I called. "I can see the children launching boats at your father's mill. I can see that the blue bellflowers have opened on the eastern meadow, a whole sea of them. I can see the mist lifting off the river. I can see the smith Odo walking to the beekeeper Iva with a bouquet, and hiding it behind his back!"

Mella laughed, ringing, head thrown back so that her red hair flew about.

"Smith Odo! To Iva! I'll tell everyone!"

"Don't tell! He's been working up to it for twenty years!"

"Then all the more reason I'll tell!" Mella cried. "Or he'll be working up to it another twenty!"

And she ran off down the path, humming, the basket swinging at her elbow, and I watched her from the window until the red speck vanished around the bend, among the apple trees.

Those were good mornings. The best of all I remember. Of all my lives.

* * *

There was also Ivo.

A boy of about twelve, an orphan, as they said in the Valley, old Bran's foundling, thin, dark-eyed, forever smeared with ink from oak-galls. He came to me in the Tower, the only one of the Valley people I let inside, and sat on a step of the staircase, on the third tier, with a piece of bark on his knees and a sharpened stick in his hand.

"Keeper," he would say. "Tell me what you see."

"Mella has already asked."

"Mella laughs," Ivo would say gravely. "And I write it down."

He wrote down everything. Every word. Which flowers had opened. Which birds had arrived. Who was born in the Valley, who died, who married whom. How many rains there had been. When the first thunderstorm came. He stored the bark in a chest at his house; old Bran told me there were already three chests, and all of them full.

"Why do you write it down, Ivo?" I asked once.

The boy looked at me for a long time, his head to one side. There was an ink stain on his cheek, always. Every day. He rubbed his cheek with the stick when he was thinking and forgot that the stick was inked.

"To remember," he said. "If suddenly everyone forgets. If the Valley is no more. Someone will find my bark and learn what it was like."

"Why might the Valley be no more?"

Ivo looked away.

"Bran says," he said quietly, "that one day the Greyness will come. From beyond the mountains. From the side where the wasteland is. And then you will light the fire on the Tower." He paused. "And then everything will end."

"What will end, Ivo?"

The boy looked at me, gravely, like an adult.

"Everything," he said. "That is what Bran says. But he does not say what will be after." Ivo clenched the stick in his hand. "So I write. So that there will be an after."

* * *

And there was Kai.

He stood at the door of the Tower. Always. Every day, every night, in rain and snow, in heat and frost. Tall, fair-haired, in an old chain-mail shirt darkened with time, in a blue cloak, with a sword at his belt and a spear on which he leaned when tired. The Guardian of the Tower. He was not of the Valley people; he had come once from beyond the mountains, from the far side, they said, and stayed. No one knew why.

Kai hardly spoke. When Mella brought the basket, he nodded to her. When Ivo climbed into the Tower, he let him pass, ruffling his hair. When I came down to the door to breathe, he stepped back a pace to give me room, and was silent.

But sometimes, at night, when I could not sleep and came down to the door, we sat side by side on the stone step and looked at the Valley. At the lights in the round windows. At the stars above the mountains. And then he spoke.

"Keeper," he said once. "Do you know why I am here?"

"No."

"I don't either," said Kai. And laughed, quietly, hoarsely, as though he had forgotten how to laugh over the years of silence. "I came from beyond the mountains. I walked a long time. A very long time. I don't remember from where. I remember only that I was looking for someone. And then I saw the Tower, and you in the window. And I stayed." He paused. "Perhaps I was looking for you."

"Why?"

Kai looked at the stars for a long time.

"To stand at the door," he said at last. "When you do what you must. So that no one will hinder you." He paused. "And so that you will not be alone when you do it."

"What must I do, Kai?"

He did not answer. He took his spear, rose, took his place at the door, straight, motionless, as always.

"Go to sleep, Keeper," he said, without turning. "It is a long way off yet. Maybe a hundred years. Maybe tomorrow." A pause. "I am here."

* * *

Old Bran lived at the foot of the crag, in a round house sunk into the slope, with a roof overgrown with moss and forget-me-nots, and a garden where grew herbs found nowhere else in the Valley. He was the oldest man I had ever known. So old that his beard, white, long, to his belt, seemed to have grown out of the earth, like the roots of an oak. He walked with a staff, not leaning on it, but rather conversing with it, tapping it on the stones, as if asking the path the way.

I went down to him sometimes, in the evenings, when Kai stood at the door and Ivo slept on the step of the third tier, covered with his cloak. Bran gave me tea of herbs he gathered himself, bitter, smelling of honey and summer, and told me things.

"The Valley has always been here," he would say, stirring the coals in the hearth. "And the Tower has always been. And a Keeper in it." He looked at me. "Not you. Others. Many. My grandfather remembered one. My great-grandfather, another. Each lived in the Tower until the time came. And then left. And a new one appeared."

"Left for where?"

"No one knows," said Bran. "One morning the door of the Tower stood open, and the Keeper was gone. And a year later, two years later, someone was in the window again. A new one. Young. Not remembering where he came from." He paused. "Like you."

"And the Greyness? Has it come before?"

Bran was silent for a long time.

"Once," he said at last. "Very long ago. So long ago that even the songs of it are nearly forgotten. One remains. An old one. It is sung only at funerals."

"Sing it."

Bran laid down the poker. Closed his eyes. And sang, in a hoarse, cracked, old man's voice, slowly, almost without a tune:

"The Greyness came from beyond the mountains, without eyes,
The Greyness came from beyond the mountains, without faces,
And all that it touched forgot itself:
The grass, that it is green; the bird, that it is a bird.

And the Keeper on the Tower saw it,
And the Keeper wept, and kindled the fire.
And the Valley burned from edge to edge.
And the Greyness went away. It had nothing to take.

And in spring grass grew from the ash.
And the grass remembered that it was grass."

Bran opened his eyes.

"That is the whole song," he said.

I sat motionless. The coals crackled softly.

"He burned the Valley," I said. "To save it."

"He burned the Valley," Bran agreed. "So that the Greyness would have nothing to take. The Greyness does not eat fire. The Greyness eats memory. It eats what knows itself. Grass that knows it is green. A bird that knows it is a bird. A man who knows his name." He paused. "But ash does not know itself. Ash cannot be made to forget. It has nothing to remember."

"And in spring?"

"And in spring something new grows from the ash," said Bran. "And it remembers. Because the ash remembers for it." He gave me a long look. "So the song says. Whether it is true, I do not know. I did not see. I was not there."

* * *

The Greyness came in autumn.

I saw it first, because I looked farthest. In the morning, when Mella knocked at the door, three times, as always, and called from below, "Keeper! What can you see?", I looked at the far end of the Valley, where the mountains came together again, where the wasteland began.

And did not answer.

"Keeper?" Mella's voice grew uncertain. "Keeper, are you there?"

Above the far end of the Valley, above the mountains, something was rising. Not a cloud. Not mist. Not smoke. Something grey, even, flat, colourless, like a wall. It did not move, and it moved. It did not grow, and it became larger. It was like looking at a sheet of paper from which the writing has been wiped away; not white, not clean, but precisely wiped. Empty where something had been.

And where it touched the earth, the farthest edge of the Valley, the farthest meadows, the farthest groves, the green... did not go out. Did not turn yellow. Did not die. It simply ceased to be green. Became the same grey. Even. Colourless. As if it had forgotten what colour it had been.

"Keeper!" Mella was not laughing now. "What is it?!"

"Run home, Mella," I said. My voice sounded as if from far away. "Tell your father. Tell everyone. Let them come to the Tower. All of them. Now."

"What has happened?"

"The Greyness," I said.

Mella froze below, at the door. Then the basket fell from her hands. Apples rolled down the path. She looked up at me, and her face was white as her apron.

And then she turned and ran down. Not home. To Bran.

* * *

They came to the Tower by noon. All of them. The whole Valley. Millers and beekeepers, shepherds and smiths, old and young, stood at the foot of the crag, on the slope, on the path, on the rocks, hundreds and hundreds, looking up. At me. In the window of the seventh tier.

And behind them, from the far end of the Valley, slowly, inexorably, crept the Greyness.

It had already eaten the far meadows. The far groves. The mill on the far bend; I saw its wheel, still turning that morning, stop when the Greyness touched it. It did not break; it stopped. As though it had forgotten that it was meant to turn. I saw the sheep on the far slope, white, fluffy, turn grey and freeze. They did not fall. They did not die. They simply stood. Not knowing that they were sheep.

Old Bran climbed the stairs of the Tower, slowly, step by step, three hundred and thirty-three steps, leaning on his staff, resting on every tier. Kai followed him, ready to catch him. Ivo came after, with his small chest of bark in his arms.

When they reached the seventh tier, Bran could not catch his breath for a long time. Then he went to the window. Looked at the Valley. At the Greyness devouring it from the edge.

"It is time," he said.

One word. Quietly. As though speaking of the weather.

* * *

"I cannot," I said.

Bran turned to me.

"I cannot, Bran." My hands were shaking. "There are people down there. Mella is there. The smith Odo is there, who has not managed in twenty years to give Iva a bouquet. Old Gwen with her sheep. Your house, Bran. Ivo was born there, or found there, no matter. Everything is there." My voice broke. "You want me to burn it all?"

"The people will leave," said Bran calmly. "Behind the Tower. Over the pass. Into the mountains. There are caves there. They can wait it out. The fire will not reach the caves."

"And the Valley?"

"And the Valley will burn," said Bran. "All of it. From edge to edge. Meadows. Groves. Houses. Mills. Gardens." He paused. "Otherwise the Greyness will eat it. And then it will not burn. It will simply forget itself. Forever. It will stand grey, even, empty. And the people who come back from the caves and walk down into it will forget too. One by one."

"How do you know?"

"The song knows," said Bran.

"The song is a thousand years old! Perhaps it lies!"

Bran looked at me with a long, sorrowful gaze.

"Perhaps," he said. "Perhaps it does lie. Then you will have burned the Valley for nothing." He paused. "And if it does not lie, and you do not burn it?"

I did not answer.

Ivo stood by the wall, hugging a small chest of bark to his chest. He looked at me with enormous dark eyes. The ink stain on his cheek.

"Keeper," he said quietly. "I have written it all down. All three chests. All of how it was. Every flower. Every song. Every house." He paused. "If you burn it, I will carry the bark to the caves. And when the grass grows, I will read it to the grass. So that it remembers."

"And if it does not remember?"

"Then I will read it again," said Ivo. "And again. Every evening. Until it remembers."

* * *

He came when the Greyness was already in the middle of the Valley.

I stood by the bowl on the topmost platform of the Tower, with a torch in my hand that Kai had lit from Bran's hearth and brought up to me. I stood and could not. The torch crackled. The wind tore at the flame. Below, on the slope, the people of the Valley were moving off behind the Tower, toward the pass, in a long, slow file, with bundles, with children in their arms, with goats on ropes, and looking back. Each one looked back. Looked up. At me.

Mella went last. She did not look back. She walked with her head lowered, pressing something to her chest, I could not see what. And at a bend of the path she stopped, raised her face, and called out. For the last time.

"Keeper! What can you see?"

And I could not answer.

And behind me someone said:

"I can see that you are afraid."

I turned.

On the topmost platform of the Tower, by the very parapet, stood a man. Tall. A head taller than I. In a grey cloak; no, not a cloak; he was grey all through, as if moulded from the same Greyness that crept across the Valley, only denser. I could not see his face. Where a face should be there was something blurred, elusive, like a face in a misted mirror.

Kai would not have let him in. Kai stood at the door, I knew it, and would have let no one pass. But he was here.

"Are you the Greyness?" I asked.

"No," he said. His voice was calm, quiet, older than mine. And, somehow, familiar. "I am the one who comes to Keepers when they are afraid. I came to the one before you. And to the one before him. I always come."

"Why?"

"To ask," he said. "Will you light it?"

"And do you want me to?"

He paused. Below, the Greyness ate another grove. The birch one. Light. Ringing. It stopped ringing.

"I do not want it," he said. "And I do not not want it. I cannot want for you. It is your Tower. Your Valley. Your fire." A pause. "I came only to say: you are not the first. Every one who stood here with a torch was afraid. Every one thought the song lied. Every one thought there might be another way."

"And is there?"

"I do not know," he said. "Perhaps there is. I have not found it." A pause. "But I am looking."

"What became of those who lit it?"

"They went," said the Grey One. "To where Keepers go." He paused. "And what became of those who did not light it, I do not know. Because after them no one came to the Tower. Because there was no one left to come. Because the Valley forgot that it had a Tower."

I looked at him. At his blurred, elusive face. And suddenly it seemed to me, for a single moment, in the glare of the torch, that I knew him. Not his features. Something else. The way he stood. As if on a threshold. One foot here, the other somewhere else.

"Who are you?" I asked.

He did not answer. He was looking down, at Mella, standing at the bend of the path with her face upturned.

"She is waiting for an answer," he said. "'What can you see.' Answer her."

* * *

I turned to the bowl.

Bronze, enormous, green with age, on three dragon's claws. The dry, resinous firewood laid in a lattice. For a thousand years no one had touched it. For a thousand years it had waited.

I looked at the Valley. For the last time.

The meadows, still green at the near edge, in blue bellflowers, in yellow buttercups. The river, transparent, with pebbles on its bed. Mella's father's mill, the wheel still turning. The round houses in the hills, smoke still rising from the chimneys. The gardens. The apiaries. The apple trees, heavy with red fruit. The road to old Gwen's house. Odo's forge. Iva's apiary. Bran's house under the forget-me-nots.

And beyond it, the Greyness. Even. Colourless. Empty. Creeping.

"Mella!" I shouted down. My voice broke. "I can see everything! I can see the whole Valley! Every flower! Every house! Every window!" I gasped. "I can see how beautiful it is!"

Mella stood at the bend. Looked at me. And then, I saw it even from such a height, she smiled. And held up over her head what she had been pressing to her chest.

The basket. With a single apple. A red one. Which she had picked up from the path that morning.

"I will remember!" she cried. "I will remember everything, Keeper!"

And vanished around the bend.

I lowered the torch into the bowl.

* * *

The fire was white.

I do not know why. The wood was ordinary, pine, resinous. The torch was ordinary. But the flame that leapt from the bowl was white, blinding, steady, like a light I had seen... somewhere... sometime... above a circle of grey stones, above a green plain, in another dream, in another life.

It rose in a column, higher than the Tower, higher than the mountains, higher than the clouds, and hung for a moment as if looking about. And then it fell. Down upon the Valley.

And ran.

Not like a fire, not in tongues, not in billows of smoke, not with crackling. It ran across the Valley as ripples run across water from a thrown stone, in circles, from the Tower to the edges, wider and wider, farther and farther. And where it passed, everything flared. Not burned; flared. For an instant. White. Every blade of grass. Every leaf. Every pebble on the river bed. Every window in the round houses. And I saw, in that flash, for one moment, every thing so bright, so real, so wholly itself as never before. As if the fire did not burn it, but named it. Aloud. By its name. For the last time.

And then went out. And ash remained.

The fire reached the Greyness. And the Greyness, I saw it, stopped. Froze. As if it had struck a wall. It stood, even, colourless, at the edge of the white fire, and went no further. There was nothing for it to take. All that lay before it had already become ash. And ash does not know itself. Ash cannot be made to forget.

And the Greyness withdrew.

Slowly. Reluctantly. Back to where it had come from. Beyond the mountains. Into the wasteland.

And the Valley was left empty. Grey. Black. Smoking. From edge to edge.

I stood on the topmost platform of the Tower, by the bowl where the last coals were dying, and wept. For the first time in all those years, in all the lives I did not remember, I wept. And my tears fell on the bronze of the bowl and hissed, evaporating.

The Grey One stood beside me. In silence.

"I burned it," I said.

"You saved it," he said.

"What difference does it make?"

The Grey One was silent for a long time.

"I do not know," he said at last. "Honestly, I do not know. I ask myself that question every time." A pause. "Perhaps none."

* * *

I do not remember how I came down. I remember only that the three hundred and thirty-three steps ended, and I came out of the door of the Tower, and Kai stood there. As always. Straight. With his spear. Only his face was wet.

"You were here," I said.

"I was here," said Kai. "I promised."

"Did you see who went up to me?"

Kai looked at me for a long time.

"No one went up, Keeper," he said. "I stood at the door. No one passed."

"There was a man up there. Grey. Tall."

"There was you," said Kai. "Alone. I heard you speaking." He paused. "I thought you were praying. Or talking to yourself. Like all Keepers. Bran used to say they all talk to themselves in the last hour." Kai lowered his eyes. "As if asking themselves for permission."

* * *

I do not know what came after. The vision, if it was a vision, began to fall apart. As a dream falls apart toward morning, when you are almost awake and clutch at the last shreds.

I remember, in fragments, caves in the mountains. The people of the Valley sitting by fires. Bran telling the children the song. Ivo laying out his bark on the stones, three chests, hundreds of pieces, and reading aloud, slowly, syllable by syllable. About the blue bellflowers on the eastern meadow. About the smith Odo and his bouquet. About the little boats at the mill.

I remember Mella, at the mouth of a cave, with the basket in which lay one red apple. She was looking at the Valley. At the black, smoking, dead Valley below. And her face was calm.

"In spring," she said to someone. Perhaps to me. "In spring it will grow. The song says so."

"And if the song lies?" I asked.

Mella looked at me. Smiled.

"Then I will plant it," she said. And lifted the apple. "Here. From this. The seeds."

* * *

And the last thing I remember.

Spring. The Valley. Black, and not quite. Through the ash, through the cinders, through the scorched earth, grass is pushing up. Thin. Pale. Timid. Green.

And Ivo, kneeling in the midst of this grass, with a piece of bark in his hands, reading to it aloud. Quietly. Slowly. Syllable by syllable.

"On the eastern meadow the blue bellflowers have opened. A whole sea of them."

And the grass around him, I saw it, I swear that I saw it, begins, a little, almost imperceptibly, to turn blue.

And on the crag at the entrance to the Valley stands the Tower. White. Empty. The door is open. At the door, no one. Only a spear leaning against the wall. And in the window of the seventh tier, no one.

For now, no one.

* * *

I opened my eyes.

I do not know in which of my lives I woke that morning. In which body. Under which sky. Afterward, in each of them, I dreamed of the Valley, and woke with the same taste in my mouth: of ash and apples.

And with the same question, to which I did not know the answer.

I burned the Valley to save it. Or I saved it by burning. Or the Greyness, having withdrawn, is simply waiting beyond the mountains for the grass to grow again, and again to know that it is grass. So that it may come again.

And I shall stand in the Tower again. With the torch. Not remembering that I have stood there before.

And someone will again climb the stairs to me, past the guardian who lets no one through, tall, grey, faceless, and ask quietly:

"Will you light it?"

And below, at the bend of the path, a red-haired girl with a basket will raise her face and call:

"Keeper! What can you see?"

And again I shall not know what to answer.
