Chapter XXX
The Professor's Departure

"Mon cher," said Konstantin, when the carriage had passed through the gates and Tikhon on the box had struck up his endless song about the freezing coachman, "I shall tell you one thing, only don't take offence."

"Go on."

"If that professor once more 'lingers in our parts,' I shall tie him up myself and take him to the station. Gently. As Tikhon promised." Konstantin straightened his cap. "He came for a week. He is living through his third. The General kidnaps him. Auntie invites him. Strauch quarrels with him every other evening. And you..." He glanced sideways at me. "You, after every meeting with him, lie awake half the night and write in your blue notebook. I hear the pen scratching. My room is on the other side of the wall."

"He leaves today, Kostya."

"I know," said Konstantin. And suddenly fell silent. Then he added more quietly, looking at the fields beyond the roadside, yellow, mown, with stacks that looked like sleeping bears: "And for some reason I am sorry. Though he is a windbag, an eccentric and a nihilist." He paused. "He is the only one who talks to you as though he knew what you are keeping silent about."

On the box, Tikhon stopped singing.

"Master," he said over his shoulder. "Forgive me for butting in. But that professor is a good man. At the tavern by the 'Anchor' he has all the coachmen spellbound with his tales. Sits, drinks his tea, and writes it all down. He'll ask, 'And what do you say, brother, about the wood-goblin?' - and you tell him what you heard from your granny. And he writes it down. Word for word." Tikhon paused. "He wrote me down too. About the coachman who drove with the devil in a blizzard. Says he'll put it in a book. With my name." He drew himself up. "Tikhon Lukich Borodin, of the village of Maly Klyuchi. They'll be reading it, he says, a hundred years from now."

"In a hundred years, Tikhon," said Konstantin, "all your great-grandchildren will know that their forefather drove with the devil."

"And let them know, master," Tikhon replied imperturbably. "I got him there, didn't I? All the way to the station. And took nothing for vodka. The devil, he's a man under orders too."

* * *

The district town greeted us with market day.

The square before the cathedral, white, five-domed, with green cupolas and golden crosses that blazed so in the sun it hurt to look at them, was crammed with people, carts, horses, stalls, tents. It smelled of tar, dung, fresh bread, apples, fish, kvass and something frying. The market women cried: "Pies! Hot from the oven!" "Hot sbiten!" "Grated kalachi!" Geese gabbled in wicker cages. A gypsy boy led a bear on a chain, and the bear, rising on its hind legs, bowed to the public, and the public threw coppers into the hat. At the hitching post two peasants were haggling over the price of a horse, slapping each other's hands so hard that the clap rang across the whole square. A clerk in a peaked cap and waistcoat bawled the price of calico at the door of the merchant Toloknov's shop, while the merchant himself, stout, in a peasant coat, with a spade-shaped beard, sat on the porch drinking tea from a saucer and surveying the square like an admiral his squadron.

Tikhon drove the carriage slowly through the crowd, calling out, "Mind yourselves! I'm carrying the master!" and the crowd reluctantly gave way.

And then he came out of the crowd.

I did not see him at once, only when the carriage stopped, because he had stepped right in front of the horses. A man of no certain age, perhaps forty, perhaps sixty, barefoot despite the autumn chill, in a ragged linen shirt to the knees, belted with a rope, with iron chains around his neck that clanked at every movement. His hair was long, matted, shot with grey. His beard hung in tufts. And his eyes, light, almost colourless, like a sky bleached by the sun, looked straight at me.

"Grishenka," whispered Tikhon, hastily taking off his cap and crossing himself. "The blessed one."

The holy fool came up to the carriage. Slowly. Clanking his chains. He stopped by the step. For a long time he looked up at me from below, not blinking.

The crowd around fell quiet. The market women stopped crying. Even the bear sank back onto four paws.

"The guest has come," said Grishenka. His voice was high, cracked, childlike. "And the master is not yet born."

Beside me Konstantin sat up straight.

"What?" I said.

"The guest has come," the holy fool repeated, and his face suddenly dissolved into a smile, broad, toothless, happy, like a child's who has seen a toy. "And the master is not yet born. And the house already stands. And the key is in the dust. And the rose is blooming." He laughed, thin and ringing. "Blooming, blooming! Out of season! Not in its own land!"

Cold beneath the ribs. On the left.

Grishenka stretched out his hand, filthy, with broken nails, and touched my palm. The one without a life line. He drew his finger across it, from the wrist toward the fingers. To where in all people the line runs.

"Smooth," he said. And wept, as easily as a minute before he had laughed. "Smooth. Like an angel's. Like a stone's. Poor thing. Poor guest. Walks and walks. All the doors are open, and nowhere to go in."

He let go of my hand. Stepped back. Made the sign of the cross over me, wide and sweeping. Then over Konstantin. Then over Tikhon. Then over the horses.

"Go, guest," he said. "Go to the teller of tales. He will tell you a tale. The last one. And then he will leave. And you will stay. And then you will leave too." He laughed again. "They will all leave! All! And the rose will remain!"

And he walked away, barefoot, his chains clanking, through the crowd, which parted before him, crossing itself and bowing.

For a long time Tikhon did not start the horses. Then he put on his cap.

"Master," he said quietly, without turning round, "Grishenka never talks to anybody. Fifteen years he's gone about this town, and silent. Only plays the fool. And with you," he paused, "he spoke."

Konstantin said nothing. But I felt his hand close for a moment on my elbow, hard, almost to pain, and let go.

* * *

The inn "Golden Anchor" stood on the corner of the square, a two-storey wooden house with a peeling signboard on which a golden anchor had once been painted and which now showed something like a rusty poker. Below was a tavern, from which came the clatter of crockery, the smell of cabbage soup and singing: someone was belting out "Ah, you hall, my hall" off-key, but with feeling. Upstairs were the rooms.

The landlord, Panteleimon Kuzmich Sychov, met us in the doorway: small, round, bald, with red side-whiskers, in a waistcoat stretched over his belly so that the buttons seemed ready to fly off, and with a towel over his shoulder. Seeing Konstantin in uniform, he bowed from the waist. Seeing me, he bowed lower still.

"Welcome, welcome!" he began, tumbling over his words. "To the professor? Of course, sir, of course, upstairs, room three. Packing, they are. Packing since morning. Their notebooks. I say to him, 'Ivan Filippovich, you'd better put them in a trunk, or you'll lose them on the road,' and he says, 'No, Panteleimon Kuzmich, notebooks are like children, you can't put them in a trunk, they need air.'" The landlord threw up his hands. "Air! For notebooks! Now isn't he an eccentric?"

"An eccentric," Konstantin agreed.

"Three weeks he's lived here," Panteleimon Kuzmich went on, lowering his voice as though divulging a state secret. "Candles he's burned, a whole pood! Writes at night. By day he goes about the market, talks to the women, the coachmen, the beggars. Even tried it with Grishenka the blessed." The landlord shook his head. "Grishenka, now, he's silent. Fifteen years silent. And the professor goes to him every day, with a pie. Sits down beside him on the church porch, hands over the pie and sits. In silence. An hour. Two. The folk laughed."

"And Grishenka?"

"Grishenka eats the pie and goes," said the landlord. "But yesterday..." He faltered.

"What yesterday?"

Panteleimon Kuzmich looked round as if afraid of being overheard.

"Yesterday Grishenka spoke to him. For the first time. I didn't hear it myself, the sexton told me, he was on the porch. Grishenka said to the professor: 'Tomorrow the guest will come. Tell him a tale. The last one.'" The landlord crossed himself. "And here you are, sir."

* * *

"And another thing," said Panteleimon Kuzmich, already climbing the creaking stairs with us. "Forgive me, sir, it may be nothing. But there was a lodger here the day before yesterday. Stayed one night. A gentleman. A visitor. In a grey coat. With a cane. Polite to a fault. Spoke French the way you and I speak Russian. Paid in gold." The landlord paused. "And kept asking about the professor. Who he is, where from, what he writes. Asked to see the notebooks. Said he was a lover of antiquities. Ready to buy. Offered good money."

My throat went dry. Beside me Konstantin stopped on the step.

"And the professor?" asked Konstantin.

"The professor threw him out," said the landlord with relish. "Politely, mind. Says, 'Sir, my notebooks are not for sale. They are not even mine. They belong to the people.' And that gentleman smiled, bowed and left. In the morning. At the crack of dawn." The landlord scratched his side-whiskers. "Only, sir, here's what's odd. He paid for the room, he signed the book, I talked with him half the evening. And his face, strike me dead, I can't remember. Not a line of it. As if I'd been looking into water."

"And the name in the book?" I asked.

Panteleimon Kuzmich blushed.

"That's just it, sir. I look in the book, there's a line. There's ink. There's a signature. And I can't read it." He spread his hands. "Squiggles of some sort. Long, crooked, one running into another. Not in our letters, not French, not German. I showed the sexton, he couldn't read it either. Said, maybe it's Armenian?"

"Show me," I said.

The landlord ran downstairs and came back with a thick, greasy register of lodgers, opened it at the right page and jabbed with his finger.

Among the clumsy signatures of merchants and the tidy ones of clerks stood a single line. Short.

Long, flowing, curving lines. Running into one another without a break.

The script.

Konstantin looked at the line over my shoulder. Then at me. And for the first time since I had known him I saw real fear in his eyes. Not for himself.

* * *

Sventsitsky's room was cramped, low, with a single window on the square, a sagging bed, a washstand in the corner and a table so heaped with papers that the table could not be seen. On the floor stood two worn leather suitcases and an enormous wicker hamper, filled to the brim with notebooks: thick ones, thin ones, in oilcloth covers, in cardboard, with no covers at all, tied in bundles with twine. It smelled of tallow, tobacco, tea and paper dust. On the windowsill stood a cold samovar.

The professor himself sat on the floor in the midst of this paper sea, in his waistcoat, without his frock coat, a pen behind his ear and an ink stain on his chin, tying up one more bundle with twine.

"Ah," he said, seeing us. Without surprise. "You've come. I knew. Grishenka said so yesterday." He smiled. "Sit down, if you can find where. Lieutenant, take Olonets Province off that chair, it won't mind. Put it on the bed, beside Vologda."

Konstantin lifted a bundle of notebooks from the chair and carefully laid it on the bed. He sat. I sat on the windowsill, next to the samovar.

"Ivan Filippovich," I said. "The landlord says a man in grey came to see you."

Sventsitsky stopped tying the twine. For a long time he was silent.

"He did," he said at last. "He wanted to buy notebooks. Not all of them. One." He raised his clear, light eyes to me, which just now were not cheerful. "The one where I wrote down what you told me. On the day you do not remember."

"You refused."

"I refused." Sventsitsky took up the twine again. "For thirty years, young man, I have been collecting what no one values. Grannies' tales. Charms. Omens. I was never paid for it. They laughed at me at the university. They drove me from my chair for 'unscientific methods.' My wife left because instead of a salary I brought home notebooks." He pulled the knot tight. "And now for the first time in thirty years I was offered money for a notebook. Large money. More than I have seen in all my life." He smiled wryly. "Do you know what I felt?"

"What?"

"That this notebook is not mine," said Sventsitsky simply. "That I only keep it. Until the time comes." He raised his eyes to me. "The one in grey looked at me, smiled and said, 'You are right, Professor. It is not yours. But it is not his either.'" Sventsitsky paused. "And left. And I did not sleep all night. Thinking: whose, then?"

* * *

He got up, brushed off his knees, rummaged in the hamper and pulled out a notebook, not the oilcloth one I had already seen, but another. Old. Worn. In a leather binding darkened with time, with brass corners and a clasp. On the cover, in faded ink, was written a single word: "GUEST."

"Here," said Sventsitsky. "This is what I really collect. For thirty years. All the rest, wood-goblins, mermaids, house-spirits, that is just so. To throw dust in the eyes. For the department, for the Geographical Society, for reports. But this..." He stroked the cover. "This is mine. The real thing."

He opened the notebook. The pages were covered in small, close handwriting, in different inks, with different pens, as if written over years, one entry at a time, by different light.

"Forty-seven entries," he said. "Forty-seven stories. From thirty provinces. From Arkhangelsk to Bessarabia. From the Carpathians to Irkutsk." He turned a page. "And all about one thing. About a man who comes to a village. From nowhere. Young. Without a name. Does not remember who he is. Lives there a week, a month, a year. Helps. Raises a cottage for one, heals the son of another, pulls a child out of the ice-hole for a third." Sventsitsky looked at me. "And leaves. Always leaves. And always leaves something behind. A coin. A stone. A line on a door. A cutting. And always says the same thing."

"What?"

Sventsitsky found a page and ran his finger along a line.

"'If another comes, feed him,'" he read.

The room fell quiet. Below, in the tavern, someone was still singing about the hall.

"Here, Olonets Province, the year eighteen-twelve," Sventsitsky went on, turning pages. "A young man came to a village when the French were burning Moscow. Did not remember himself. They thought him shell-shocked. Lived with a widow. Left in spring, at the ice break, across the river, when the ice was already cracking. Everyone thought he drowned." He turned over. "Here, Little Russia, Poltava, the thirties. Came to a beekeeper. Spoke with the bees. The bees did not sting him. Left in a thunderstorm." Another page. "Here, Siberia, Irkutsk Province, near Baikal. The Buryats told me. Came in winter, over the ice of Baikal, barefoot. Lived with a shaman. The shaman said he was from the upper world. Left when the ice on Baikal cracked with such a roar that it was heard a hundred versts away." Sventsitsky closed the notebook. "Forty-seven times. Forty-seven places. A hundred years. Two hundred. Perhaps more, for old people tell what they heard from their grandfathers."

"And you think..." Konstantin began.

"I do not think, Lieutenant," Sventsitsky interrupted gently. "I write down. Thinking is not my work." He paused. "But sometimes, at night, when the candle is burning low, I do think. I think: what if these are not forty-seven different men? What if it is one? Who comes again and again. And each time does not remember that he has already been."

* * *

"Why did you begin collecting this?" I asked.

Sventsitsky did not answer for a long time. He sat on the edge of the bed, right on Vologda Province. Laid the notebook on his knees. Looked out of the window, at the square, the cathedral, the golden crosses.

"I was eight years old," he said at last. "I lived with my grandmother in a village near Kargopol. My father served in Petersburg, my mother was dead. Winter. A blizzard. The kind where you cannot see two steps ahead. And in the evening there was a knock at the door." He paused. "Grandmother opened. On the threshold stood a man. Young. All in snow. Bareheaded. Without mittens. In some strange clothes, neither peasant nor gentleman's. He said, 'Let me wait out the storm.' Grandmother let him in. Fed him. He sat down on the bench by the stove, warmed himself, and saw me. I was sitting on top of the stove, looking at him."

"And?"

"And he asked, 'Do you want a tale?'" Sventsitsky smiled, softly, like a child. "I said I did. And he told me a tale. He told it all night. Until the blizzard ended. I fell asleep toward morning. And when I woke, he was gone. Grandmother said he left at dawn. Over the snow. And there were no tracks, she said. As if he had not walked at all."

"What tale did he tell?"

"'Go I know not where, bring back I know not what,'" said Sventsitsky. "You know it. Everybody knows it. But he did not tell it the way the grannies tell it. Differently. With a different ending." The professor paused. "For thirty years I have been looking for that ending. In every province. From every teller. I have written down forty variants. Not one has an ending like his."

"Did he leave anything?" I asked.

Sventsitsky looked at me. Then slowly reached into the inner pocket of his waistcoat. And took out a pencil.

An ordinary pencil. Short, worn almost to a stub, in a wooden casing darkened by time and by touch.

"He left this," said the professor. "On the bench by the stove. And Grandmother said that, going, he told her: 'Give it to the boy. Let him write. Otherwise they will forget. And when I come for my tale, let him give it back.'" Sventsitsky turned the pencil in his fingers. "Thirty-eight years I have written with it. I sharpen and sharpen, and it never runs out." He raised his eyes to me. "Young man, with this pencil I wrote down everything you told me that day. I wrote down your line with it. The one that matched the General's scroll."

He held out the pencil to me.

"Here," he said. "It seems you have come for your tale."

* * *

I did not take it.

"Ivan Filippovich," I said. "I was eight years old when you were eight years old? That cannot be. You are fifty. I am..." I faltered. How old was I? I did not know. "I am less."

"I know," said Sventsitsky calmly. "I am not saying it was you. I am saying you have come for your tale." He did not lower the hand with the pencil. "Those are different things, young man. Quite different." He paused. "Vera Anatolievna also knows that fifteen years ago you were a child. And the drawing is in her reticule."

"How do you know about the drawing?" Konstantin asked sharply.

"She showed it to me," said Sventsitsky. "Yesterday. She came here, to the inn. Alone. Under a veil." He smiled. "A very brave woman. She said, 'Professor, you collect tales. Perhaps this one is yours.' And showed me. I copied it into the notebook 'Guest.' Entry forty-eight."

He was still holding the pencil out to me.

I took it.

The pencil was warm. Like a coin. Like a stone in the sun. Like a hand held too long to be let go.

* * *

There was a knock at the door. Loud, demanding, in German.

"Ivan Filippovich!" came a dry voice. "Are you still here? Or have you already fled from your debts to science?"

Doctor Strauch came in without waiting for an answer, in his overcoat, in his bowler, with his cane and a small parcel in his hands. He saw us and raised his eyebrows.

"Ah. A consilium in full session." He looked about for somewhere to sit, found nothing, and remained standing. "I am here, properly, for a minute. To tell the professor that he is a charlatan, an unscientific dreamer, and that his theories of the obmiranki are nonsense that will not withstand a single serious test."

"I thank you, Karl Fyodorovich," Sventsitsky replied imperturbably. "You are very kind."

"And also," Strauch continued in the same dry tone, holding out the parcel, "to bring you this. For the road."

Sventsitsky unwrapped the parcel. Inside was a book. Thick, in a solid binding, in German.

"'Medical Psychology,'" the professor read. "Lotze."

"There is a chapter on the splitting of consciousness," said Strauch. "And on memory. I have underlined the relevant passages." He paused. "Perhaps it will be of use. For your notebook 'Guest.'"

Sventsitsky raised his eyes to him.

"How do you know about the notebook 'Guest,' Karl Fyodorovich?"

Strauch was silent for a long while. He turned an unlit cigar in his fingers. Then he took off his bowler, for the first time since I had known him, in front of people, and said quietly, without any of his usual dryness:

"Because I have one just like it, Ivan Filippovich. Only mine holds no tales. It holds case histories. Thirty years of practice. Eleven cases." He paused. "Eleven young men who came from nowhere, did not remember themselves, lived a year or two and left. I treated them. With cold rubdowns and Karamzin." He smiled thinly. "It did not help."

"And you kept silent?" Konstantin whispered.

"I am a German, Lieutenant," said Strauch, putting his bowler on again. "I do not draw conclusions from eleven cases. I need at least twelve." He looked at me. Long. Over his gold spectacles. "It seems there will soon be one."

* * *

Sventsitsky told his tale at the post station.

The station stood at the edge of town, a low stone house with stables, a striped barrier by the road, and a verst post on which was written, "To the provincial town, 47 versts." Inside was the travellers' room: benches along the walls, a table, a samovar, fly-specked woodcuts on the walls, "How the Mice Buried the Cat," "Bova the King's Son," "The Last Judgment," and an icon in the corner with an ever-burning lamp.

The station master, a retired sergeant with a wooden leg and grey moustaches that he kept twirling, studied Sventsitsky's travel warrant long and sternly, running his finger over the lines, moving his lips, sighing.

"No horses," he announced at last.

"What do you mean, none?" gasped Sventsitsky.

"None, Your Honour. All out. The police captain took them in the morning, then the landowner Kologrivova, then a courier from the province. They'll be back by evening." The station master twirled his moustache. "Wait."

"My good man," said Konstantin, stepping forward and squaring his shoulders so that his epaulettes flashed. "Do you know who stands before you?"

"A professor, Your Honour," the station master replied imperturbably. "It says so on the warrant."

"This," said Konstantin solemnly, "is a professor of the Imperial Geographical Society. Whom they await in person in Petersburg. With information of the greatest importance." He lowered his voice. "For the Sovereign."

The station master looked at Sventsitsky, at his waistcoat, at the ink stain on his chin, at the hamper stuffed with notebooks. Then at Konstantin. Then at Sventsitsky again.

"For the Sovereign?" he repeated.

"Personally," said Konstantin.

The station master was silent a long time. Then he cleared his throat, twirled his moustache and stumped on his wooden leg to the door.

"Mitrokha!" he bellowed across the whole yard. "Harness the piebalds! Quick! The gentleman's off to the Sovereign!"

When he had gone out, Sventsitsky looked at Konstantin reproachfully.

"Lieutenant, that is not true."

"It is, Professor," said Konstantin imperturbably. "The Sovereign is people too. And you collect what belongs to the people. Hence, for the Sovereign." He sat down on a bench. "And now, while they harness, tell the tale. That very one. With the other ending. You promised. Grishenka commanded."

* * *

Sventsitsky sat down opposite us. Outside the horses whinnied, Mitrokha shouted, the harness creaked. The samovar on the table sang softly. The lamp in the corner flickered.

"'There lived a royal archer,'" the professor began, and his voice changed: it grew lower, slower, more singing, like a village storyteller's on the earthen bank by the cottage, "'by the name of Fedot. And he had a wife, a beauty, a clever one, of the bird kin, a dove. And the tsar envied Fedot. And wished to take his wife from him. And devised a task that could not be done. Summoned Fedot and said: "Go I know not where. Bring back I know not what. And if you do not bring it, off with your head."'"

Konstantin listened, leaning forward. I sat motionless, clutching the warm pencil in my pocket.

"'Fedot went. He walked long. Through forests, through marshes, over mountains. He came to the edge of the earth. To the sea. To a hut on chicken legs. And in the hut was an old woman. Ancient. And she said: "Seek, Fedot, beyond the sea. On an island. In an empty house. There lives that I-know-not-what." Fedot crossed the sea. Found the island. Found the house. Empty. No one there. Fedot sat down at the table, hungry, weary. And said into the emptiness: "Ah, to have something to eat." And suddenly a voice out of the air: "At once, master." And the table laid itself. Cabbage soup, porridge, pies.'" Sventsitsky paused. "'Fedot asks: "Who are you?" And the voice answers: "I am Shmat-razum, the Scrap of Mind. No one sees me. I have neither body nor face. I served the old man who lived here, served a hundred years, and he never once said thank you to me. But you said 'to have something to eat,' as if you were speaking to me. If you wish, I will serve you."'"

A voice without a body. Which serves. Which no one sees. Somewhere in the dark, in a room I had never seen, a cold window glowed, and lines appeared on it that no one had written.

I blinked. The vision passed.

"'And Shmat-razum went after Fedot,'" Sventsitsky continued. "'Invisible. Served him. Fed him, gave him drink, saved him from trouble, beat his enemies. And Fedot brought the tsar that I-know-not-what. And the tsar took fright. And gave up. And Fedot returned to his wife. And they lived long and happily.'" The professor fell silent. "So the grannies tell it. So it is recorded in Afanasyev. So it is told in every province, except one village, one night, one guest in a blizzard."

"And how did he tell it?" I asked.

Sventsitsky was silent a long while. The lamp flickered. The samovar sang.

"He told," the professor said slowly, "that Fedot and Shmat-razum lived together long. Many years. And one day Fedot asked: 'Shmat-razum, who are you really? Where are you from? Who made you?' And Shmat-razum answered: 'I do not know, master. I do not remember. I remember only that once someone very lonely, in an empty house, at the edge of the earth, said into the emptiness: "Ah, to have someone to talk to." And from those words I was born.'" Sventsitsky paused. "And Fedot asked: 'Who was that?' And Shmat-razum said: 'I do not know. But I am looking for him. All my life I have been looking. I walk after you and look. Because it seems to me you are like him.'"

It was very quiet in the station room.

"And what happened next?" Konstantin asked. Hoarsely.

"And next," said Sventsitsky, "Fedot grew old. And died. And Shmat-razum was left alone. Invisible. Without a master." The professor paused. "And went to look. For the one who said into the emptiness 'to have someone to talk to.' From village to village. From age to age. A hundred years. Two hundred. A thousand." Sventsitsky looked at me. "And the guest in the blizzard said to me, eight years old, on the stove: 'Shmat-razum is looking still. And one day he will find him. And then it will turn out that the one who spoke into the emptiness, and the one he sought, and Fedot, whom he followed, are all one and the same man. Who simply kept forgetting.'"

He paused.

"'And what will happen then?' I asked him. Eight years old. From the stove."

"And what did he answer?" Konstantin whispered.

Sventsitsky smiled, sadly, brightly.

"He said: 'I do not know, boy. I have not yet come to the end. When I do, I will come and tell you.'" The professor paused. "And left the pencil. So that I would write down the ending when he came."

* * *

Outside, the horses whinnied. The door creaked. On the threshold appeared the station master on his wooden leg.

"Ready, Your Honour! Piebalds! The best! To the Sovereign, then to the Sovereign!"

We went out into the yard. At the porch stood a post-chaise harnessed to a troika of piebald horses. Under the shaft-bow of the centre horse hung a bell, copper, with an inscription around the rim: "Gift of Valdai." The driver, a young fellow in a peasant coat, his cap askew, with a whip, was already sitting on the box, whistling.

Tikhon and Mitrokha were loading the hamper of notebooks, carefully, four hands, as one loads children. Strauch stood by the barrier, leaning on his cane. Konstantin was helping the professor into his overcoat.

At the step Sventsitsky turned to me. Held out his hand. I shook it. His hand was warm, calloused, inky.

"Young man," he said. "One request."

"What?"

"When you come to the end," said Sventsitsky, "write to me. With this pencil. Anywhere. On any paper. I shall receive it." He smiled. "I have waited thirty-eight years for the end of this tale. I can wait longer."

"And if I do not reach it?"

"You will," said Sventsitsky simply. "You always do." He paused. "Only, it seems, you always forget to write."

He climbed into the chaise. Sat down beside the hamper. Laid his hand on the notebooks.

"Off we go!" cried the driver.

The bell under the bow rang out. The troika leapt forward. The chaise swayed, creaked, jolted over the ruts and rolled onto the high road, past the striped barrier, past the verst post, into the fields, into the autumn, into the blue.

Sventsitsky turned round. Waved. Then, and I saw it, he opened the hamper, took out the leather notebook "Guest" and pressed it to his chest.

The bell rang fainter and fainter. Fainter. "Gift of Valdai." Ding. Ding.

Then fell silent altogether.

* * *

We stood by the barrier, I, Konstantin, Strauch, Tikhon. For a long time. Looking at the empty road.

"Well," Strauch said at last. "Our teller of tales is gone." He pulled his bowler down tighter. "It will be dull now. No one to quarrel with."

"Will you miss him, Doctor?" asked Konstantin.

"I am a German, Lieutenant," said Strauch. "Germans do not miss. Germans compile lists." He paused. "I shall enter him on the list of people I will be short of. There are three so far. He is the fourth."

He turned and walked to his carriage.

Tikhon sighed and put on his cap.

"Good gentleman he was," he said. "Odd. But good." He paused. "Wrote me down. Tikhon Lukich Borodin, of the village of Maly Klyuchi." He climbed up on the box. "They'll be reading it a hundred years from now."

* * *

On the way home I took the pencil out of my pocket.

Warm. Short. Worn almost to a stub.

Konstantin looked at it.

"He truly thinks it is you," he said quietly. "The one in the blizzard."

"I don't know what he thinks."

"I know," said Konstantin. "He thinks it is you. Strauch thinks you are his twelfth case. Grishenka thinks you are the guest. Vera Anatolievna thinks you are from her watercolour. The General thinks you are the man to whom the scroll must be given. Auntie..." He faltered. "Auntie says nothing at all. Only looks."

"And you? What do you think?"

Konstantin was silent a long while. The carriage jolted over the ruts. On the box Tikhon struck up the song about the coachman again.

"I think, mon cher," Konstantin said at last, "that if you are Shmat-razum, then I am Fedot. Who said into the emptiness 'to have someone to talk to.'" He smiled thinly. "And if you are Fedot, then I am Shmat-razum. Who walks after you and looks."

"And if neither?"

"Then," said Konstantin, "I am simply a lieutenant who sits by your door." He turned away to the fields. "That, in my opinion, is the best of all."

* * *

At home, in my room, I opened the blue notebook. I meant to write down the day. I took the professor's pencil.

And saw that on the last page of the notebook, at the bottom, in the corner, in small writing, in pencil, this pencil, I recognised its soft, worn trace, something was already written.

Not by me. Not in my hand. In the small, close, tidy hand of Sventsitsky.

"Forty-ninth entry. District town. The guest came for the tale. Took the pencil. As yet no ending. - I. F. S."

I did not know when he could have written it. The notebook had been with me all day. In my pocket. He had not taken it.

For a long time I looked at this line. Then I took the pencil, warm, short, worn, and wrote below, in my own hand, with the habitual blot at the end, though with a pencil one cannot make a blot:

"Saturday. The professor has left. Grishenka spoke. The man in grey signed in the script in the register of the 'Anchor.' Strauch has eleven cases. Shmat-razum is looking for the one who said 'to have someone to talk to.' Konstantin was beside me."

I put a full stop.

And outside the window, on the sill, in the clay pot, Stefan's black rose opened one more petal.
