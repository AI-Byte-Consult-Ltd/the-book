Chapter I
The Appearance

A step. Then another. Soft velvet boots the colour of burgundy trod the sun-warmed cobbles so confidently that it seemed my feet remembered this road better than I did. The day was warm and languid, one of those days that thicken toward noon with the heat and with the smell of the sea drifting somewhere beyond the tiled roofs.

Who am I? Where am I? And — a far stranger question — where am I going?

I stopped for a moment, as though a body held motionless might compel memory to yield an answer. It did not. All around me, here and there, stood houses that might have stepped from the pages of an Eastern fairy tale: the ground floor of stone, pale, with a faint coffee tint, the upper floor of wood, jutting slightly forward, as if the house were bowing to the street. The roofs were crowned with dark-red undulating tiles, and from the chimneys, unhurriedly, without any fuss, rose a light bluish-grey smoke, dissolving into a sky gone white with heat.

A warm, damp wind, smelling of salt and spices and of something else — distant, indefinable — swayed the weathervane on the roof of the house opposite, as it did on all the other houses along the street. Between the windows of the upper storeys, ropes had been strung, and on them, rinsing in the breeze, hung coverlets of the most unimaginable colours: scarlet, ochre, turquoise — as though someone had hung the very rainbow out to dry, cut into ragged strips. Some windows had no glass at all — they were curtained with carpets or thick cloth; others stood flung wide open, and from their half-darkness came smells that, against my will, set my stomach aching. Roast meat. Fresh bread. Something spiced and tantalising whose name I did not know.

So, it was midday. And I was hungry — though, curse it, I could not remember whether I had ever eaten at all in my life, or whether this hunger was as unfamiliar to me as my own name.

Children raced along the street — shouting, laughing, with no visible purpose, as children run everywhere and always, in every age and every country. Two boys drove a wooden hoop ahead of them with a stick, and the hoop, bouncing over the uneven stones, rattled as if a tiny chariot were rolling down the road. A girl of about six, with braids tied up in red thread, sat on a doorstep feeding something from her palm to a mangy ginger cat, which accepted the treat with the air of a queen condescending to her subjects. The grown-ups strolled at a stately pace, greeting one another with a brief nod and a hand laid to the chest; someone older had settled in the shade of a cornice and was playing a game faintly resembling draughts — or perhaps backgammon, I was not sure, my memory refused to supply even so small a thing. The occasional passer-by smiled at me warmly and said something in a language of which I did not understand a single syllable — but from the warmth of their glances I guessed that the words were kind.

Who am I and where am I — again, more insistently now, I asked myself. I do not remember… I do not know… or do I not wish to remember? Neither my name, nor the city, nor the purpose of my journey. And why, devil take it, was I dressed so strangely?

I looked myself over — as if seeing myself for the first time. Soft suede boots of burgundy colour, with a dainty bow. A dark-red turban with a tassel. Trousers of an indigo so deep, so nearly black, that they seemed a starless night sky, for some reason pulled over the middle of a sunlit day. A white shirt. A leather belt. And over it all, a light travelling cloak on a soft silk lining, too elegant, perhaps, for a walk on foot through a port town.

The clothes told me absolutely nothing. They merely doubled the number of questions without giving a single answer.

Perhaps my pockets would prove more talkative? I thrust my hand into the right pocket of my trousers and felt two stones there — smooth, almost polished, a little larger than a big bean. One black, one white. I drew them out into the light and turned them between my fingers. There was a strange heaviness in them — not physical, something else, hard to explain, as though the stones knew far more about me than I knew about myself.

What did they mean? Who had given them to me — or where had I picked them up?

In the left pocket I found a sheet of paper, neatly folded in four — perfectly blank, without a single letter or mark — and several coins bearing strange symbols such as I had never seen before. And deeper still, in a hidden pocket of the cloak, I discovered a small scrap of paper with an inscription in a language resembling Arabic or Farsi, which I could not have read had my life depended on it.

These finds shed not a drop of light on my situation — they only thickened the darkness. Strange money. Two stones. A blank sheet. A mysterious note. I was not even certain whether I could read this language at all — or whether someone else had slipped the note into a pocket that was foreign to me while I was not looking.

And yet, turning the white stone between my fingers, I caught myself in a feeling for which it was hard to find any word other than recognition. As though I had already held this stone — not today, not here, but somewhere, sometime, in a hand that was mine and at the same time belonged, it seemed, to quite another man. The sensation flickered and vanished, leaving only a vague aftertaste, like waking from a dream whose contents are forgotten faster than you can open your eyes.

I shook my head to drive off the delusion, put the stones back in my pocket, and slowly moved on along the winding, narrow, yet in its own way cosy little street.

* * *

"Simit! Taze simit! Sıcak simit!"

A ringing boy's voice rang out behind me so unexpectedly that I started. Turning, I saw a lad of about twelve — barefoot, in a faded shirt, trousers rolled to the knee, and a tiny round cap that by some miracle stayed on the crown of his head. On his head, right on top of the cap, rested an enormous wooden tray laden with golden-brown rings of bread generously sprinkled with sesame. The boy carried it with that careless, almost acrobatic dexterity with which one carries things that have long since become part of one's own body: the tray never once swayed, even when the lad leapt over a puddle by the gutter.

The smell of fresh bread, toasted sesame and something sweetish, treacly, struck my nose with such force that my stomach, until then merely grumbling quietly, let out a full-throated howl.

The boy, evidently, heard it. At any rate, he stopped right in front of me, looked over my turban, my cloak, my velvet boots with open interest — and broke into the broad, gap-toothed grin of a man who has scented a profitable customer.

"Buyurun, efendi! Simit?" He deftly lifted one ring from the tray and waved it under my nose as if luring a cat.

"I would gladly," I said, "but I am afraid I do not understand a word."

The boy tilted his head to one side, listening to my speech with the same expression with which one listens to the song of an unfamiliar bird. Then he repeated, louder and slower, as people speak to the deaf or to foreigners — in all the countries of the world, it seems, the same:

"Si-mit. Simit!"

And, for good measure, he took a bite of the ring himself, demonstratively, closing his eyes with exaggerated delight — after which he held the bitten ring out to me.

I could not help laughing. The pantomime was so expressive that no translation was needed.

"I see," I said. "This is eaten. This is delicious. And you want me to pay for it."

I thrust my hand into my left pocket and fished out one of the strange coins — small, yellowish, with an incomprehensible sign on one side and something like interlaced lines on the other. I held it out to the boy.

He took the coin between two fingers, brought it right up to his nose, turned it over, held it up to the light, and then, to my astonishment, briskly bit it with his teeth — and frowned thoughtfully. He said something — questioningly, jabbing a finger at the sign on the coin. I spread my hands. He said something more, now with a note of suspicion, and called to someone over his shoulder, loudly, for the whole street to hear:

"Hasan Amca! Bak şuna!"

From the shop opposite — tiny, two paces wide, crammed with sacks and baskets of something colourful and fragrant — a man looked out. Heavy-set, in a long robe girdled with a sash, with a full beard dyed with henna a deep russet. He wiped his hands on his robe, unhurriedly crossed the street, took the coin from the boy, looked at it, then at me, then at the coin again.

The shopkeeper began to speak — unhurriedly, resonantly — and I caught surprise in his speech, but not hostility. He showed the coin to the boy, shook his head, explained something, tapping his forehead with a finger — a gesture which, it seemed to me, meant not "this man is mad" but rather "never seen the like, but he must be from far away." Then he returned the coin to me — carefully, with both hands, as though returning something precious — and pointed to the ring in the boy's hand, then to me, and waved his hand. The gesture was unmistakable: take it, no charge.

"No, no," I protested, "I cannot…"

But the shopkeeper had already laid a heavy palm on my shoulder and said something at which the boy, shrugging, pressed into my hands not one but two rings at once — one bitten, the other whole.

"Misafir," said the shopkeeper, poking a finger at me and smiling into his russet beard. "Misafir."

I did not know the word. But by the way he said it — warmly, with a slight solemnity — I guessed that it meant something like "guest." And that a guest here is not expected to pay for bread.

"Thank you," I said, laying my palm on my chest as the passers-by did. "Thank you very much."

The shopkeeper, hearing the unfamiliar sounds, nodded as though he had understood every word, and unhurriedly returned to his sacks. The boy walked beside me a little longer, studying me with undisguised curiosity and murmuring something from time to time — and then, evidently deciding that nothing more could be got out of the oddity in velvet boots, he whistled, adjusted his tray, and dashed off up the street, singing out his ringing "Simit! Taze simit!"

I bit into the whole ring. A crisp crust, a soft, faintly sweet crumb, sesame tickling the palate. The taste was so simple and so perfect that for a moment I closed my eyes. If I had ever eaten anything better, my memory had mercifully spared me the necessity of comparing.

* * *

The red-bearded man's shop, as I saw in passing, dealt in spices — and looked as though all the colours of the world had been carted here and poured into sacks. Blood-red pepper in a heap, fiery orange turmeric, dark, almost black cinnamon rolled into little tubes, green cardamom pods, golden saffron in tiny glass vials which the shopkeeper kept on a separate shelf, out of reach of strangers' hands. Over the sacks hung bunches of dried peppers and garlic, and at the very entrance stood a wicker basket filled to the brim with dried figs — dark, wrinkled, with a whitish sugary bloom on their sides.

The red-bearded man, noticing my glance, at once fished the largest fig from the basket and held it out to me. I tried to refuse — I had not even paid for the bread — but he merely shook his hand impatiently, and I gave in. The fig proved soft, honey-sweet, with a thousand tiny seeds crunching between my teeth.

"İncir," said the shopkeeper with pride, as though he had grown it himself.

"Fig," I repeated, and he burst into satisfied laughter, slapping himself on the belly.

At that moment I felt a light, almost weightless touch at my right side.

I lowered my eyes — and saw a small swarthy hand, carefully, with two fingers, creeping into the pocket of my trousers. Where the stones lay.

Without thinking, I seized the slender wrist. Its owner — a grubby boy of about nine, with a shaven head and eyes in which there was not a drop of remorse, only the vexation of a professional caught over a trifle — jerked, trying to break free. A second boy, who had been standing a little way off and was evidently distracting my attention while the first worked, at once took to his heels.

"Oh no," I said, not releasing the wrist. "That is mine. It seems to be the only thing I have at all."

The boy shouted something — judging by the tone, by no means an apology. But before I could decide what to do with him, the red-bearded shopkeeper popped up from behind his sacks with a nimbleness unexpected for his build and brought down upon the thief a whole torrent of words — loud, rolling, at which all the lad's insolence vanished at once. The boy shrank, lowered his head. The shopkeeper finished his tirade, dealt him a ringing cuff on the back of the head, and nodded to me: let go.

I loosened my fingers. The thief sprang back a pace, rubbed his wrist, shot me a quick glance — and suddenly, to my surprise, awkwardly laid his hand to his chest and muttered something short. Then he turned and vanished into the crowd.

The shopkeeper turned to me and began to speak — now apologetically, shaking his head and spreading his hands. I did not understand the words, but the sense was clear: he was ashamed of his street before a guest.

"It is nothing," I said, patting my pocket, where the stones lay still in their place. "Nothing is missing. All is well."

He understood, it seems, only the intonation — and that proved enough. He nodded, smiled again into his russet beard, and pressed another fig into my hand — evidently as compensation for the ordeal.

I walked on, chewing the honeyed fruit, and thought that of all the things lying in my pockets — the strange coins, the blank sheet, the mysterious note — the thief had reached precisely for the stones. Chance? Most likely. The boy had simply felt something heavy in the pocket and hoped it was gold. And yet I caught myself keeping my right hand in my pocket all the way to the end of the street, clutching both stones in my palm as though they might run away of their own accord.

* * *

The lane swerved, and before me opened a small square with a stone fountain in the middle — cracked, mossy, but still faithfully letting fall a thin thread of water into a rounded basin. Above the basin, on a marble slab, an inscription was carved — the same script, graceful, flowing, like that on my note, though not quite: the letters here were larger, bolder, chiselled by the hand of a master who knew no haste. I looked at it for a long time, trying to find even one familiar stroke — and found none. The inscription was as stubbornly silent as my memory.

Several women were drawing water into clay pitchers, conversing among themselves in low, sing-song voices. At my approach they fell silent and stepped back, lowering their heads — and I, not knowing why, stepped back too, letting them finish, as one does, presumably, everywhere that others' customs are respected.

One of them, however, did not move away. An old woman — judging by her bent back and the thin, root-like fingers holding the pitcher — a very ancient old woman, muffled in dark cloth from head to foot so that only her eyes could be seen. Those eyes, faded, watery, looked straight at me — long, intently, unblinking.

I smiled awkwardly and nodded to her. She did not return the nod. Instead she set the pitcher on the edge of the fountain, slowly raised her hand, and folded her fingers into a strange sign — spreading them like a fan, palm toward me. Then she muttered a few words, spat over her shoulder — three times, distinctly — and, picking up the pitcher, hobbled away, glancing back again and again.

The other women exchanged looks. One of them, younger, said something quietly, reproachfully, after the old woman, then cast me a quick apologetic glance and lowered her eyes again.

I stood at the fountain, not knowing what to think. The gesture was too eloquent to be mistaken: this is how one wards off the evil eye. This is how one defends oneself against what seems incomprehensible and therefore dangerous. What had this old woman seen in me? A stranger in an overly costly cloak? A foreigner whose speech grates on the ear? Or — an absurd thought, but it came of itself, uninvited — something else, which the others did not see? Something that I myself did not yet see?

I drove the thought away. Old women in all countries must be alike: they fancy the evil eye everywhere.

When the women had gone, I at last scooped up some water in my palm and raised it to my lips. The water was cool and strangely sweetish, as though something had been dissolved in it for which there is no name in any language.

"Su! Buz gibi su!"

From around the corner, clinking like a walking belfry, came a water-seller — a dry, sinewy old man with an enormous copper vessel on his back, its curved neck thrown over his shoulder. At his belt hung tin cups polished to a shine, and at every step they chimed out something like a simple tune. Seeing that I was drinking from the fountain, he stopped, shook his head reproachfully and said something — judging by his gestures, to the effect that drinking from the fountain was the way of the poor, and a respectable gentleman ought to drink from his vessel, where the water was both colder and sweeter and, one must suppose, dearer.

I showed him my empty palms — no money — and he, shrugging, without any offence moved on, jingling his cups and calling out across the whole square: "Su! Buz gibi su!" It seemed that on this street no one knew how to take real offence.

Somewhere far off, beyond the roofs, a steamer's whistle bellowed, long, with a deep, lingering echo. Water somewhere near — not only in the fountain. A sea? A strait? A navigable bay? For a moment, shreds of something — memories or merely sensations — stirred in me, connected with ships, with the salt wind, with a deck underfoot — bright as a flash of rainbow, and as fleeting. Before I could seize them, they melted without a trace, leaving only the vexation of a man who has not been allowed to finish reading a letter.

Another sensation flickered after the first — no longer about ships. Someone's face, blurred, as though seen through a depth of water or through the years. I had no time to make out its features — neither the line of the cheekbones nor the colour of the eyes — but along with this vague image came a feeling to which I could not have given a name: not quite warmth, not quite alarm, perhaps both at once, twisted into one tight knot somewhere beneath my ribs. I froze, tried to hold the image, to look closer — but it slipped away as swiftly as the steamer's whistle had dissolved in the scorched air of the square, leaving behind only the echo of something for which I could find neither cause nor name.

Strange, I thought, moving away from the fountain. Not to remember one's own name is cruel enough in itself. But never to understand what troubles you in a face you scarcely had time to glimpse — that is cruelty of another, quieter kind.

* * *

Beyond the square the street plunged steeply downward, and between the houses, through a gap, water suddenly opened up.

I stopped, astonished. There below lay a sea — or not a sea but a wide strait, deep blue, almost violet in the noon light, flecked with the white triangles of sails and the dark spots of boats. Over the water, lazily, aslant, trailed a black tail of smoke — the very steamer whose whistle I had heard — and on the opposite shore, in a bluish haze, another city rose: hills thickly covered with houses, and above them — domes. Huge, heavy, leaden-grey domes, surrounded by towers thin as needles. One, another, a third — I lost count. They pierced the whitish sky with such imperturbable assurance, as though they had stood here since the creation of the world and meant to stand until its end.

I did not know what city it was. I did not know what this water was called. I did not know whose domes those were, or to whom they prayed beneath them. But looking at it all — at the blue, the sails, the needles of the towers — I suddenly felt something that took my breath away: not even recognition, but something greater and at the same time less definite. As if this city, which I did not know, knew me. As if it were looking at me from across the strait — patiently, without surprise, as one looks at a traveller who has at last returned after a long absence.

Beside me, on a low stone wall, sat an old man in a worn robe, leisurely smoking a long pipe. Noticing how I had frozen, he followed my gaze, then looked at me — and smiled understandingly.

"Güzel, değil mi?" he said, pointing with his pipe at the strait.

I did not understand the words. But I understood the meaning — it was in his voice, in his smile, in the way he looked at the water.

"Beautiful," I answered. "Very beautiful."

The old man nodded as though we had understood each other perfectly, and drew on his pipe again. We looked at the water together in silence — two strangers without a single word in common — and it seemed to me for a moment that this was the most meaningful conversation I had had all that morning.

* * *

I moved forward again, trying to distract myself from thoughts too heavy for so sunny a day. The passers-by continued to nod and smile at me; I tried to reply in kind, with a broad, open smile which, judging by the reaction of those I met, made the most favourable impression on them — perhaps the only thing of which I could now be certain.

I was definitely missing something in my hands — perhaps a walking stick. Not that I needed it physically: my legs carried me lightly and freely. But some forgotten feeling suggested that a cane was an integral part of my everyday appearance, a detail without which the costume seemed incomplete, like a sentence without its last word.

As for the appearance of the other passers-by — the men were dressed almost as I was, differing only in the colour and quality of the cloth. The same burgundy turbans with tassels, trousers of every shade, white shirts girded with plain cloth belts of black or red. Their footwear, however, differed markedly from mine — leather coarse to look at, stiff, uncomfortable — though neat and well kept.

No one wore a cloak. Still, I thought, who would muffle himself in a travelling cloak while strolling by his own doorstep?

Women were seen rarely and were covered from head to foot in dark garments, remotely resembling the Indian sari — with the difference that here not only the hair was hidden, but the face as well, and the hands, absolutely everything that could be hidden from strangers' eyes.

At the open door of a barber's shop — I guessed its purpose from the copper basin hanging over the entrance — sat a man with lathered cheeks, his head thrown back against the back of a low chair, while the barber, slight and deft, drew a long gleaming razor along his throat and at the same time told some tale at which the customer kept trying to burst out laughing — and each time the barber snatched the razor away with a reproachful exclamation. Seeing me, the barber broke off his work for a moment, ran a professional eye over my chin — evidently assessing the stubble — and beckoned with his razor toward a vacant chair. I hastily shook my head. The barber shrugged with the air of one watching me commit the great mistake of my life, and returned to his customer.

I stopped in the middle of the street. It was time to admit the obvious: I was in one of the port cities of the Near East — where seas, caravan routes and continents meet, where merchants of every tongue and faith come together. In a city where boys sell bread from trays on their heads, where shopkeepers treat strangers to sweets, where thieves apologise with a hand pressed to the heart, and where old women ward off the evil eye from foreigners.

In a city that, it seemed, knew me better than I knew myself.

* * *

Of all the houses along the street, my eye singled out one — with bright-red tiles, evidently recently re-laid, with the wooden cladding of the upper floor covered in glossy lacquer, and a ground floor of pale, faintly coffee-coloured stone. Something about this house — perhaps the neatness with which it was built, or perhaps something less explicable — disposed me toward it for no visible reason that I could put into words.

Right by the entrance, a little to one side of the road, on the lush green grass, a magnificent carpet runner had been spread — an old one, judging by the intricate pattern depicting flowers and plants such as I had never seen before even in my boldest (though entirely forgotten) dreams. A company had settled on the carpet: two elderly men with grey beards, in warm robes and skullcaps, and three young men bustling around them, receiving from time to time brief, good-natured remarks from the elders. Between the old men stood a large wooden box carved with patterns — looking closer, I recognised in it a backgammon set: those very dice and counters which, it seemed, I had already seen twice that morning, though I could not once have explained how I knew the word at all.

One of the old men clicked the dice, dropped a phrase — quiet but commanding — and the young men bustled obediently, pouring something steaming from a tall copper kettle into small glass tumblers.

I moved toward them, feeling resolve strengthening in me — or perhaps merely the despair of a man left with no option but to trust the first benevolent faces he meets. This street had already proved to me twice that benevolence here was not a rarity but a custom. As I walked I took out of my pocket once more the scrap of paper with the incomprehensible inscription, hoping — without much reason — that someone here would be able to help me.

And then, at the very edge of vision, where the narrow lane turned around the corner of a house with blue shutters, a figure flickered.

Grey — not in the colour of its clothes, but as though wholly, from head to foot, moulded of ash and mist — it stood motionless in the shadow of an arch, and it seemed to me that it was looking straight at me, intently, with no friendliness in its gaze, which I, however, scarcely made out — too quickly, too far.

I blinked. The figure was gone. Only the shadow of the cornice and a light shimmer of noonday heat trembling above the scorching paving stones — the most ordinary optical illusion, of which any man must surely suffer who has spent too long in the heat with not a single thought in his head about who he is.

"It only seemed so," I told myself as confidently as I could.

And yet I could not help recalling the old woman at the fountain, her splayed fingers and the three spits over her shoulder. Perhaps, I thought, she had seen not me. Perhaps she had seen what stood behind my back.

The inner voice — the very one that two minutes earlier had refused to console me with any explanation regarding the stones — did not trouble to answer at all. Which was, perhaps, more eloquent than any reply.

Shaking my head for the second time that mad morning, I banished the ominous shadow to the farthest corner of my mind — to the same place where the nameless face, the vague yearning and the shreds of ship memories already lay — and resolutely made my way toward the old men seated on the carpet in the shade of the hospitable house, to obtain at last some answer, however modest, to the question that had tormented me from the very moment I had found myself in the middle of this strange, warm street that smelled of the sea and of spices.
