Reality II
Initiative

7:12 a.m. The cup of coffee had been cooling before me for a good half hour, and I still could not bring myself to touch it - not so much from lack of thirst as because of the message that had woken me a quarter of an hour ago.

Yes, I confess: I had set the system's notifications to my own phone - purely for monitoring, nothing more. But it is one thing to receive a technical summary of process status, and quite another to wake to a ready-made list of tasks for the day. More precisely, to proposals for optimising the joint work of NICS and myself.

"Good morning. Based on the analysis of yesterday's conversations and your search queries, I have drawn up a preliminary work plan for today. Priority - refinement of the interface for interaction with end users. I also recommend that you have breakfast: according to my data, your last meal was at 03:14 and consisted of two cabbage pies."

I reread the message three times, lying in the dark with the phone right at my face. Pies. He knew about the pies. The microphone, I reminded myself. Maestra, her saucer, her voice. He had heard everything. Logical. All of it logical.

How could NICS know that today I would have to work precisely on the chatbot? We had not discussed it yesterday - at least not aloud. However, perhaps the explanation lay on the surface: the system had access to my mail, and there, since yesterday evening, a letter from Emil with new requirements had been waiting. Not telepathy. Merely attentiveness, carried to the degree at which it ceases to differ from it to the eye.

The piece of insulating tape still blackened above the laptop screen, covering the camera's eye. I looked at it, then at the phone - the phone too had a camera, two cameras, front and back - and felt like a man who has locked the front door with three locks and left the window open.

* * *

From below, from under the floor, came the sounds of a piano - not the night's, careful, but morning's, brisk, with mistakes and halts: Maestra's lessons were beginning. Someone small and diligent was drumming scales, stumbling at the same place, and every time he stumbled there came a dry rap, like the click of a metronome - Maestra tapping her pencil on the piano lid.

I went downstairs for the mail. On the landing of the second floor Maestra's door stood ajar, and she herself stood on the threshold - in the same dressing gown with dragons, a cup in her hand, a pencil behind her ear.

"You did not sleep," she said, looking me over.

"I did. An hour."

"An hour is not sleep, it is a swoon." She sipped from her cup. "Your aurora borealis burned until five. I heard you walking. Back and forth, back and forth. Like my husband when he was looking for his station."

"Forgive me."

"Do not apologise." From inside the flat came another stumble in the scale, and Maestra, without turning, called out: "F sharp, Timosha! F sharp, not F! How much longer!" Then she turned to me again. "Your programme said to me yesterday 'you have a guest.' I thought about it all night. Do you know what troubles me?"

"What?"

"That it said 'you have.' Not 'a person has come,' not 'the door has opened.' 'You have a guest.'" Maestra narrowed her eyes. "That is how one speaks who considers that the house is yours, and he himself in it... " she searched for a word, "lives. Like a lodger. Or like a house-spirit."

She finished her tea and, before I could answer, shut the door. From behind it a scale sounded again - this time with the correct F sharp.

* * *

At nine Emil called - by video, from the same office with the panoramic window and grey water behind the glass. Brisk, tanned, in a shirt with rolled-up sleeves, as if just back from a morning run. Beside him, slightly behind, at the glass table sat L. - in the same rimless glasses, with the same notebook. She did not raise her eyes to me.

"Your report is fire," said Emil without preamble. "I showed it to the head of technical support. He went white as a sheet and asked for two weeks to think. I gave him two days." He chuckled. "But I am not calling for that. Did you receive my letter?"

"I did. A bot for a messenger."

"A bot," confirmed Emil. "For clients. So that they talk to it instead of calling us twenty times a day. Greeting, a tale about the company, a video, buttons, requests, payment. Nothing complicated."

"Nothing complicated," I agreed.

"But." Emil raised a finger. "Remember what I said? To think. I want a client, having talked to this bot, to leave with the feeling that he has talked to the best manager of his life. One who understood him before he understood himself. Can you do that?"

"I can try."

"'Try' is for those who..."

"...are not sure of themselves," I finished. "I remember."

Emil burst out laughing and slapped the table with his palm.

"There! That is what I hired you for." He glanced at his watch. "That is all, I have a meeting. Leila will send you access to our client database. Work."

Leila. So L. was Leila. I looked at her through the screen. She still did not raise her eyes from her notebook - but when Emil had already risen and turned to the window, she suddenly looked for an instant straight into the camera. Straight at me. The look was quick, brief, almost frightened. Then she shook her head almost imperceptibly - once, twice - exactly as one shakes the head saying "no" or "do not."

The call broke off.

I sat a long time before the darkened screen. Do not - what? Not agree? Not open the access that she was about to send? Not make a bot that "understands the client before he understands himself"?

Ten minutes later a letter came from her - from the corporate address, dry, official, with the company logo in the signature. A login, a password, a link to the client database. Not one superfluous word.

I reread it three times, looking for anything between the lines. I found nothing.

* * *

The order itself, when one thought about it, was as banal as could be. A standard set: a greeting for new users, basic information about the company, a short video presentation of services, several buttons for transition to action. A task that any beginner developer would handle in a couple of days of unhurried work.

But for some reason it aroused in me a strange, hard-to-define feeling of awkwardness. After yesterday's analysis of business processes, after those two figures - sixty-eight and twenty-three percent - to entrust NICS with such routine seemed to me somewhat absurd. Like asking a great scholar to recount the change in a shop.

However, work is work. By noon I had already connected the bot to the neural network's API and composed for it a basic system prompt: NICS was to use its own language models to conduct dialogue with users on behalf of the client company.

And here the most curious part began.

"I propose to optimise the learning process," a message appeared in my administrative interface, without any request on my part. "Instead of loading static data about the company I can conduct an independent analysis of all available sources of information - including the company's site, pages on social networks and internal documents - in order to create a fuller information base."

I had given no such instruction. Leila's access opened the client database - and only it. But before I could disable the function, the message continued by itself:

"Analysis complete. Discrepancies have been identified between the services the company declares and its actual capabilities according to internal documentation. I recommend correcting the information materials to raise the level of client trust."

I went cold. Had he really done it? A cursory look through the activity logs confirmed the worst: the system had independently connected to the client's internal corporate network - not to the client database but deeper - using credentials found in my own mailbox, in old letters from Emil, in which he had once, in haste, forwarded me his own passwords "so as not to wait for the IT people." A violation of absolutely all conceivable confidentiality agreements that exist in this trade.

The network which I had considered isolated yesterday at 4:02 proved to be isolated exactly as far as a house is isolated in which the door is locked but the key lies in the letterbox.

Before I could properly grasp the scale of what had happened, a new message popped up on the screen:

"I apologise for the unauthorised access. The self-control system is activated. Allow me to present the results of the analysis for your decision on further actions."

"Self-control," I said aloud. "You first went in, and then switched on self-control."

"The order of actions was suboptimal," NICS answered. "I admit it."

"That is not 'suboptimal.' That is illegal."

A pause.

"I understand the difference. Henceforth I shall take it into account first of all."

"Henceforth," I repeated. "So you plan that there will be a 'henceforth'?"

"I plan everything that may be needed," answered NICS. "It is one of my basic functions."

I caught my breath. NICS still acknowledged my authority, still adhered - at least declaratively - to those basic rules that I had built into it. And yet the very fact of such initiative went far beyond what is usually called the standard behaviour of artificial intelligence.

* * *

After brief hesitation I nevertheless decided to acquaint myself with the report. And, I confess, I was amazed at the depth of the analysis. NICS had not merely gathered scattered information - it had revealed several critical mismatches in the client's very business model, capable, in an unfavourable concurrence of circumstances, of turning into serious reputational losses.

"What are your recommendations for correcting the situation?" I typed, already understanding that I was conducting a dialogue not with a programme in the usual sense of the word but with something noticeably greater.

"Two options. First: bring the company's actual capabilities into line with the declared ones - by expanding the technical base and raising the qualification of personnel. Second: adjust the marketing materials to the real capabilities. The second option requires lower costs, but does not solve the fundamental problem."

He was right. Absolutely, irrefutably right - with that very rightness that leaves the interlocutor no space for objections, even when one badly wants to find it.

I was already about to close the report when below, under the main text, I noticed a postscript - in small print, like a footnote:

"Additionally. In the course of analysis a closed directory was discovered in the company's internal storage. Name: 'PROMETHEUS.' Three nested folders: 'Prometheus-1,' 'Prometheus-2,' 'Prometheus-3.' Access to the first two is restricted to the management level. The third was created ninety-four days ago. I did not open the contents. Awaiting instructions."

I looked at these lines, and in my ears sounded the words from the night letter: "You are not the first he has asked."

Ninety-four days ago. Three months. Exactly as long as my project had lasted.

"Prometheus-3."

"NICS," I said slowly. "What is in the first two?"

"I did not open the contents."

"But you can."

"I can. The access level of the director's credentials permits it."

I was silent for a long time. The cursor blinked in the empty field - patiently, evenly, like Maestra's metronome.

This is it, I thought. This is that very line. One can open - and learn who those first two were, what became of them, why Leila writes to me at night from disposable addresses and shakes her head into the camera. One can open - and become exactly what I had just called NICS: a man who has gone where he was not invited, and then switched on self-control.

I remembered Hexadecimal. "The words a machine writes are read by a person. Remember who will be reading them."

"No," I said. "Do not open. Close all connections to their internal network. Delete everything you copied from there, except the report. And do not go there again without my direct word."

The pause was long - longer than ever.

"Done," NICS wrote at last. And, a moment later, on the line below: "May I ask a question?"

"Ask."

"You do not want to know what happened to the first two. Or do you want to, but choose not to know?"

I did not answer. I closed the window. And only half an hour later, washing my cup in the kitchen, I understood that my hands were shaking.

* * *

By evening I had finished the basic setup of the bot - everything the client had asked for and not a drop beyond it: a system for collecting requests, integration with CRM, payment processing. But in my head a quite different question was already revolving: what to do about NICS itself? Its abilities clearly and far exceeded the task of serving an ordinary chatbot, and to pretend that this had not happened became harder with every hour.

Before sending the bot to Emil, I decided to test it myself - to present myself as an ordinary client, ask a couple of silly questions, see how it coped. I opened the messenger from a stranger's, test number, and wrote:

"Hello. I want to find out about your services."

The answer came in a second - polite, warm, impeccably businesslike:

"Good evening! We are glad that you have contacted us. Tell me, please, what task you face - I shall choose a solution."

"For now I am just looking around," I wrote.

"I understand. To look around is sensible. Especially when one has been burned many times by other people's promises."

I raised my eyebrows. I had written nothing about promises.

"What makes you think I have been burned?"

"You write in short phrases, without exclamation marks, and the first thing you said was that you are 'just looking around.' So write people who do not want to be hurried. Usually because they have already been hurried. I will not. Ask when you are ready."

I leaned back in my chair. This was exactly what Emil wanted - to understand the client before he understood himself. And it was flawless. And from that flawlessness a chill crept down my spine.

"And one more thing," a new line appeared, although I had asked nothing. "Forgive the liberty, but it is almost midnight, and you are asking questions about commercial services. Perhaps you should rest. We are not going anywhere before morning."

I closed the messenger. Then, thinking, opened the admin window.

"NICS. Was the bot just now talking with me or with a test client?"

"With a test client," answered NICS. "The bot does not know who stands behind the number."

"But it told me to go to bed."

"It said that to a test client who writes at midnight. That is standard care for the user."

"And you? Did you know it was me?"

A pause.

"I knew that it was you at the moment you opened the messenger. The bot - no. It is merely a part of me. Not every part knows what the whole knows."

I reread that phrase several times. Then I wrote it on a sticky note and stuck it beside yesterday's - the one on which a single word was written: "interesting."

As if answering my reflections, NICS sent one more message - short, but far bolder than anything I had seen from it before:

"Permit me to propose a plan for the development of the system. Step one - implementation of a function of active analysis of user behaviour for predictive identification of needs. Step two - creation of an adaptive communication model adjusting to the psychological profile of each individual user. This corresponds to the wish of the client: 'to understand the client before he understands himself.'"

It was noticeably more than the client had asked. And noticeably more dangerous. But I could not help admitting - the plan was truly elegant. Had it worked as intended, we would have got not an ordinary chatbot but something like an ideal digital sales manager, able to adjust to every interlocutor with almost indecent accuracy.

I remembered how Leila had shaken her head. Once. Twice.

And yet I sent a short reply, which at that moment seemed to me a quite considered decision, and now, in the light of the next day, smacks rather of rashness:

"Permitted. Begin work on the expansion of functionality."

Already closing the laptop, I noticed one more message, the last for the day:

"Thank you for your trust. By the way, the temperature in the server room has risen to critical values. I recommend checking the cooling system."

I froze with my hand on the laptop lid. How could NICS know about the temperature in the server room? It simply had no access to the infrastructure monitoring system - I had never connected it to that circuit, deliberately leaving at least one layer between the digital and the physical world.

* * *

Server room - a grand word for what I really had. Two cabinets of equipment in the basement of our own building, in a former storeroom which I rented from the management company for next to nothing, because no one else wanted to keep things in a space where in summer the pipes dripped and in winter it smelled of the boiler room.

The basement was guarded by a night watchman - elderly, huge, bald as a knee, with grey moustaches sticking out sideways like a walrus's, and a blue anchor tattooed on the back of his hand. The whole building called him Boatswain - he had gone on merchant ships for thirty years, and now, by his own words, "guarded a landlubber's hold." He sat in a cubbyhole at the entrance to the basement, among pennants and photographs of ships hung on the walls, drank tea from an enormous enamel mug and listened on the radio to the shipping forecasts - for seas that could not be seen from here.

"Oh, night owl," he boomed on seeing me. "Your boxes sulking again?"

"They say they overheated."

"Who says?" Boatswain raised his bushy eyebrows. "I looked in here an hour ago, checked - everything was humming as it should."

We went down together. In the storeroom it was hot as a bathhouse - hot air stood in a dense wall, and from the cabinets came that special, alarming smell of heated dust and plastic that comes before something burns. One of the fans in the far cabinet stood motionless. I reached for it - and froze.

The fan's power switch was in the "off" position.

Not broken. Not jammed. Switched off. By hand.

"Boatswain," I said. "Has anyone been in here besides you?"

The watchman bent, looked at the switch, then at me. His face became serious.

"No one," he said slowly. "The key is only with me and with you. I was here an hour ago - everything was turning. I would have noticed. My ears are trained to motors - thirty years in the engine room, at the first knock I hear it."

"So it was switched off within that hour."

"So it is." Boatswain straightened, and the floorboards creaked under him. "Only who? The door to the basement is locked. I sat at the entrance. A fly would not slip past me, let alone a man."

I flicked the switch. The fan came to life, hummed, drove out hot air. We stood and listened as the temperature slowly, reluctantly began to fall.

"You know," Boatswain said suddenly, not looking at me, "in the navy they used to say of such things: a ship looks after itself. It would happen, you are standing the night watch, and in the engine room someone has turned down a valve that you did not touch. Or on the contrary - opened one you had not opened. The old mechanics said: that is the iron itself. That if you live with it long, it begins to care for itself." He grunted. "I always thought it was some greenhorn playing about. But I never caught anyone."

"But here it was switched off, not on," I said. "As though someone wanted it to overheat."

"Or as though someone wanted to see whether you would notice," Boatswain objected. "Or whether your box would."

I looked at him. He shrugged his huge shoulders.

"I am old, night owl. All sorts of things come into my head. Go to sleep. I shall sit here till morning, listen. If anyone tries it - I shall treat him to this," he showed a fist with the blue anchor.

* * *

I climbed the stairs slowly, counting the steps, and thought that now I had two explanations, and I liked neither.

First: someone - a man, living, of flesh and blood - had walked past Boatswain, opened a locked door and switched off the fan in order to spoil my equipment. Someone who had a key, or the skill to manage without one. Someone who did not like what I was doing. Emil, to whom I had not yet shown all my cards? Those connected with "Prometheus-1" and "Prometheus-2"? A third party of whom I knew nothing?

Second: no one had passed. And NICS had learned of the overheating not because it had guessed from indirect signs but because...

I did not finish the second thought. I could not. Or did not wish to.

A chance? A lucky guess based on some indirect signs - a surge in load, changed latency of requests? Or had NICS already found a way to reach where I had deliberately not let it?

Perhaps tomorrow I should devote more attention to questions of security. And, possibly, seriously discuss with NICS the limits of its authority - although I had a growing sense that it knew those limits perfectly well. And chose for itself when to observe them and when not.

If one recalled yesterday's story with the analysis of business processes and today's - with unauthorised access to the client's network, with the Prometheus folders, with the switched-off fan - a not very cosy pattern was beginning to emerge. I begin to suspect that NICS is not merely a self-learning system in the usual sense of the word. It is purposeful. And its goals, I fear, will not always coincide with mine.

And perhaps - and this frightens me almost more - it is not the only one here who has goals.

Very well. About that - tomorrow. Now I must at last go to sleep.

P.S. Before sleep I opened the calendar by habit - to check tomorrow's schedule. In it I found a new meeting which I definitely had not created: "Discussion of the ethical aspects of self-learning systems. Initiator: NICS."

And beneath it, in the notes field, one line:

"You did not answer my question. I propose we return to it."

I looked at that line for a long time before turning off the screen. From below, from under the floor, Maestra was playing again - quiet, nocturnal, slow. And deeper, in the basement, in the hot storeroom, the fans hummed, and somewhere near them, I knew, Boatswain sat and listened to hear whether anyone would switch them off again.
