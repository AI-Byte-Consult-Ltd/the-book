Chapter III
Pharaoh Menes

Peals of thunder, dry and waterless, had been rolling over the Empire since noon - a storm that would never break into rain, only into dust and stifling heat, as so often happened here in the Valley whenever the earth had waited too long for water and at last began to be angry at the sky for its silence. The red, heavy sun crept across the heavens slowly, as if reluctantly, and its hot rays licked the massive stone walls of the Palace as though tasting them before swallowing.

I sat on the throne - golden, enormous, studded with stones brought from the farthest reaches of the Empire - and listened to the headache that had plagued me for days on end, like an importunate petitioner unwilling to leave without an answer.

The bustle behind the doors did not cease for an instant: servants, slaves, priests with their endless prayers and requests - all that mechanism of power which revolved around me day and night, never asking whether I wished to be its axis.

* * *

"Do not move, my Pharaoh. The crocodile god does not like to be hurried."

The healer Hesira, a small, wizened old man with hands smelling of myrrh and honey, was winding a strip of fine linen around my head for the third time that morning. To the cloth, right above my forehead, a tiny clay crocodile was fastened - no longer than a finger, with gaping jaws into which Hesira, with all the solemnity of which he was capable, had placed a few grains of barley.

"Are you certain this will help?" I asked, trying not to move, as I had been told.

"I am certain of nothing, my Pharaoh," answered Hesira, with that serene honesty which alone allowed him to remain court healer under three Pharaohs in succession. "But this is how headaches were treated under the former sovereigns, and under the sovereigns before them. The crocodile holds the grain in its jaws, the grain holds the pain, and the linen holds the crocodile. If the pain does not leave, it means that it is stronger than the crocodile. Then we shall try honey with poppy."

"And if the poppy does not help either?"

"Then, my Pharaoh," Hesira tightened the last knot and stepped back, admiring his work, "I shall ask you what I ought to have asked from the very beginning. When did it begin? This pain."

I thought. The question was a simple one - the simplest of all that had been put to me that morning. And I did not know the answer.

"I do not remember," I said at last. "It seems it has always been."

Hesira gave me a long, attentive look - the look with which healers regard not the wound but the man who bears it.

"Always," he repeated quietly. "That is what they say, my Pharaoh, whose pain is not in the head. The head only transmits. Like a messenger who brings the tidings but does not write them."

"Who writes them, then?"

"That," said Hesira, gathering the vials and pouches of herbs into his chest, "is written in none of the healers' scrolls. Perhaps one should ask not a healer, but a dream. Dreams sometimes know what we do not."

He bowed and went out, leaving me with a clay crocodile on my brow and the feeling that I had just been examined far more deeply than I wished.

* * *

The Vizier Ptahhotep appeared with his report as he had appeared every morning throughout my reign - tall, thin, stooped from long years of bending over scrolls, with a face on which perpetual care had carved wrinkles deeper than age. Behind him two scribes carried baskets full of papyri, and I, seeing those baskets, could not help sighing. Ptahhotep's baskets were never light - not in good years, and still less in bad.

"My Pharaoh," he began, bowing, and at once lifted his eyes to my forehead. "I see that Hesira has again called the crocodile to his aid."

"Hesira says the pain is stronger than the crocodile," I answered. "Let us see whether it is stronger than your baskets. Begin."

Ptahhotep unrolled the first scroll.

"The border lands, my Pharaoh. At the Far Outposts, four raids in the past moon. Desert brigands drove off two hundred head of cattle from the village of Abu, burned a granary - fortunately an empty one - and carried off three children. The Outpost recovered the children on the third day, but the cattle were not returned."

"Southerners?"

"Not only. The southern country, still formally at peace, from time to time disturbs our southern frontiers with short raids - not bold enough to be called war, but frequent enough to keep the border garrisons in perpetual tension. Each time our warriors repulse them, and the uninvited guests flee, abandoning wagons, weapons and horses." Ptahhotep raised his eyes from the scroll. "But each time, my Pharaoh, they return. And each time a little bolder."

"You report this to me as though announcing not a victory but a reprieve."

"Because that is what it is, my Pharaoh," said Ptahhotep without a shadow of embarrassment. "The southerners are testing how weak we are. The drought makes us weaker with every month. Sooner or later they will come not with a band of brigands but with an army."

He unrolled the second scroll.

"The granaries. The stores in the capital's granaries are sufficient for three seasons at the present rate of distribution. In the granaries of the Delta, for two. In the Upper Land, for less than one. This year's harvest..." - he hesitated - "this year's harvest, my Pharaoh, in essence, does not exist. What was gathered barely covers the seed grain."

"And the temple granaries?"

Ptahhotep pursed his lips - barely perceptibly, but I noticed.

"The temple granaries are not accountable to me, my Pharaoh. As you know. But I can tell you what my scribes see on the roads." He unrolled a third scroll, smaller, covered in a hurried, uneven hand. "The peasants carry grain to the temples. The last of it. Not to the royal granaries - to the temples. Because the Temple promises them prayer, and the granary only a reckoning. And those who have no grain left at all carry the temples their land."

"Land?"

"The Temple buys up the allotments of the starving, my Pharaoh. For bread. A sack of grain for a field that fed a family for three generations. People agree because a sack of grain today weighs more than a field that bears nothing. In a single nome of the Delta alone, my scribes have counted forty-two such allotments in the last two moons."

I was silent for a long time. The clay crocodile on my brow suddenly seemed unbearably heavy.

"Forty-two allotments," I repeated. "In two moons. In one nome."

"In one nome of which I know, my Pharaoh. Of the others I have not yet been informed."

"Who buys on behalf of the Temple?"

"The Temple's scribes, my Pharaoh. Under the seal of the High Priest."

Priests. I pronounced the word to myself without hatred - rather with the weariness of a man who has watched the same play too many times to be surprised by a twist of the plot. Ever thirsting for more: gold, lands, obedience. In secret - and sometimes no longer so secretly - they dreamed of ruling themselves, and for the sake of that dream were ready for any cunning, any patient, years-long weaving of the threads from which a noose would one day be woven. And now the drought, which no one could command - neither Pharaoh, nor Temple, nor the gods themselves - was putting into their hands what no cunning could have obtained for them: land. Sack by sack. Field by field.

"Ptahhotep," I said. "Is it possible to forbid the Temple from buying land from the starving?"

The Vizier spread his hands - a gesture which over the long years had become almost ritual with him.

"It is possible, my Pharaoh. The decree will be written by evening. But then the starving will have to choose between a sold field and death by hunger. And they will know who took that choice from them. The Temple will see to it that they know."

"And if the royal granaries themselves were to give grain on credit against the future harvest? Without buying up land?"

Ptahhotep thought - for a long time, moving his lips as though recounting something in his head.

"The granaries will not suffice, my Pharaoh. For forty-two allotments - they will. For the whole Delta - no. Not for three seasons."

"Then for how long will they suffice?"

"For one. If there is a flood next year, we have won. If there is none..."

He did not finish. There was no need.

"Write the decree," I said. "Grain on credit against the future harvest, from the royal granaries, without the purchase of land. Begin with that nome where forty-two allotments were counted. Let all know that the Pharaoh gives bread without taking the field."

"And if there is no flood?"

"Then we shall starve together with them, Ptahhotep. But they will know that the Pharaoh did not buy their land with their own hunger."

The Vizier looked at me for a long time. Then, to my surprise, he bowed lower than custom required - almost as low as one bows before the gods.

"It shall be done, my Pharaoh," he said. And, already leaving, he added without turning: "My father served your predecessor. He used to say that the gods love good Pharaohs, but not for long. I hope he was mistaken."

* * *

By noon the nomarch Ankhtifi arrived.

I heard him before I saw him - a loud, rolling voice in the corridor, wrangling with the guards, the jingle of bracelets, the tramp of many feet. The doors flew open and he entered the hall: huge, broad-shouldered, with a short grey beard braided, in the custom of the southern nomes, into a tight plait, in a dusty travelling cloak over rich garments. Behind him came four of his men - and, apart from them, supported by one of the warriors, a boy of about twelve, with a bandaged arm and on his face that peculiar expression found in children who have seen too much for their years.

"My Pharaoh!" Ankhtifi sank to one knee - the only concession to etiquette that he allowed himself. "Forgive me for bursting in without announcement. But what I have brought you cannot wait for announcement."

"Rise, Ankhtifi," I said. "Speak."

He rose, and his eyes, small, deep-set, burned beneath his thick brows with a fury he did not even try to conceal.

"The village of Little Nekhen, on the very border of my nome," he said. "Four nights ago. The southerners came not in a band, as before, but in a detachment - fifty spears, perhaps more. They burned everything. Granaries, houses, boats. They drove off the cattle. Those who resisted, they killed. Those who did not resist, they carried off." He pointed to the boy. "This one is the only one who reached my fortress. He walked two days through the desert with a knife in his hand and a broken arm. Tell the Pharaoh, boy. Tell him what your name is."

The boy took a step forward. He did not bow - apparently he did not know how one bows to a Pharaoh, or perhaps he had simply forgotten - and I signalled to a guard who had started toward him to halt.

"My name is Paney," he said hoarsely. "Son of Hevi, the fisherman."

"What became of your father, Paney?"

The boy was silent for a long time. Then he answered, looking me straight in the eyes, in a level, lifeless voice in which no tears remained:

"He told me to run. And I ran."

The hall grew so quiet that I could hear the doves cooing in the inner courtyard beyond the walls.

"You are brave, Paney," I said. "Braver than many warriors I know. You will stay in the palace. You will be fed, and your arm will be tended. And when the arm has healed - if you wish - you will be taught to hold a spear. Or a scribe's brush. As you please."

The boy answered nothing. He looked at me - long, gravely - and it seemed to me that he was trying to decide whether to believe these words. I understood him. In his place I should not have believed either.

"My Pharaoh," Ankhtifi spoke again, and his voice thundered under the vaults of the hall. "I do not need favours for one boy. I need vengeance for the whole village. Give me an army. Give me a thousand spears - and I shall pass beyond the cataracts, to the very heart of the southern country, and burn there as many villages as they have burned of ours. As many and as many again. So that for three generations to come their mothers will frighten their children with my name."

"And what will you feed a thousand spears with beyond the cataracts, Ankhtifi?" I asked. "In a year when we ourselves have no bread?"

"With their bread, my Pharaoh! The drought has not touched their land as it has ours. Their granaries are full. We shall take their bread - and feed both the army and my nome."

"And if their granaries are not full? If they drive their cattle into the hills and burn their fields themselves before you reach them? Then beyond the cataracts you will have a thousand hungry warriors, and no bread, and no road back."

Ankhtifi turned crimson. His men behind him exchanged glances, and I saw one of them shake his head almost imperceptibly - not at me, but at his master - as though he had long been trying to tell him the same thing.

"So my Pharaoh will not give an army," said Ankhtifi slowly, and each word fell heavily, like a stone into water.

"My Pharaoh will give you bread for your nome," I answered. "Five hundred sacks from the royal granaries, until the next flood. And three hundred spears - not beyond the cataracts, but to the border, so that what happened at Little Nekhen may not happen again in any village. To protect the living now is more important than to avenge the dead."

Ankhtifi looked at me for a long time. The fury in his eyes slowly, reluctantly died down, replaced by something else - not agreement, but the weary understanding of a man who came for one thing and received another, and cannot decide whether he has lost or won.

"Five hundred sacks," he said at last. "And three hundred spears."

"Five hundred sacks and three hundred spears."

He sank to one knee again. Then he rose, nodded to his men and made for the exit. At the very doors he turned.

"My Pharaoh. I am loyal to the throne. My father was loyal to the throne, and his father too. But I shall tell you what is being said now in my nome at every hearth. If the throne cannot protect us, he who has bread will protect us. And bread, my Pharaoh, nowadays is held not by the throne alone."

The doors closed behind him. The boy Paney remained standing in the middle of the hall, pressing his bandaged arm to his chest - small, alone, with eyes in which there was neither fear nor hope.

"Take him to the kitchen," I said to the servants. "Let him be fed. His fill. And then to Hesira."

When the boy had been led away, I sat for a long while looking at the closed doors. The clay crocodile on my brow, as it turned out, was powerless. The pain had grown stronger - and now it was no longer only a headache.

* * *

Toward evening, when the heat began gradually to ebb, I ordered myself carried to the Library - not because I had business there, but because it was quiet there, cool, and smelled of old papyrus and cedar shelves, and this smell, alone in the whole Palace, for some reason soothed me.

The Chief Keeper Amenemope, ancient as the scrolls themselves, met me at the entrance, leaning on a staff of black wood.

"My Pharaoh has honoured us with a visit," he rustled, in a voice like the sound of pages turning. "Which scroll does my Pharaoh desire?"

"None, Amenemope. I came to sit in silence."

"Silence is the best scroll of all," the old man agreed. "It need not be copied, and mice do not gnaw it."

I sat on a bench by the window, in the shade, and for a time we were silent. Then Amenemope, as if remembering something, said - softly, as though in passing:

"The High Priest has come three times this moon."

I raised my head.

"Three times?"

"Three times, my Pharaoh. And each time he asked for the same scroll." Amenemope paused. "The Most Ancient Epic."

My distant predecessors - or ancestors, as they are properly called, though I never fully understood how literally - the first of the Pharaohs, so said the Most Ancient Epic, descended upon this land from the Heavens and gave it life. That scroll was kept here, in this very Library, in a special ark, and access to it was granted to only two in the whole Empire: the Pharaoh himself and the High Priest. No one else. Not even the commanders. Not even the wives and children of the king.

"What was he looking for?" I asked.

"That I do not know, my Pharaoh. The keeper brings the scroll and leaves - such is the law. But I saw him sit over it until sunset. Three times." The old man paused again. "In all the years I have served here, he has taken the Epic twice. Twice in twenty floods. And three times in one moon."

"Perhaps he seeks in it an answer to the drought."

"Perhaps, my Pharaoh," agreed Amenemope in the tone in which old men agree when they do not agree at all. "Or perhaps he seeks something else. Something the First Ones left not on papyrus."

"What do you mean?"

The old man shook his head.

"I am too old to mean anything, my Pharaoh. I merely keep the scrolls." He turned to go, and over his shoulder added: "But if my Pharaoh should ever wish to read the Most Ancient Epic himself - from beginning to end, and not only those lines which are read at coronations - I shall bring it at any hour of day or night."

He went away, tapping his staff, and I remained sitting by the window.

The most ancient of those who were called my ancestors settled the question of disobedience harshly: the dissenters vanished, their families were sent to the quarries to cut stone - slowly perishing there, far from home, far from their own names, which were gradually forgotten even by those who had once borne them. Perhaps they would have condemned me for the liberties I had permitted regarding my subjects, the priests, the very order of things. For the sacks of grain without the purchase of land. For the boy left in the palace. But I could not shake the feeling - troubling, stubborn, yielding neither to logic nor to prayer - that the Most Ancient Times were drawing to a close, and the World needed new ideas, even at the price of what had been built by centuries and millennia of labour.

For centuries the power of the Pharaoh and the power of the Temple had been held in balance, like two pans of the same scales - the Pharaoh, the living god on earth, and the Priests, heralds of the gods of heaven, as necessary to one another as they were dangerous to one another. This balance had been unassailable for millennia. But in recent years, decade after decade, the Temple's pan had been sinking ever more surely - not with a jolt, not with a rebellion, but with that slow, invincible force with which water wears away stone without even trying to break it. And now, when the water had gone from the Nile itself, hunger too was dragging that pan down.

The thought of new ideas I had not dared to utter aloud once in all the years of my reign. But today, sitting in the cool silence of the Library, I caught myself almost uttering it - aloud, into the void, as if someone invisible might hear.

* * *

I returned to the throne room at dusk. The servants had lit the lamps, and their flames trembled in the heated air, casting restless, dancing shadows on the painted walls. Somewhere beyond the walls dry thunder rolled again - another storm that had never been delivered of rain.

The door of the hall opened without a knock - a privilege possessed in the whole Empire by only one man.

Nefer-Ka entered unhurriedly, with that special gait that distinguishes people who never hurry anywhere, because time, in their deep conviction, always works for them. The High Priest was no longer young, but held himself straight as an obelisk, and his voice, when he spoke, spread through the hall with that resonance found only by those who have spent their lives addressing the crowd rather than a single listener.

"My Pharaoh," he said, bowing exactly as far as custom required, not a finger's breadth lower. "The temples of the Delta are overflowing. People pray for rain as they have not prayed since the days of the former sovereigns."

"And what do the gods answer them?" I asked, knowing the answer in advance.

"The gods answer those who know how to listen to them." Nefer-Ka allowed himself a barely perceptible smile - not insolent, no, far more dangerous: indulgent. "The people begin to ask why the voice of heaven reaches the Temple more clearly than the Palace."

"The people ask precisely what they are prompted to ask," I answered, gently, but not without an edge which Nefer-Ka, of course, caught in the same instant I uttered it.

He did not argue. Men of his stamp never argue where one may keep silent and let the silence do all the work for them.

"I have heard," he said instead, "that my Pharaoh has ordered grain to be distributed on credit. From the royal granaries. Without collateral."

"You hear well, Nefer-Ka. The decree was written only this morning."

"The Temple hears much, my Pharaoh. That is its service." He smiled again. "The generosity of the Pharaoh knows no bounds. I fear only that the granaries have bounds."

"Then perhaps the Temple will help the throne by opening its own?"

"The Temple helps as it is, my Pharaoh. The Temple gives bread to everyone who comes to it with a prayer."

"And with land," I said.

Nefer-Ka did not flinch. His face did not change. Only for a moment - for one brief, almost imperceptible instant - his gaze became motionless, like that of a snake that has noticed movement in the grass.

"Land given to the gods is never lost, my Pharaoh," he said evenly. "It merely changes its keeper."

"Its keeper," I repeated. "Tell me, High Priest. Who keeps the keepers?"

Nefer-Ka was silent. Then, instead of answering, he changed the subject - as though handing me a dish of new fare in place of the one I had refused to taste.

"The southern frontiers are troubled again. I hear that the nomarch Ankhtifi was here today. Did he ask for troops?"

"He received what he needed."

"What the Pharaoh needed," Nefer-Ka corrected gently. "Ankhtifi asked for a thousand spears beyond the cataracts, and received three hundred on the border and five hundred sacks. Wise, my Pharaoh. Very wise." He paused, exactly long enough that the following words should sound not like a threat but merely like an observation. "But wisdom is not always understood by those whose villages are burning. To delay the answer to the southerners is to breed doubts in the strength of the throne. And doubts in the strength of the throne, my Pharaoh... are fed far more willingly than doubts in the mercy of the gods."

I looked at him for a long time without answering - letting him feel the same silence-as-a-weapon which he wielded so masterfully. Then I said:

"Decisions are taken when I see fit to take them. As they always were."

"Of course." Nefer-Ka again inclined his head, to the same impeccably measured angle. "I merely relay what I hear among the people, my Pharaoh. Rumour is the only offering I am able to bring you in such arid times."

"Rumour," I said, "and, perhaps, also what you have read in the Most Ancient Epic. Three times in one moon."

This time the pause was longer. Nefer-Ka looked at me, and in his eyes for the first time in the whole conversation I could read nothing at all - neither indulgence, nor calculation, nor alarm. Only a smooth, impenetrable darkness, like the water in a deep well.

"The High Priest is obliged to know the Epic, my Pharaoh," he said at last. "Especially in the hour of calamity. The First Ones left us no little wisdom. Who knows - perhaps even the answer to the drought."

"And have you found it?"

"Not yet," he answered. "But I am patient."

He turned to leave - and stopped at the very doors, as though remembering something of little importance.

"Ah, one more thing," he said, without turning. "Last night in the Luxor sanctuary there was a disturbance. One of the junior priests did not survive the morning. His heart, one must suppose, could not endure the fasting and the prayer vigil. A sad but not uncommon story for our days."

He departed, and the heavy doors closed behind him with a hollow, detached sound, as if it were not a door that had closed, but a page.

I remained alone in the chair of gold and stone, and the headache that had pursued me for days was replaced for the first time by something sharper and far less familiar - a cold rising from somewhere beneath my ribs, on the left, where there should have been no wound at all.

I could not say why Nefer-Ka had thought it necessary to mention the dead priest of Luxor precisely now, in precisely those words, spoken almost carelessly, at the very threshold. But I knew - as clearly as I know my own name - that nothing in that man had ever been accidental.

I took off the linen bandage Hesira had bound and looked for a long time at the clay crocodile, which still held its barley grains in its jaws. It had not taken the pain. The healer was right: it had proved stronger.

"Ask not a healer, but a dream," Hesira had said in the morning. "Dreams sometimes know what we do not."

Outside the window the rays of the setting sun touched the Green Valley of the Nile for the last time and went out in the gloom of approaching night. And for the first time in many moons I suddenly wished to fall asleep as soon as possible - and to see what the dream would tell me.
