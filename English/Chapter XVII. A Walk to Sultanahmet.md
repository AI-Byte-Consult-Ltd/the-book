Chapter XVII
A Walk to Sultanahmet

The appointed day arrived as imperceptibly as the previous ones had slipped by - in a lazy alternation of tea, backgammon with Suleyman (I won, to my own astonishment, some five games off him, though the old man insisted that the victory had been granted me out of pure hospitality), and long hours on the balcony, where I looked at the city, which was beginning little by little to grow familiar to me, in the way any place grows familiar if one spends enough sleepless, watchful evenings in it.

Suleyman appeared on the threshold of my room early in the morning - clad not in his house robe but in a stout travelling kaftan, girded with a wide sash into which was tucked a short knife with a carved handle, more a keepsake than a weapon. On his wrist, over his prayer beads, the red thread with half of the split nazar was still wound. He nodded to me in silence, making it clear: it was time.

"He says," translated Ferhat, who had sprung up behind his back, dishevelled, with hair still wet from washing and a piece of flatbread in his hand, "that we shall go by the same way the stranger took, the one his father saw. He remembers the way. His father told him. Step by step."

"And you?" I asked. "Aren't you due on shift?"

"I am," said Ferhat with his mouth full. "But I told the harbour master that my aunt had died."

"Your aunt has died?"

"I have no aunt," Ferhat replied imperturbably. "So she can die as many times as she likes. This year she has already died three times. The harbour master sent a basket of figs to the funeral." He grinned. "God will forgive. God loves it when people help guests."

Suleyman, who had not understood a word but had evidently guessed everything from the intonation, rapped his grandson on the back of the head with his knuckles - not hard, more for form's sake - and we went out.

* * *

We left the house when the sun had barely torn itself from the edge of the sea and the air still kept the coolness of the night, that peculiar, short-lived freshness which port cities grant their inhabitants for only an hour or two before the heat enters fully into its rights. The streets, which at this hour were usually thronged with peddlers and the first customers, now seemed almost deserted - only a few figures hurried somewhere on their business in the morning dusk, and the cats, those eternal, imperturbable masters of Eastern cities, stretched lazily on steps still warm from the evening.

At the gate Kemal caught up with us. Silently, as always. He stood beside his grandfather and walked on as though it had been agreed. Suleyman looked at him, was about to say something - and did not. He only laid his palm on the boy's head, for a moment, in passing, and under that palm Kemal smiled faintly.

Akmal, of course, did not catch up with anyone - he simply flew out from around the corner, overtook all three of us and raced ahead like a dog let off its leash, turning round every so often to check that we were following.

"He will wait for us at every crossroads," Ferhat sighed. "And at every crossroads he will ask whether it is soon. Like when we were children and went to the sea."

"Did you often go to the sea?"

"Every Sunday. With our father." Ferhat paused. "He would take Akmal on his shoulders and me by the hand. Kemal did not walk yet then - Mother carried him on her back. Father used to say: 'Look at the water. The water remembers everyone who has gone out over it. If you look long enough, you can see.'" He shrugged. "Akmal still looks. Every day. He thinks I don't know."

He said it lightly, as if in passing. But I saw him turn away before he had finished.

* * *

Our way ran along the coast - first through quarters already familiar to me from short outings with Akmal, then farther, where the houses grew higher and more imposing, and the streets noticeably cleaner, as though a different, more exacting part of the city breathed the very air here. Suleyman walked unhurriedly but with assurance, in the stride of a man for whom this road had long become something habitual, though not devoid, judging by the way he glanced about from time to time, of a certain reverence for the places passed.

From time to time he stopped - at a fountain, at an old grove of plane trees, at a small mosque with a leaning minaret - and said something, pointing with his hand. Ferhat translated:

"Here the stranger drank water. Father saw it. He scooped it in his palm, drank, and laughed. He said: 'The same water as everywhere. Water is one everywhere.'" And then, at the grove: "Here he sat down in the shade and wrote something. For a long time. Father stood by a shop and watched. Then the stranger got up, and on the ground where he had sat a drawing was left, made with a stick. Two circles. Father came to look - but the wind had already wiped it away."

Kemal, hearing this, stopped at the roots of a huge plane tree. He crouched down. Ran his palm over the ground as if searching for something. Then he rose and went on, saying nothing.

We passed a hill from whose summit the whole bay opened to view - I stopped for a moment, unable to tear my eyes from the water, which reflected the morning sun with that dazzling, almost painful brightness that occurs only over the sea in a windless hour. The minarets scattered across the whole visible expanse seemed from here like thin needles stuck into the bluish haze, and the domes of the mosques like frozen drops of mercury, heavy and perfect in their roundness.

"Beautiful," I said aloud, though I was addressing, essentially, only myself.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, "that this view has no price. He traded for forty years and knows the price of everything. Silk, pepper, people. But not of this. He says: that is why everyone stays here. They come for a day - and stay for life. The city keeps those it likes."

"And those it does not like?"

"Those it lets go," said Ferhat. "At once. With the first ship." He smiled. "It has not let you go yet."

* * *

Descending the hill, we passed a place fenced off with scaffolding and high hoardings of fresh planks - judging by the hammering that carried from there and the calling of workmen in several languages at once, this was where the new Palace of the Sultan was being raised, of which I had heard almost from the first minute of my stay in this city. The building itself, not yet finished, already astonished by its scale - the walls, faced with pale stone, stretched along the whole shore, vanishing somewhere around the bend, and the dome of the central part, still hidden by scaffolding, could be guessed beneath it as a gigantic, half-formed shadow.

At the gate of the building site, on an overturned wheelbarrow, sat a mason - elderly, grey-moustached, with hands white from dust - smoking a long pipe and waiting until the overseer turned away. Suleyman, passing by, stopped, and they exchanged a few phrases - judging by the tone, old acquaintances. The mason burst out laughing, slapped his knees and said something, pointing with his pipe at the palace.

"What does he say?" I asked.

Ferhat was choking with laughter.

"He says he has been building this palace for eight years. And that in eight years the Sultan has changed the architect three times and the plan seven times. And that when the palace is finally finished, the Sultan will probably say he wanted something completely different and order it pulled down." Ferhat wiped his eyes. "And Grandfather answered that this is a good thing: while they are building, people have bread. Better to build for ten years than to finish in one and dismiss everyone."

"Grandfather does not like this building site," he added, lowering his voice, though there seemed to be no reason for such caution. "He says too much money goes into stone and too little into people. But it is better not to repeat that aloud."

The mason, seeing us off, suddenly called out to me - in his own tongue, but with an evident question, pointing his pipe first at me, then at the palace.

"He asks," translated Ferhat, "whether you are an architect. A new one. The fourth. He says you have the face of a man who is about to redo everything."

I laughed. The mason laughed too, waved his pipe - and at once, noticing the overseer, hid it behind his back with the air of an innocent babe.

* * *

Beyond the building site began the quarter of Sultanahmet proper - and here the city changed almost imperceptibly, yet quite definitely. The streets grew narrower, the houses older, and the stillness that reigned in this part of the city even at the height of the morning seemed somehow special, almost reverent, as if the very air here were soaked with the memory of centuries that had passed before even one of us was born.

And then the street parted - and we came out onto a huge, elongated square across which the wind roamed.

In the middle of it, one after another, stood three columns. The first was thin, tall, grey, built of rough stone blocks, as if it had been put up in haste and never been faced. The second was strange, dark, of bronze, like three snakes twisted together into a tight rope - only without heads: where the snakes should have had heads, the column broke off in uneven, broken edges. And the third...

The third was an obelisk.

Tall, slender, sharp as a needle, of rose granite, gleaming in the morning sun as if it had just been taken from the quarry. It stood on a marble pedestal, resting on it not with its base but on four small copper cubes at the corners - as though it hovered in the air, barely touching the ground. And from the very point to the base, on all four faces, it was covered with signs.

Birds. Eyes. Snakes. Beetles. Little men squatting on their haunches. Wavy lines of water. Solar discs.

I stopped. I stopped so abruptly that Kemal, who was walking behind, ran into my back.

"What is this?" I asked. My voice sounded hoarse.

"An Egyptian stone," said Ferhat. "It was brought from Egypt. Long ago. Very long ago. More than a thousand years ago, when there were still Greek kings here. They say it was twice as tall, but it broke in transport, and only the upper part was set up here." He shrugged. "Grandfather says nobody in the world can read what is written on it. Those signs have been forgotten."

I came closer. The wind stirred the skirts of my cloak. The pouch at my belt - I felt it at once - had grown heavier. Not warmer. Heavier. As at the Column of Constantine. As in that dream of the columns, on the first day.

I raised my eyes to the signs. And among the birds, snakes and suns I saw one - quite small, high up, almost at the very point. An eye. A human eye, lined with a long stroke, with a curl beneath it like a tear.

I did not know this sign. I had never seen it. But I knew - knew as firmly as I knew that my feet stood on the ground - that it was an eye which had once been torn out, torn into pieces, and then put together again.

"Misafir?" Ferhat touched my elbow. "You have gone pale."

I did not answer. Kemal stood beside me, head thrown back, looking where I was looking - at the eye by the point of the obelisk. Then he slowly raised his hand and pointed at it with a finger. Then at my belt, at the pouch. Then again at the eye.

"He says," Ferhat translated uncertainly, though Kemal had not said a word, "I don't know what he says."

Suleyman, standing a little apart, looked at the three of us - at the obelisk, at me, at his grandson with his raised hand - and told his beads. Click. Click. Click.

"Father," he said at last, quietly, and Ferhat translated after him, "used to say: the stranger also stood here. For a long time. By this stone. And wept." Suleyman paused. "Father thought - from the sun. Here there is always wind and sun. Eyes water."

I passed my palm over my face. The palm was wet.

"Here there is always wind," I said. "Eyes water."

"Yes," agreed Suleyman, and there was not a drop of doubt in his voice. "Wind."

* * *

A little farther, at the edge of the square, under the awning of a coffeehouse, a crowd was gathering. Bursts of laughter came from there, someone's piercing voices - two voices, one high and shrill, the other low and sedate - and the jingle of a tambourine.

"Karagöz!" yelled Akmal, jumping on the spot, and without waiting for permission he dived into the crowd.

"The shadow theatre," Ferhat explained, smiling. "Grandfather, may we? Just a little?"

Suleyman sighed with the air of a man who sixty years ago had himself slipped away to see Karagöz - and nodded.

Under the awning, between two posts, a white canvas was stretched, lit from behind by a lamp. And on the canvas shadows moved - two flat, coloured, translucent little figures cut from leather, with thin sticks attached to their arms and heads. One was fat, short-legged, with an enormous beard, in a turban like a pumpkin. The other was gaunt, tall, with a pointed beard and an important bearing.

"Karagöz," Ferhat whispered, pointing at the fat one. "The simpleton. Muddles everything. And Hacivat, the clever one. Knows everything. They always quarrel."

The shadows quarrelled. Fat Karagöz squealed, jumped, fell, struck gaunt Hacivat on the head, Hacivat gravely straightened his turban and replied in long, flowery phrases, and the crowd laughed till it wept. Ferhat translated to me in a low voice, breaking off with laughter:

"Hacivat says: 'O Karagöz, light of my eyes, set me a riddle that I may show you my wisdom.' Karagöz says: 'Very well. What is this: it stands on the shore, feet in the water, head in the clouds, and itself does not budge?' Hacivat thinks and thinks... 'That is, without doubt, a minaret reflected in the Bosphorus!' Karagöz: 'No! It is my uncle Mehmet, who went to catch fish and fell asleep!'" The crowd roared. "Hacivat says: 'That is a stupid riddle!' Karagöz: 'But the answer is clever! And with you it is the other way round!'"

I laughed along with everyone - without noticing how. Kemal, standing beside me, laughed soundlessly, covering his mouth with his palm. Even Suleyman, standing behind, smiled into his beard.

And then the shadows on the canvas suddenly changed. Hacivat went away. Karagöz remained alone. The lamp behind the canvas dimmed. And the invisible puppeteer - invisible, but his voice now sounded quite different, soft, without the squeal - spoke for Karagöz slowly, almost sadly.

"What does he say?" I asked in a whisper.

Ferhat stopped smiling.

"This is the end of the performance. It is always ended this way. Karagöz says..." He paused, listening. "He says: 'Do not look at our mistakes. We are only shadows on a canvas. The canvas is the world. The lamp is He who holds the light. And the shadows come, quarrel, laugh and go. And no one knows who cut them out of leather and who moves them with the sticks.'" Ferhat swallowed. "'And when the lamp goes out,' says Karagöz, 'all the shadows will become one shadow. And then it will no longer matter who was Karagöz and who was Hacivat.'"

The lamp behind the canvas went out. The crowd clapped and whistled, someone threw the puppeteer a copper coin. And I stood and looked at the empty white canvas on which two shadows had just lived, and could not move.

* * *

Then Suleyman led us past a huge cathedral with a round dome - so huge that, standing at its walls, one had to throw back one's head to see its edge - to which, at the corners, slender minarets had been added, like needles stuck in a cushion.

"This was a church," said Ferhat. "The main church of the Greek kings. A thousand years. Then our people came - and made it a mosque." He paused. "Grandfather says that inside, under the plaster, faces can still be seen. Of Greek saints. Golden ones. They were smeared over, but they show through all the same. Through the lime. A little more each year. The imam says it is damp. The old men say it is they, looking. They want not to be forgotten."

Bells. A men's choir beneath the enormous dome. Candles. People with crosses in their hands.

I had already seen this place. At night, that time when the tall guest stood by my couch and the walls of the room melted. I had seen it as it was - not a mosque but a cathedral. I had heard bells that are no longer here.

"Ferhat," I said. "Did bells once ring here?"

He looked at me in surprise.

"They did. Long ago. More than four hundred years ago. How do you know?"

"I don't," I said. "I just thought so."

Ferhat looked at me for a long time, attentively, eyes narrowed. On his cheek, I noticed suddenly, there was a dark smudge - soot, probably, from Karagöz's lamp, beside which he had stood too close. Small, uneven, almost unnoticeable. He stood before me with his head tilted slightly to one side, as he always did when he was thinking something over - and for one brief moment it suddenly seemed to me that I had already seen this face. Not Ferhat's - another. Younger. Thinner. With just such a smudge on the cheek - only not soot but ink. Bent over some scroll in the dim light of a lamp. Somewhere very far away. In a place that smelled of cedar, dust and old papyrus. And that someone, the other one, also looked at me just so - head tilted a little, as if waiting for me finally to guess something that was right under my nose.

My hand rose of its own accord - to wipe away that smudge. I stopped it halfway. Lowered it.

"You have soot on your cheek," I said.

"Eh?" Ferhat rubbed his cheek with his palm - the wrong one, smearing the stain even more. "Karagöz. I always stand too close. Since childhood. Father used to say - I want to climb behind the canvas and see who is there."

"And did you?"

"Once." Ferhat smiled. "An old man was sitting there holding the sticks. The most ordinary old man. I was so upset that I cried all the way home. Father said: 'Silly. What matters is not who holds the sticks. What matters is that the shadows are alive while the lamp burns.'" He shrugged. "I did not understand then. And I don't quite understand now."

I did not understand either. But the strange face that had flashed before me - the face with the ink smudge, bent over a scroll - did not go away for a long time. I did not know who it was. I had never seen him. And yet, whenever I closed my eyes, it appeared again - and looked at me as though it knew something about me that I did not know myself.

* * *

We passed several small mosques, each of which, despite its modest size, possessed its own unrepeatable character: one flaunted tiles of the deepest turquoise, another a plain dome, yet flawless in proportion, a third hid in the depths of a courtyard enclosed by a low stone wall, entwined with ivy so old that the wall itself seemed to be held up by its roots alone.

At the entrance to some courtyard, right on the ground, beside a dark stone well, sat an old Greek woman in black, with a rosary in her hands - not like Suleyman's, but with a cross at the end. Before her on a kerchief lay thin wax candles. Akmal, running past, almost overturned her kerchief, and the old woman burst into Greek abuse so ringing that pigeons fluttered up from the roof.

Suleyman stopped, bowed to her - the old woman, seeing him, instantly softened - and they began to talk in a mixture of Turkish and Greek. The old woman pointed a finger downward, into the well.

"She says," Ferhat translated, "that beneath us there is water. A whole underground palace of water. With columns. Hundreds of columns, she says, stand in the water under the ground, and it is dark there, and fish swim - white, blind ones. Her grandfather went down there by boat when he was a boy." Ferhat listened further. "She says that in the farthest corner two columns stand on stone heads. Women's heads. With snakes for hair. One head lies on its side, the other upside down. So that they will not look. Because whoever looks them in the eyes will turn to stone."

"Who put them there?" I asked.

The old woman, as if she had understood the question, said something with a shrug.

"No one knows," translated Ferhat. "She says: the Greeks say - the Romans. The Romans say - earlier still. And her grandfather said that those heads had always been there. That the city was built around them. That they guard the water - and what lies beneath the water."

"And what lies beneath the water?"

The old woman looked at me. Narrowed her eyes. Then she said - slowly, in Turkish, with a strong accent, so that even I almost made out the words:

"What you lost. Whoever lost it will find it."

And, turning away, she went back to telling her rosary with the cross, as though we were not there at all.

* * *

At last we turned into a narrow, winding alley, so tight that the balconies opposite almost touched overhead, leaving only a thin, broken strip for the sunlight. At the end of the alley the domes of a bathhouse could be seen - old, peeling, with grilles on the windows from which steam poured - and at its doors, on a bench, sprawled a bath attendant: enormous, bare to the waist, with a shaved head, a towel on his shoulder and such a belly that it seemed it could be used as a table.

"Suleyman Agha!" he boomed on seeing the old man. "Forty years I haven't seen you! Still dry as a dried fish! Come in, I'll steam you - you'll grow twenty years younger!"

"If I grow twenty years younger," answered Suleyman, and Ferhat translated, roaring with laughter, "I shall have to marry my wife again. And a second time I shall not survive it."

The attendant laughed so that his belly shook like jelly. Then he saw me - and stopped laughing.

"And who is this?" he asked, narrowing his eyes.

"A guest," said Suleyman.

"A guest," repeated the attendant. He looked at me for a long time. Then he said something which Ferhat did not translate at once.

"What did he say?"

"He said," Ferhat hesitated, "that some twenty years ago a similar one came to his bath. Just like you. A stranger. Without a name. He asked only for hot water and silence. He sat in the steam room for three hours and did not sweat." Ferhat shrugged. "The attendant says he then told everyone for half a year: a man came who does not sweat. No one believed him."

"And where did he go afterwards?"

The attendant listened to the question and spread his enormous hands.

"Away," translated Ferhat. "That way." The attendant pointed into the depth of the alley, where we were headed. "To where the door in the wall is."

Twenty years ago. Suleyman's father - half a century ago. Karapet's great-grandfather - earlier still. How many of them had there been, nameless, coming to this city, seeking one and the same door?

* * *

Suleyman stopped at an inconspicuous door in a blank stone wall - a door so modest that I, had I been looking for it on my own, would certainly have walked past without noticing. Beside the door, walled into the masonry, a fragment of an ancient column stuck out - grey, pitted, with traces of some carved pattern, almost worn away by time. "Near the old column. Or pillar. Or stone."

"Here," he said in his own language, and this word, simple and short, I understood without translation, to my own astonishment - whether I had involuntarily learned it in these days, or simply guessed from the intonation alone.

My heart beat faster than was fitting for the mere examination of someone else's door. I went closer, peering at the wood darkened by time, at the iron hinges covered with rust, at the stone lintel worn so smooth that the original pattern which must once have adorned it could now be read only in vague, half-effaced hints.

Ferhat knocked - at first softly, then, getting no answer, more insistently. A minute passed, another; at last slow, shuffling footsteps sounded behind the door - and mewing. A great deal of mewing. The door opened just enough to let a strip of light inside and to let out a wary glance - and three cats at once.

Her name was Hatice Hanim. I learned that later. At first I saw only a small, dry old woman in a white kerchief, with a face so densely wrinkled that it resembled a baked apple, with very lively, very cunning eyes - and around her feet, tangling in her hem, rubbing, purring, demanding attention - cats. Ginger, white, striped, black. I counted seven. Then lost count.

The conversation that followed I naturally understood only by intonation and scraps of gesture, but its general sense soon became clear even without translation: the door had been opened by a widow who had lived in this house, if her own words, relayed to me later by Ferhat, were to be believed, a good forty years, having inherited it from her husband's parents. She had neither written nor received any note. She had never before met any wanderer looking for this place. The name which I, lacking one of my own, tried to convey by gestures - pointing to myself, then spreading my hands in sign of ignorance - provoked in her not the faintest glimmer of recognition.

"And where do the cats come from?" I could not help asking.

Hatice Hanim, hearing the translation, laughed - ringingly, like a girl.

"She says," translated Ferhat, "that when her husband was dying he said: 'Do not stay alone.' She asked: 'With whom?' He said: 'With someone. Whoever comes.'" Ferhat smiled. "She says, the cats came. One, then another. Now there are eleven of them. She says - her husband probably did not mean cats. But the cats came first. And whoever comes first, you feed."

"If another comes - feed him," I said quietly, to myself.

Ferhat looked at me. He understood. Suleyman's father. The stranger with the letter.

"She says," Ferhat continued, having listened to the hostess's long, circumstantial account, "that this house had belonged to her husband's family not for the first generation. Formerly a small shop was kept here - something like a warehouse for fabrics arriving with the trade caravans. Then the shop was closed, the premises downstairs were given over as lodgings for one of the nephews, and she herself settled upstairs with her husband. She had noticed nothing unusual in the house. Except perhaps..."

He fell silent, listening to the rest of the sentence, and his brows rose slightly.

"Except perhaps?" I repeated, unable to restrain my impatience.

"Except perhaps the cellar. There they kept locked an old chest, inherited from the earlier owners of the shop - no one managed to open it, the key had not survived, and there was no need to break it open: there was room enough in the cellar as it was." Ferhat listened to the end. "And one more thing. The cats do not go there. Not one. In forty years. She says it is the only place in the house where her cats do not go. Not even after mice."

* * *

The hostess stepped aside, letting us in, and I, ducking my head beneath the low lintel, stepped into a half-dark corridor smelling of dust, old wood, cats and something else - that peculiar stale scent of rooms which seldom see daylight. A narrow stair led down to the cellar, lit by a single tiny window under the very ceiling, through which a slanting ray of morning sun barely made its way.

The cats accompanied us to the top step of the stair - and stopped. All eleven. They sat in a row at the edge, like spectators in a theatre, and looked down, without taking a step.

The chest - the very one the hostess had spoken of - stood in the far corner, covered with a ragged cloth that had once, no doubt, been bright and had now faded to an indeterminate, dusty shade. I came closer, drew back the cloth - and froze.

The wood of the chest had blackened with time, the metal corners were coated with an even layer of patina, and on the lid itself, where a keyhole ought to have been, there was something else dark: not a lock in the usual sense of the word, but a massive metal plate with the same script, the same pattern that I had seen on my note - and on the stones when they grew warm - carved deep, by the sure hand of a master who had spared neither time nor effort on this work.

I stretched out my hand, touched the cold metal with the tips of my fingers - and for a moment it seemed to me that the floor swayed beneath my feet, that the very air in this cramped, dusty cellar had thickened, filled with the same recognition that had overtaken me not for the first time in these days: the sharp, inexplicable feeling that I stood not before a chance find but before something belonging to me by right, a right which, however, I could not have explained by a single reasonable cause.

"Are you all right?" asked Ferhat, having noticed, no doubt, the change in my face.

"Yes," I lied, snatching my hand from the chest faster than I should have. "Just... a familiar pattern."

Kemal, who had descended last, silently went to the chest, crouched down and looked at the plate for a long time. Then he took from his bosom a fragment of the blue bead - the one he had picked up from the floor on the night of the guest - and laid it on the lid, beside the script. As if a gift. Or as if a mark.

Hatice Hanim, on the top step, gasped and pressed her hand to her chest.

"She says," translated Ferhat, "that if a child lays a nazar on a thing, then the thing must be cared for. That children see whom to protect." Ferhat paused. "And that her husband said the same about this chest. 'Look after it. It is not ours. They will come for it.'"

I knelt before the chest, examining the plate more closely. There was no keyhole as such at all - only an even surface of metal covered with the same indecipherable script, without a single gap to catch a pick or a key. I took both stones from the pouch - the black one and the white - and, obeying instinct rather than sensible calculation, pressed them to the plate, where the pattern seemed especially dense.

Nothing happened. The stones remained cold, mute, as ordinary to the touch as they had always been, except for those rare minutes when something - I still did not know what - made them respond with warmth.

"Not now," I muttered under my breath, putting the finds back into the pouch, and I was not sure myself what there was more of in those words: disappointment or, strangely, relief.

Kemal silently took the bead fragment from the lid. Then, after thinking, put it back. And climbed the stair without looking round, past the cats sitting in a row.

* * *

We stayed in the house another half hour - the hostess, touched, it seems, by the very fact of our visit, treated us to strong coffee brewed in a small copper pot, and to a short but sincere account of her own life, which Ferhat translated for me in a low voice while Suleyman nodded at every word with the air of an old acquaintance, though they were meeting, it seems, for the first time. On my knees all this while lay a ginger cat, the fattest, purring so loudly that Ferhat had to translate louder.

"She has set you a riddle," said Ferhat when Hatice Hanim, narrowing her eyes slyly, said something, looking at me. "She says: all guests who drink coffee with her must guess a riddle. Otherwise the coffee will do them no good."

"Go on."

"'In my house there are twelve rooms,'" translated Ferhat. "'In each room there are thirty windows. Into every window in the morning the sun comes in, in the evening the moon. Who lives in this house?'"

I thought. Twelve. Thirty.

"A year," I said. "Twelve months. Thirty days each. The sun by day, the moon by night."

Hatice Hanim threw up her hands and clapped - so ringingly that the cat on my knees leapt up and walked away, tail offendedly raised.

"She says you are the first in ten years to guess at once," translated Ferhat. "She says her husband also guessed at once. He was clever. He died of thinking too much." Ferhat giggled. "That is her joke."

On leaving, I gave the hostess a few coins - those very strange ones, found in my pocket on the first morning - not so much in thanks for the hospitality as in the hope that perhaps someone better informed than myself might one day identify their origin.

Hatice Hanim took the coins, looked at them - long, attentively - and suddenly, without saying a word, went into the depth of the house. She returned in a moment and held out to me... a coin. One. Exactly like mine. With the same incomprehensible sign on one side and interlaced lines on the other. Worn, darkened by time.

"She says," whispered Ferhat, and his voice trembled, "that this coin lay in her husband's casket. All his life. He said it had been left by the one who brought the chest. And he said: 'Give it to the one who will have the same.'"

I took the coin. It was warm - not from the old woman's hand, no. With a warmth of its own.

* * *

We made the return journey in a silence far deeper than that which had accompanied us on the way here. Suleyman, it seemed, felt that to question me about anything now would be superfluous - and Ferhat, immersed in his own thoughts, glanced at me from time to time with the same expression of quiet, sympathetic concern I had noticed on his face as early as the first evening of our acquaintance. Kemal walked beside me - so close that his shoulder sometimes touched my elbow. Even Akmal no longer ran ahead.

At the Egyptian obelisk, which we passed again, I stopped for the last time. The eye by the very point looked down at me - calmly, patiently, without surprise. As one looks at someone who is many centuries late, but has come all the same.

"A thousand years," said Ferhat beside me. "Even more. It stands, and no one can read it."

"Perhaps someone can," I said.

"Who?"

I did not answer. I did not know. But somewhere far, far away, at the very bottom of a memory I did not have, a youth with an ink smudge on his cheek bent over a scroll and moved his lips, deciphering the signs - birds, snakes, eyes, wavy lines of water - one after another. Easily. As we read letters.

Only at the house itself, when Suleyman had already vanished behind the door and Ferhat and I remained alone on the threshold, did he finally break the silence.

"That pattern," he said quietly. "On the chest. You recognized it, didn't you?"

I did not at once find an answer. To lie to him, after all that this family had done for me, seemed to me almost unthinkable - and yet to confess to what I myself did not fully understand was no easier.

"It seems to me," I said at last, choosing my words carefully, "that I have seen it somewhere before. But where exactly and when, I cannot say."

Ferhat nodded slowly, as though this answer, for all its vagueness, did not surprise him in the least.

"Grandfather believes," he said, "that certain things find a man themselves, when their time comes. It is not the man who seeks the thing - the thing seeks the man. Perhaps this chest was simply not yet ready to open to you."

I looked at him - at his young face, serious now, deprived of its usual, almost carefree gaiety, with a trace of soot on the cheek that he had never wiped off - and thought of the words which Suleyman and old Hasan had read from my note: keep until the appointed time. Return.

"Perhaps," I said softly. "Perhaps you are right."

"And one more thing," said Ferhat after a pause. "There, by the big mosque. You looked at me as though you had seen someone else. Whom?"

I was silent for a long time.

"I don't know," I said honestly. "Someone I seem to be meant to remember. He looked like you. A little. When you tilt your head."

Ferhat laughed - softly, shyly - and looked away.

"Grandfather says that every person has a double," he said. "Somewhere in the world. Sometimes in another city. Sometimes in another time." He rubbed his cheek, finally wiping away the soot. "I hope my double at least knows how to read those Egyptian scrawls. Because I make mistakes even writing my own surname."

We stood a little longer in the evening dusk, saying nothing more - and in that silence there was far more understanding than any words could have held. And in my pocket, beside the note and Kemal's drawing, lay a stranger's coin, warm as a hand held too long to let go.
