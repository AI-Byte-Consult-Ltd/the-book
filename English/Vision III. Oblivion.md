Vision III
Oblivion

I do not know how many days this storm lasted.

That is the first thing worth saying - because the count of time, usually so simple, so self-evident, had long since lost all meaning for me in this enormous, ever-rocking iron womb. Three days? Five? Seven? The ship was flung from side to side with that monotonous, merciless regularity with which a storm flings a vessel when it has no intention of subsiding either for human weariness or for human prayer.

This ship was not merely large. It was monstrous. The largest of anything people had ever launched on the water - so the newspapers spoke of it, so its officers proudly repeated, so the passengers of the third class whispered, crossing themselves, seeing it for the first time from the quay: an iron mountain six hundred feet long, with five funnels, six masts and two paddle wheels at its sides - each as high as a three-storey house - and one more enormous screw at the stern. Four thousand people it could take aboard, they said. A city. A floating city, crossing the Atlantic from the Old World to the New.

Now this city was drowning. Not in water - not yet. In noise. In din. In the shriek of iron grinding on iron. In cries. The storm had fallen on us on the third day of the voyage, at the very edge of the Grand Banks of Newfoundland - and in the first night alone it tore off both gigantic wheels, like a child tearing the wings from a fly, broke the rudder and turned the mountain-ship into a log helplessly tumbling on waves each of which was higher than our masts.

The sailors said there had not been such a storm in a hundred years. A century storm. So it was nicknamed aboard on the second day: the century storm. As though it had been gathering for a whole century somewhere beyond the horizon and had at last waited for us.

* * *

My valet - Prokhor Ilyich, who had served me, if my own memory is to be trusted, for more than one decade - sat in the corner of the cabin on a stool bolted to the floor, imperturbable as always, though the pitching made even him grab at the wall at times. He said nothing without need. He simply was near - with that particular, unobtrusive presence of a man who has long ceased to need words to prove his devotion.

The cabin was first class - spacious, finished in mahogany and mirrors, with velvet curtains and brass lamps on gimbals which, as was intended, remained horizontal in any pitching, but because everything around them was heaving, it seemed that they were swaying while the world stood still. Across the floor, from wall to wall, at every roll my travelling trunk rolled with a crash, having torn from its fastenings yesterday. Prokhor had twice tried to tie it down. The third time he waved a hand.

"Prokhor," I called, not turning, going on looking into the dark, water-washed porthole, beyond which nothing at all could be seen but maddened, foaming blackness, "tell me honestly. Do you believe that I am in my right mind?"

He paused - whether weighing his answer or simply giving me time to rethink the question - and at last spoke, in that even, anxiety-free voice in which he always spoke, even when the world around us seemed to be falling apart:

"You ask this question for the third day running, sir. My answer has not changed since the first time."

"Then remind me of it. I seem to begin to forget even my own questions, let alone the answers to them."

"I believe that you are what you have always been," he answered. "Even if you yourself no longer believe it."

The ship heeled so that the trunk crashed into the door. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead, in the next cabin, a woman screamed - long, piercingly - and at once fell silent. Then came the sound of breaking glass, and someone swore in English.

"Let us go to the saloon, sir," said Prokhor, rising. "There are people there. When there are many people, fear is divided among all, and each gets less."

* * *

The main saloon occupied, it seemed, half the ship - an enormous hall with columns faced in mirrors, with sofas along the walls, with chandeliers which now, torn from their hooks, lay in heaps of crystal in the corners, and with a piano lashed to a column with ropes, as a raving madman is lashed. On the floor, between broken glasses and overturned tables, stood water - ankle-deep, it rolled from wall to wall with each heel, carrying off cards, gloves, someone's hat with feathers.

And people. Some forty, perhaps more. Those who could not stay in their cabins. Someone was praying. Someone was drinking. Someone, clutching a column, simply stared into emptiness with glassy eyes.

"Oh!" a piercing voice rang out. "There he is! The Russian! The one who doesn't sleep!"

Toward me, grabbing the backs of sofas, picked her way a lady - a stout Englishwoman of about fifty, in a lilac travelling dress soaked to the knees, with dishevelled greying ringlets and with a parrot in a cage which she pressed to her breast like an infant. The parrot - huge, green, with a red tail - rolled a mad yellow eye and from time to time hoarsely shouted something in Spanish.

"Mrs Aldridge," Prokhor whispered to me. "From Bristol. A judge's widow. Knows everything about everyone on this ship."

"You are a count, are you not?" Mrs Aldridge seized my sleeve, nearly dropping the cage. "The maid told me, and the laundress told her, and your steward told the laundress, that you are a Russian count. Or a prince. That you have not gone to bed for four nights. That you have..." she lowered her voice to a theatrical whisper, "scars on your back. Two. Here." She jabbed me with a finger between the shoulder blades. "The laundress saw them when she was washing your shirt - blood was showing through. Is it true? Were you wounded in a duel? Or did you escape from Siberia?"

"Ave Maria purísima!" the parrot croaked.

"Quiet, Cristóbal!" Mrs Aldridge shook the cage. "He lived with a Spanish missionary until the man died of fever. Now he prays like a nun. Perfectly insufferable bird. So is it true? About the scars?"

"Madam," I said, "if your laundress had seen everything she tells, she would have to wash the shirts of the whole Atlantic Ocean."

Mrs Aldridge was taken aback for a moment - then burst out laughing, loudly, like a man, slapping me on the shoulder.

"Witty! Very witty! I like it!" She bent closer. "But I shall find out all the same, Count. I always find out. My late husband used to say: 'Georgiana, you ought to have served at Scotland Yard instead of marrying a judge.' I said: 'Edward, I married a judge to know whom they put away.'"

The ship dropped from the crest of a wave, and all who stood fell to the floor. The parrot howled: "Dios mío!" Mrs Aldridge, sitting in the water, pressed the cage tightly to her and briskly adjusted a ringlet.

"Never mind," she said. "I have drowned three times in Bristol. In a boat, in a pond and in a bath. God does not take me. Says that with me he would have nothing but trouble."

* * *

In the far corner of the saloon, by the column with the lashed piano, stood a man in black. Tall, thin as a pole, with a narrow pale face, with long, shoulder-length grey hair, in a black frock coat buttoned to the throat, and with an enormous black book in his hands, which he held above his head like a shield. He held to nothing. He stood with legs wide apart, swaying with the ship, and shouted - in a voice that drowned even the roar of the storm:

"...and I saw a new heaven and a new earth! For the first heaven and the first earth were passed away, and there was no more sea! Repent, sinners! Repent! This is not a storm - these are the trumpets! The seventh seal is opened! He who shall fall from heaven like a star - he is here! He is among us! On this ship!"

"The Reverend Ezekiel Crow," whispered Prokhor. "From Boston. Going to preach in New York. Certain that the end of the world will come this year. Last year he was certain it would come last year."

The Reverend Crow suddenly turned - and his burning, sunken eyes stopped on me. He slowly lowered the book.

"You," he said. Not cried. Said - quietly, but I heard it across the whole saloon. "You. I saw you on deck the first night. You stood in the rain and looked at the sky. And the lightning did not strike you. It struck the mast three paces from you - and went round. I saw."

"Lightning often strikes masts, Reverend," I said. "It is iron."

"'How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!'" proclaimed the Reverend, raising the book again. "'How art thou cut down to the ground, which didst weaken the nations!' Who are you? Tell me your name!"

The saloon froze. Even Mrs Aldridge stopped adjusting her ringlet. Even the parrot fell silent.

I looked at this mad, burning man - and could not answer. Not because I was afraid. Because that question - tell me your name - suddenly struck me where no storm had reached. I knew what I was called in my passport. I knew what Prokhor called me. But at that moment, standing ankle-deep in water in the middle of a rocking saloon, I suddenly understood that I was not sure which of those names was the true one.

"Leave him, Reverend."

The voice sounded calm, mocking, with a soft French accent. From behind a card table, fastened immovably to the floor, rose a man - short, elegant, in an impeccable dark-blue frock coat that by some miracle remained dry in the midst of the general deluge. About forty, with a thin black moustache, with pomaded hair parted evenly, and with a pack of cards in long, deft fingers which shuffled it without stopping even when the ship heeled.

"If this gentleman were the devil," said the Frenchman, smiling, "he would long since have won all my money. And he has not even sat down at the table. What sort of devil is it that does not play cards?" He bowed to me. "Chevalier Auguste de Lambert. I travel for pleasure. And for other people's money. Would you care for a game, monsieur? Fear is best cured by gambling."

"He is a cardsharp," Prokhor whispered. "He has twice been caught. The captain wanted to put him ashore in Newfoundland, but had no time - the storm began."

The Reverend Crow muttered something about the Whore of Babylon and cards as her instrument, but turned away and began again to call upon the ceiling.

I sat down at the table.

* * *

We played écarté. The Chevalier dealt with the ease with which one breathes, and chattered without pause - of Paris, of Monte Carlo, of the women he had beaten and the men who had beaten him. At the table, besides ourselves, sat two others: a fat, sweating American cotton trader from New Orleans, in a white waistcoat which he kept wiping with a handkerchief, and a small, dry, grey-bearded man in a black skullcap on the back of his head, with a loupe on a cord round his neck and a velvet pouch which he did not let out of his left hand for an instant.

"Mr Jacob van der Heyden," the Chevalier introduced him. "From Amsterdam. Deals in diamonds. Is carrying to New York a whole fortune - in that pouch. Is it not so, Mr van der Heyden?"

"In this pouch, Chevalier," the old man answered drily, "only my supper. Ship's biscuit. The fortune is in the captain's safe. I am not so foolish as you hope."

The cotton trader guffawed, wiping his forehead.

I played without looking at the cards. I do not know why. I simply took them, laid them down, took them again - and did not look. My thoughts were far away - somewhere under my ribs, at that point where from the very beginning of the storm a dull, aching pain had settled.

After the third game the Chevalier stopped chattering.

After the fifth - stopped smiling.

After the seventh he slowly laid the pack on the table and looked at me with a long, very attentive look.

"Monsieur," he said quietly. "For forty years I have played cards. I know every way of cheating that mankind has devised, and a dozen I devised myself. You do not cheat. I would see. " He paused. "But you do not look at the cards. And you have not lost a single trick. Not one. In seven games."

The American stopped wiping his forehead.

"Maybe just luck?" he said uncertainly.

"I too am lucky sometimes, monsieur," said the Chevalier, not taking his eyes from me. "But I am never so lucky that I do not look at the cards."

Old van der Heyden slowly took the loupe from his neck. Took my hand - without asking, with dry, cold fingers - and turned it palm up. Studied it long through the loupe. The lines. The skin.

"Interesting," he said at last. "Very interesting. You have no life line, young man."

"What?"

"A life line. Here," he drew a nail along my palm. "Everyone has one. Long, short, broken. You - none. Smooth. Like a stone that has lain long in a river." He released my hand. "For fifty years I have looked at stones through this glass. Diamond, ruby, emerald. I know how what the ages have grown looks." He paused. "Your palm is like a stone, young man. Not like a hand."

"Perhaps in Amsterdam it is the custom to pay compliments in riddles?" I asked, trying to smile.

The old man did not smile in return.

"In Amsterdam," he said, "there is an old riddle. Jewellers set it to apprentices. 'Two stones lie in one palm: one black as pitch, the other white as milk. Which is more precious?'" He looked at me over the loupe. "The apprentice always answers: the white. Or the black. And is always wrong."

"And what is the answer?"

"The one in the other palm," said the old man. And tucked the loupe under his coat.

* * *

"Charades!" Mrs Aldridge suddenly cried from some corner. "Let us play charades! My husband said: in a storm one must occupy the head, otherwise fear will occupy it!"

Someone groaned. Someone laughed. But soon, to my surprise, half the saloon - drenched, terrified, holding to columns and to one another - was already playing.

Mrs Aldridge set the first - in English, but the sense was clear without translation:

"'My first syllable is what a ship does when it sinks. My second is what we are all doing now. And the whole is what we have not got now!'"

"Sink..." the American pondered. "Sink-i... No..."

"Hope!" someone shouted.

"No!" Mrs Aldridge triumphed. "Rest! Rest - that is when a ship sinks, it... well..." She got tangled. "Never mind. My charade was bad. Who is next?"

The saloon roared with laughter - for the first time in three days. Laughed hysterically, to tears, holding to the wet columns, but laughed.

The next to volunteer, to general surprise, was the ship's cook - a huge, broad-shouldered black man in a white apron, with a gold earring in his ear, who had made his way into the saloon with a cauldron of hot broth which he ladled out to all and sundry without asking what class anyone was. His name was Samuel, but everyone called him Cook Sam.

"I don't know charades, ma'am," he said in a deep, rumbling bass. "I know a riddle. A sea one. My grandfather set it to me, and his grandfather to him, there on the islands from where we were all brought." He paused, and the saloon grew quiet. "'I walk on the sea, but I am not a ship. I burn, but I am not fire. I am seen only in a storm. What am I?'"

All were silent. The storm roared beyond the bulkheads.

"A lantern?" suggested the American.

"Lightning," said the Chevalier.

Cook Sam shook his head - slowly, solemnly. And raised a hand, pointing upward, to the ceiling of the saloon, beyond which were the deck and the masts.

"St Elmo's fire," he said. "Dead sailors. Those who drowned but found no peace. They come in a storm and sit on the masts. Glow. Watch. Look for whom to take with them." He paused. "Last night I saw them. On all six masts. At once."

No one laughed.

"And whom are they looking for, Sam?" I asked.

The cook looked at me. Long. With a heavy, calm look.

"My grandfather said, sir," he said, "St Elmo's fire never takes one who glows himself. They look for those who are in the dark." He lifted his cauldron and moved on, ladling broth. "You, sir, have nothing to fear. You are yourself like a fire on a mast. I saw."

* * *

From the crowd at the staircase leading down to the third class came a voice - thin, girlish, trembling. Someone was singing. The saloon gradually quieted, turning toward the sound.

A girl was singing. Quite young, about sixteen, red-haired, in a patched dress and an enormous man's jacket from someone else's shoulders, barefoot, wet to the skin. She stood on a step, holding the handrail, and sang - in a language I did not know - a melody that made the heart contract. Slow, drawn out, sad, with so many repetitions that it seemed the song itself did not want to end.

"Irish," Prokhor whispered. "From the third class. There are seven hundred of them there. Going to America from famine. She came to ask for a doctor for her younger brother. He has a fever."

"What is she singing?"

"I do not know, sir. But they say it is a song of a girl who waited for a sailor on the shore for seven years. And in the eighth he returned - but she did not recognise him, because the sea had changed his face."

The girl finished. The saloon was silent. Then Mrs Aldridge - loud, absurd, gossiping Mrs Aldridge - silently rose, made her way through the water to the stairs, took off her own warm shawl and laid it on the girl's shoulders.

"What is your name, child?"

"Mary, ma'am. Mary Casey."

"Lead me to your brother, Mary Casey," said Mrs Aldridge. "I was a judge's wife for thirty years. I know how to treat a fever better than any ship's doctor." And, turning to the saloon, she commanded: "Cristóbal - someone! Hold the bird! Do not let it pray - it will frighten everyone!"

And went down into the third class, holding the red-haired girl by the hand.

The cage with the parrot was for some reason thrust upon me.

The parrot Cristóbal fixed me with a mad yellow eye. Then tilted his head - and suddenly, quite distinctly, not in Spanish but in some language which I had never heard but which echoed in me with a strange, distant tremor, croaked a single word.

I did not understand it. But it was like - I was for some reason sure of this - a name.

* * *

"He repeats what he heard from the missionary," said a man who had come up to me. "Do not be frightened. Parrots are the best keepers of other people's words. They remember what people have long forgotten."

It was a no longer young, tidy German in spectacles, in a frock coat buttoned on every button, with a barometer in his hands - a real one, in a brass case - at which he glanced as one glances at a watch, awaiting a train.

"Professor Gottfried Altmann," he introduced himself with a brief bow. "Heidelberg. Meteorology. I am going to study American storms." He smiled without mirth. "As you see, the American storm decided to come to me itself, without waiting."

"What does your instrument say, Professor?"

"My instrument," said Altmann, rapping the glass with a nail, "says what cannot be. The pressure has been falling for the fourth day. It is already lower than in any storm I have ever read in records. And records, young man, have been kept on ships for more than a hundred years." He raised his eyes to me. "A century storm, the sailors say. They do not know how right they are. A hundred years. Exactly. I checked the old logs in the captain's library - at this same latitude, on these same days, exactly a hundred years ago, an English frigate vanished. Two hundred men. In a storm described only by two survivors." He paused. "Their description coincides almost word for word with what is happening now."

"A coincidence," I said.

"Naturally," agreed Professor Altmann. "I am a scientist. I believe in coincidences." He rapped the barometer again. "But here is what is curious. One of those two survivors was a Russian. A young nobleman. He travelled with his servant. The records say that he stood on deck through the whole storm and that lightning passed him by. And then, on the last night, he went to the rail - and no one saw him again. The servant was saved. Said afterward that his master had not drowned. That he had 'gone back to where he came from.'"

In the saloon the storm roared. Cristóbal in the cage in my hands was silent, head tilted.

"What was the servant's name?" I asked. My voice sounded strange to me.

"That is not recorded in the log," said the Professor. "Only initials. 'P. I.'"

I slowly turned.

Prokhor Ilyich stood by a column three paces from me. Just as imperturbable. Just as calm. And looked at me with the same long, sad look with which he had looked in the cabin.

* * *

We returned to the cabin together. The trunk was still rolling on the floor. The porthole was still black.

I went to the small mirror fastened to the wall of the cabin and peered at my own reflection, swaying in time with the ship. A face like any - tired, haggard from insomnia, but a most ordinary, human face, such as thousands may be met on any Petersburg street. Nothing in it betrayed what I, if the scraps of my own memory were to be believed - and to believe them was growing harder with every day of this accursed storm - had once been.

I unbuttoned the collar of my shirt, drew the cloth aside, baring my shoulders - and ran my fingers over two barely visible, long-healed marks under the shoulder blades, symmetrical, as though left by something that had once been attached precisely there and afterward torn or hacked out by the root. On stormy nights like this these marks ached with a dull, old pain, like wounds healed in the flesh but never healed otherwise. Tonight blood showed on the shirt again. Mrs Aldridge's laundress had not lied.

"Wings," I whispered, addressing the reflection rather than Prokhor himself. "It is not merely that I fancy they were? Or am I really going mad, inventing for myself a divine origin in order somehow to justify my own worthlessness?"

"You are not going mad, sir," answered Prokhor in the same even tone. "You have simply been a man too long. Longer than is safe for anyone born not on earth."

I turned to him sharply, feeling something inside me - whether hope or irritation or both at once - break out faster than I could master it.

"The Professor said - a hundred years ago. This same storm. A Russian nobleman and his servant. 'P. I.'" I stepped toward him. "Prokhor. Was it you?"

Prokhor was silent a long time.

"I have crossed this ocean many times, sir," he said at last. "With you. You do not remember. You never remember."

"Then tell me! Tell me who I am, if I myself am not able to remember! You remember - you said yourself just now that you remember!"

Prokhor looked at me with a long, sad look - a look with far more human compassion in it than an ordinary servant would have.

"I could tell," he said quietly. "But this truth, I fear, will not lighten your burden but only double it. You fell to this earth of your own will, many centuries ago - not as punishment, but from curiosity, from thirst to understand what had remained incomprehensible even to you yourself, for all your antiquity and all your strength. You wished to live a human life not from afar, not through the veil of divine omniscience, but from within, with every cell, every nerve, every tear and every joy. And you lived it. Many times. So many that you forgot - and that, sir, is not madness, but simply the price paid by anyone who wears another's skin too long."

"The Reverend Crow called me Lucifer," I said. "Son of the morning. The fallen."

"The Reverend Crow reads a book that men wrote, sir," said Prokhor. "And men always write of those who came down to them one of two things: that he is a god or that he is a devil. The middle they do not understand." He paused. "Do you know what your name means in the language in which it was devised? Not 'fallen.' 'Bearer of light.' The morning star. The one that rises before the sun, so that people may know: the night is ending."

I sank down on the edge of the narrow bunk, feeling the earth - or rather the water, the ever-heaving water beneath us - slip from under my feet in a far deeper, far more frightening sense than the simple pitching of a storm.

"And you?" I asked after a long silence. "Who are you, if not a simple servant?"

"I am here to remember for you what you cannot hold yourself," answered Prokhor simply, with no solemnity in his voice. "And to remind you of it when the time comes. It seems to me, sir, that the time is drawing near."

* * *

There was a knock at the cabin door - loud, insistent. Prokhor opened.

On the threshold stood Mary Casey - the red-haired Irish girl in Mrs Aldridge's shawl, barefoot, wet, with enormous frightened eyes. Behind her, holding to the wall, was Mrs Aldridge herself, dishevelled, with sleeves rolled up.

"Count," said Mrs Aldridge without preamble. "The boy is worse. The ship's doctor is drunk as a cobbler and says he can do nothing." She paused. "The boy is delirious. He keeps repeating one thing. Mary says - in Gaelic. Something like 'bring the bright gentleman.' Mary thinks this is about you. I think it is nonsense. But I am a judge's widow, Count. I know: when nothing else is left, one must check even nonsense."

I followed them down.

The third class was hell. A dark, low hold, packed with people like a barrel with herrings. Swaying lanterns. Water to the knee. Cries, weeping, prayers in a dozen languages. The smell of vomit, wet wool and fear. Seven hundred people who had fled famine for the New World - and found a storm on the way.

The boy lay on a bunk by the very bulkhead - small, about six, red-haired like his sister, with burning cheeks and dry, cracked lips. His eyes were open but looked into nothing. He was whispering something.

I knelt down beside him. Mary said something to him in Gaelic. The boy slowly turned his head - and his eyes, clouded with fever, rested on me.

He smiled.

"You have come," he said. Not in Gaelic. Not in English. In that very language in which the parrot Cristóbal had croaked his word. In a language I did not know - but which I understood. "I knew you would come. You always come when it is dark."

I took his hot hand in mine. I do not know why. I simply took it - and held it.

He closed his eyes. His breathing - hoarse, rapid - gradually grew slower. Steadier. After some time which I did not count, Mary, sobbing, touched his forehead - and cried out.

"Cold," she whispered. "Ma'am, he... he is cold... that is... the fever has broken... he is sleeping..."

Mrs Aldridge bent, felt the boy's forehead, then his neck. Straightened. Looked at me. And for the first time since I had known her, said nothing.

I rose. My hand, with which I had held the boy's, burned - as though I had held it over a fire.

As I left, through the hold behind my back, in a whisper, from mouth to mouth, in a dozen languages, ran one and the same word. I did not turn. I did not wish to know which.

* * *

The ship lurched especially hard, and somewhere above, on deck, an alarmed, straining cry was heard - whether of the boatswain giving orders or of the wind itself, which had at last found a crack through which to shout all its fury. I rose from the hold and, saying not another word to Mrs Aldridge, nor to Mary, nor to Prokhor waiting at the stairs, made my way up.

"Sir," Prokhor called after me, for the first time in all this time allowing himself something like anxiety in his voice, "one should not go on deck in such weather."

"I need air," I lied - or did not lie at all, because at that moment I did not myself know for certain what I needed more: air, an answer, or simply deliverance from the unbearable weight of my own body, alien to me.

He did not stop me - perhaps he knew that to restrain me at such a moment was useless, and perhaps he understood something which at that moment I myself did not. A hundred years ago, I thought, climbing the companionway, he probably did not stop me either.

The deck met me with a wall of water and wind that struck me with such force that I barely kept my feet, clinging to the wet, icy rail. Lightning one after another split the sky over the raging sea, snatching from the darkness the crests of waves rising higher than the funnels themselves - and in this mad, dazzling chaos there was something remotely, frighteningly like freedom. That very freedom which I perhaps had not felt for thousands of years, since the very moment when I first descended to this earth in a guise so alien to my true nature.

On the masts - on all six masts at once - fires burned. Pale, bluish, trembling, like candles in the wind which no wind could blow out. St Elmo's fire. Dead sailors, Cook Sam had said. They look for those who are in the dark.

They did not look at me. They looked at the sea. All, as one. As if awaiting something from there.

By the mainmast, clinging to a rope, stood the Reverend Crow - hatless, grey hair plastered to his skull, the black book pressed to his chest. He saw me. And did not cry out. He simply looked - long, unwaveringly - and his lips moved. I could not hear the words over the roar of the wind. But from the movement of his lips I understood: he was not cursing. He was praying. For me.

I moved along the rail, to where the deck ended, breaking off into the blackness of the raging sea - step by step, whether in search of an answer or in search of an end to all that had accumulated in me over these long, forgotten centuries of human life. What if Prokhor is mistaken? What if there is no fallen grandeur hidden under this weary, haggard human shell - but only a lonely passenger, driven mad by storm and sleeplessness, who has imagined himself something greater than a mere mortal because to admit his own unremarkable madness was simply unbearable for him?

Gone back to where he came from, said the servant a hundred years ago.

I stopped at the very rail, feeling icy spray on my face, feeling the wind tear at my clothes as if trying to strip them off together with the last remnants of the human guise to which I had clung so long and so unsuccessfully. Below, a few feet beneath me, the water roared - black, bottomless, ready to take into itself absolutely anything it was allowed, without a single question, without a single demand for explanations.

I laid my hands on the rail, preparing to throw over it a body so dubiously my own - and in that very instant the world around me froze.

Not time - time flowed on as before, I felt it with every cell of my wind-blown body - but something else, something within the storm itself. The lightning that split the sky at that moment hung for a fraction of a second longer than lightning should hang, pouring over the raging sea a light so even, so unnaturally long, that the very night for a moment seemed day. The fires on all six masts blazed up at once - and turned. All. To me. And the water beneath me, for just one short breath, ceased to obey the wind - froze in a ripple, stood in a wave that neither fell nor moved, as if the element itself held its breath, awaiting my next step with the same tense attention with which I myself awaited the answer to a question never asked aloud.

And in that frozen, glowing silence I heard behind me - very close, quite near - the hoarse, mocking voice of the parrot Cristóbal, who could not possibly have been here, on deck, in the storm. He was repeating that very word. The one. In the language I did not know.

My name.

I almost remembered it.
