Chapter XXXV
The Order

The letter with the green seal lay inside my shirt for three days before I resolved to carry it.

Not because I was afraid. Though I was afraid, too. But because every evening, lying down on the divan in Suleyman's house, I would take it out, lay it on my palm and look at the seal. Two circles, one filled, the other empty. A wavy line between them. The same sign that Kemal drew in charcoal on the flagstones of the courtyard. That had shown as a watermark in the paper of my note. That had been carved above a bench in... where? I did not know where. It seemed to me, in a round room, underground, among glowing stars I had never seen.

On the fourth morning Ferhat sat down beside me on the balcony, looked at the letter in my hand and said:

"You will not hand it over until you stop being afraid. And you will not stop being afraid until you hand it over." He paused. "Grandfather says that about debts. Come. I will walk you to the gates."

* * *

The old palace of the sultans stood at the very tip of the cape, where the Golden Horn meets the Bosphorus and the Bosphorus meets the Sea of Marmara, where three waters flow into one. From the sea, from Mustafa's boat, I had seen its walls, ancient, rising straight from the water, and the cypresses above them, and the domes, and the golden spires of the pavilions. Now we walked to it by land, from Hagia Sophia, past a fountain with a carved canopy, across a square, to an enormous gate.

The gate was such that an elephant with a palanquin on its back might have passed through. A high arch faced with marble, with inscriptions in gold on black above it, sayings which Ferhat read aloud to me slowly, haltingly: "The Sultan, the shadow of Allah on earth..." On either side of the gate were niches. Ferhat lowered his voice.

"They used to display heads here," he said. "Of the executed. Viziers, pashas, rebels. So that all could see what befalls those who disobey." He paused. "Grandfather saw it when he was a boy. The last one. He says it still comes to him in dreams."

The niches were empty. Only pigeons sat in them, ruffled, looking at us with round orange eyes.

At the gate stood two guards, in blue uniforms of the new pattern, with fezzes on their heads and rifles, smoking, leaning against the marble with the air of men who long ago understood that they are guarding emptiness.

"The Sultan does not live here any more," Ferhat explained. "Twenty years. He moved to the new palace, on the shore, the one they are still building. And here, archives. The treasury. Old servants. Kitchens where once they cooked for five thousand and now for fifty." He smiled crookedly. "And shadows."

He showed the guards the letter. Without opening it, only the seal. Green wax. Two circles.

The guards exchanged glances. One threw down his cigarette, crushed it with his heel and, without a word, went off into the depths of the courtyard. The other signalled with a gesture to wait.

We waited long. The sun rose above the domes. The pigeons cooed in their niches.

Then from the depths of the courtyard, from behind the cypresses, slowly, with dignity, a man came out.

* * *

He was enormous. Not fat: enormous. Taller than anyone I had met in this city, broad in the shoulders but soft, flabby, as though modelled from dark clay and not yet fired. His skin was black, deep, like a night without a moon. His face smooth, beardless, round, with heavy eyelids and a soft, almost womanly mouth. On his head a tall white cap like a sugar-loaf, wound at the base with white muslin. A long robe, dark green, silk, trimmed with fur despite the heat. At his belt a dagger in a silver sheath and a bunch of keys. Many keys. Old. Huge. Jingling at every step, like the housekeeper's in the old house behind the grille, I thought, and did not understand where the thought came from.

Beside me Ferhat drew his head into his shoulders.

"The agha," he whispered. "A eunuch. One of the old ones. Who served in the harem." He swallowed. "Misafir, be polite. At court they are a power. Even now."

The eunuch stopped three paces away. Glanced at Ferhat, fleetingly, as one glances at furniture. Then at me, long, attentively, from above.

"The letter," he said. His voice was unexpectedly high, thin, melodious, like a choirboy's, and from this mismatch with his enormous body a chill ran down my back.

I held out the letter. He took it with two fingers, huge, soft, nails dyed with henna. Looked at the seal. Did not open it.

"Ahmet," he said. Not asking. "The old madman. Forty years waiting." He raised his eyes to me. "I am Sumbul Agha. Keeper of the keys of the old palace. Follow me." He looked at Ferhat. "And you wait here."

"I am his interpreter, Agha," Ferhat stammered.

"He needs no interpreter," said the eunuch. "Where he is going, all languages are spoken." A pause. "And none."

* * *

We walked a long time.

Through the first courtyard, enormous, overgrown with grass, with plane trees under which, as Sumbul Agha said, janissaries once stood waiting for their pay, and where now someone's goats grazed. Through the second gate, with two octagonal towers like pencils, where the eunuch stopped and said: "Here, beneath these towers, the executioner waited for the viziers whom the Sultan invited to supper." Through the second courtyard, with a long gallery beneath which ran the chimneys of the kitchens, twenty, thirty, forty flues, and the eunuch said: "Here they cooked for five thousand. Pilaf, sherbet, baklava. I remember the smell. Sixty years ago. I was a boy. They brought me from the Sudan. I was eight."

"They... brought you?"

"Bought," said Sumbul Agha evenly. "In the market in Cairo. For twenty gold pieces. Made me what I am. And brought me here." He paused. "I do not complain. I have long since stopped complaining. I served three sultans. I saw princes die. Sultanas weep. Viziers cut down. I held the keys of rooms where no one entered but the Sultan." He jingled the bunch at his belt. "And of one room where not even the Sultan entered."

"Which?"

Sumbul Agha did not answer. We passed through a third gate, narrow, dark, and found ourselves in a third courtyard. Small, quiet, planted with roses and cypresses. Here were no goats, no guards, no chimneys. Only a fountain murmuring in a marble basin, and pavilions around, graceful, faced with tiles, blue, turquoise, white, with golden inscriptions, with domes like caps.

The eunuch led me to one of the pavilions, the most modest, in the far corner, by the very wall beyond which below the sea was sounding. The doors were wooden, dark, without ornament. He took from his belt a key, the largest, the oldest, black with age, and inserted it in the lock. Turned it.

"Here is the archive," he said. "Not the one for officials. That is in the other wing, papers about taxes, wars, land." He opened the door. "This is another. No Sultan entered here. No vizier. No mufti. Only we."

"Who is we?"

Sumbul Agha looked at me with a heavy, calm gaze from under swollen lids.

"Those who guard the threshold," he said. "Enter. He is waiting."

* * *

Inside it was dark, cool and smelled of paper.

Not as it smells in a bookseller's shop or in Suleyman's library. Differently. Deeper. Older. As it must smell in the earth where bones have lain a thousand years: dry, bitter, sweet. It smelled of parchment, leather, sealing wax, ink of oak-galls, cedar shelves, the dust of ages, and of something else, barely perceptible, bitterish, steppe-like, like wormwood in August.

The room was round.

I did not grasp it at once; at first my eyes grew accustomed to the half-dark, to the thin rays falling through narrow windows just below the dome. Then I saw. The walls were shelves. From floor to dome. In a ring. Without beginning and without end. And on the shelves, books. Scrolls. Notebooks. Bundles of letters. Caskets. Chests. Wooden boards covered with signs. Clay tablets. Parchments rolled in tubes and tied with cords of every colour.

Thousands. Tens of thousands.

And in the centre of the round room, just beneath the dome, behind a low table heaped with papers, sat an old man.

* * *

He was tiny. So small that he was scarcely visible behind the pile of books on the table, like a nestling in its nest. Dry, bent, with a face like crumpled parchment, with a perfectly white beard, long, thin, forked at the end like a swallow's tail. On his head a modest white turban, carelessly wound, slipping to one side. On his nose, spectacles. Two pairs, one over the other. And behind them his eyes, huge, magnified by the lenses, black, shining, alive, like a young man's.

His fingers, thin, long, black to the second joint with ink, lay on an open book. He did not raise his head when we came in.

"Sumbul," he said. His voice was quiet, dry, rustling, like a page being turned. "You are late. The sun is already above the second gate."

"He stood at the gate for three hours, Efendi," said the eunuch. "He was afraid."

"He was right to be afraid." The old man at last raised his head. Looked at me through the double spectacles. Long. "Did Ahmet Baba seal the letter?"

"He did."

"With the seal I gave him forty years ago?"

"With that one."

The old man nodded. Held out his hand. Sumbul Agha laid the letter in it. The old man broke the green wax, carefully, as one breaks bread at a wake, unfolded the sheet. Read. A single line; I saw there was only one line. Folded the sheet. Laid it on the table.

"'Arrived,'" he said. "He wrote one word. 'Arrived.'" The old man took off the upper pair of spectacles, wiped them, put them on again. "Forty years of waiting, and one word. Ahmet was always sparing with words. It is his only virtue."

He stood. He was even smaller than he had seemed sitting. He barely reached my shoulder.

"I am Niyaz," he said. "Niyaz Efendi. The Keeper. A hundred and two years." He bowed slightly. "And you are the guest. Without a name. With two stones. With a note in Farsi. With a blank sheet." He paused. "Sit, guest. We have much to speak of. And I am old. I may not have time."

* * *

I sat. On a cushion by the table. Sumbul Agha remained by the door, enormous, motionless as a statue.

Niyaz Efendi sat opposite. Laid his palms on the table, on an open book. Thick, huge, bound in dark leather, with brass clasps, with brass corners. Its pages were yellow, thin, closely, finely written, in different hands, different inks, in different languages.

"This is the Defter," he said. "The register. In your tongue, a book of entries. We call it simply the Book of Guests." He stroked a page. "Here are written all. All whom we know. All who have come."

"How many are there?"

Niyaz Efendi looked at me over his spectacles.

"Nine hundred and seventeen," he said. "Entries." He paused. "But I am not sure they are nine hundred and seventeen people."

"What does that mean?"

"It means, guest," said Niyaz Efendi quietly, "that for a hundred and two years I have been reading this book. And my teacher read it eighty years before me. And his teacher, sixty. And so on, back, back, back. Eight hundred years. We, keepers, have been twenty-three." He paused. "And not one of the twenty-three has been able to answer one question. The simplest."

"Which?"

"How many there were," said the old man. "Nine hundred and seventeen. Or a hundred. Or one."

* * *

"Would you like me to tell you how we began?" asked Niyaz Efendi. "Ahmet Baba surely hinted. He likes to hint. It is from the Franks."

"Tell me."

The old man leaned back on the cushions. Folded his hands on his stomach. Closed his eyes, behind the two pairs of lenses.

"Alexandria," he said. "Egypt. A city built by Iskander the Two-Horned, Alexander in your tongue, on the sea shore, where the Nile flows into the Great Green." He paused. "There was a Library there. The greatest in the world. Seven hundred thousand scrolls. All that men knew. Greeks, Egyptians, Persians, Jews, Indians. All. And at the Library served keepers. Like me. Like my teacher. They copied scrolls, mended, sorted. And once, long ago, very long ago, under the last kings of the house of the Ptolemies, a man came to the Library."

I waited. I knew what would follow. I had heard it, in different words, in different languages, so many times already.

"Young," said Niyaz Efendi. "Without a name. Not remembering who he was. In strange clothes. With two stones in his hand, black and white. He came and asked to be shown the oldest scrolls. The Egyptian ones. Those that priests had brought from the temples." The old man paused. "The keeper, his name was Theokles, showed them. And the guest began to read. Scrolls that no one could read. Written in signs that even the priests had forgotten. He read them as we read a child's primer. Aloud. A week. A month."

"What was in the scrolls?"

"Theokles wrote it down," said Niyaz Efendi. "Not all, he could not keep up. But something." The old man bent over the Defter, turned back, far, to the very beginning, to pages that were not paper but parchment, dark, brittle. "Here. The first entry. In Theokles' hand. In Greek."

He read, slowly, running his black finger along the line:

"'The guest read a scroll brought from the temple at Heliopolis and said: here it is written that one day one of the First will return, not remembering himself, and will walk in a circle until he remembers where the beginning is. And I asked him: who are the First? And he laughed and said: I do not remember. But it seems that I am one of them.'"

In the round room it became very quiet.

"And then," Niyaz Efendi went on, "the guest left. At night. As they always leave. And left two things on Theokles' table. A stone, white. And a sheet of papyrus with one line. In signs that no one could read." The old man raised his eyes to me. "Theokles kept them all his life. And before death passed them to a pupil. And said: 'Write. If another comes, write it down. And pass it to your pupil.'" Niyaz Efendi paused. "So it began. We."

"The Keepers of the Threshold."

"The Keepers of the Threshold," the old man nodded. "Then the Library burned. Not at once, in parts. Caesar, the Christians, the Arabs, whoever did not burn it. And the keepers left. With the book. With the stone. With the papyrus. To Constantinople, to the Greeks. Then some went to Baghdad, to the House of Wisdom, to the caliphs. Then to Cordoba, to the Andalusian emirs. Then to Samarkand, to Ulugh Beg, who watched the stars. Then back to Constantinople, when ours took it." He stroked the Defter. "The book walked the world. Eight hundred years. From continent to continent. And wherever it was, guests came. And we wrote them down."

* * *

"Show me," I said. My voice sounded as if from far away.

Niyaz Efendi nodded. And began to turn the pages.

He turned slowly. Page after page. Century after century. And read, now aloud, now to himself, moving his lips, now in Greek, now in Arabic, now in Persian, now in languages I did not recognise, and translated for me without lifting his eyes from the lines.

"Constantinople. The year eight hundred and ninety-one of the Hijra. The guest climbed the wall at Blachernae on the last night before the assault. Gave a coin to the Greek commander, Manuel of the house of Melissenos. Said: 'The city will change its name, but will remain.'" The old man raised his eyes. "Do you know this entry?"

"I know it," I whispered. "Kyra Theodora..."

"Kyra Theodora is our 'well,'" said Niyaz Efendi. "So we call the families to whom guests come. To drink. They are not members of the Order. They do not know of us. But we know of them." He turned a page. "Tarnovo. Bulgaria. The year Tsarevets fell. The guest planted a rose by the monastery. Left, leaving two stones." Another page. "Tabriz. Persia. The guest came to a merchant and asked him to write out his accounts in shikasta script. The merchant taught him. The guest left. Forty years later the merchant taught that script to a young Turkish trader named Hasan." The old man looked at me. "Hasan Amca. Your red-bearded friend. He does not know that he learned the hand in which your note was written."

Cold beneath the ribs. On the left.

"Khiva," read Niyaz Efendi. "The Turkmen sands. The guest came to a one-eyed old man at a well. Left a scroll. Said: 'Give it to him who comes for water. He will not be the one. But he will pass it on.'" Another page. "Jinan. China. A monastery in the mountains. The guest folded paper cranes. Nine hundred and ninety-nine. The last he did not fold, saying: 'Let another fold it.'" The old man paused. "Two hundred years later a Japanese monk in a temple at Shimoda received this story from his teacher. And began to fold himself. Nine hundred and ninety-nine. The last he gave to..." he faltered, turned the page, squinted, "a Russian. An officer. From a frigate wrecked by a wave."

"The General," I said. And did not myself understand where that word came from.

Niyaz Efendi gave me a long look.

"It does not say 'General' here," he said. "It says: 'a Russian officer who seeks faces.'"

* * *

He turned on. The pages grew younger, the paper whiter, the ink brighter.

"Argentina. The pampa. The guest comes to the shepherds' fire every forty years. Does not age. Sings the duel. Leaves without answering." Niyaz Efendi raised a finger. "A Jesuit wrote us of it. A Spaniard. A member of our order in Buenos Aires. A hundred years ago."

"You have people in Buenos Aires?"

"We have people everywhere, guest," said the old man calmly. "Few. One or two in each great city. They do not know one another. They write here. Once a year. Once in ten years. When there is something to write." He turned a page. "An island in the Pacific. Fiji. The guest walked over red-hot stones, and the stones cooled. An English missionary wrote to us. The one who was later eaten." Niyaz Efendi shook his head. "A good man. Neat. Wrote legibly."

"England?"

"England," the old man nodded. Turned a page. "Salisbury Plain. A circle of stones. The guest came at dawn on the summer solstice. White light from above. The guest held on to a stone and did not leave." Niyaz Efendi raised his eyes. "Did not leave, guest. For the first time in eight hundred years of records, a guest did not leave. He held on to the stone. A shepherd wrote to us. Illiterate. At the vicar's dictation. The vicar sent the letter through London, through our man in the Rothschild bank."

"When was that?"

Niyaz Efendi looked at the page. Then at me. Then at the page again.

"There is no date here," he said slowly. "There is only the entry. It... appeared."

"How, appeared?"

"By itself," said the old man. "One morning. I opened the book, and it was there. In the shepherd's hand. At dictation. With the vicar's signature." He paused. "But no letter from London had come. I checked. No letter had come at all."

* * *

Niyaz Efendi was silent for a long time. Then closed the Defter. Laid his palms on it.

"There are entries," he said quietly, "that we did not write. They appear by themselves. My teacher called them 'the arrived.' We do not know who writes them. We do not know when. Sometimes they are of the past. Sometimes of what is happening now, far away, in another city." He paused. "And sometimes of what has not yet been."

"Of the future?"

The old man did not answer. He opened the Defter again, but not at the beginning, not in the middle, but at the very end. Where the pages should have been blank. They were not.

"Here," he said. "Look for yourself. I shall not read aloud. I do not want Sumbul to hear. He sleeps badly enough as it is."

I bent over the book.

The last pages were covered in different hands. Some in the familiar script. Some in letters I had never seen, angular, clear, even, as if printed. And among them one entry, made in ordinary ink, but in such a hand that I froze.

Mine.

With a blot at the end. Though I make a blot only when I hold the pen back.

I could not read those letters. But I read.

"The city of two towers. The fifty-first tier. A guest with a sailor's chest. The machine asks: 'What should I feel when I see the ocean?' The guest does not know the answer. The guest says: 'Perhaps that you are at home.'"

Beneath this entry, another. Shorter. In the same hand.

"A Russian general will sail into the city. With him a young man. With the guest's face."

And lower still, quite short. One line. In the script.

I could not read it. At all. I looked at it, and the lines swam, flowed, slipped away like water through fingers.

"This one," said Niyaz Efendi quietly, "appeared yesterday. In the morning. When Ahmet Baba was sealing the letter." He paused. "I cannot read it. None of us can. Twenty-three keepers in eight hundred years have not been able to read a single line of the script. Not one." He raised his eyes to me, huge, black, magnified by two pairs of lenses. "Can you?"

I looked at the line. The lines flowed. Curved. Slipped away.

And suddenly, for a moment, shorter than a breath, they stopped. Settled.

Three signs I recognised. Not read, recognised. As faces are recognised in a crowd.

"Keep." "Appointed time." "House."

The rest was gone.

"No," I said. "I cannot. Almost."

Niyaz Efendi looked at me for a long time. Then slowly closed the book.

"Almost," he repeated. "Eight hundred years, no one. And you, almost." He took off his spectacles. Both pairs. Wiped them. For a long time. "So the appointed time is near."

* * *

"Niyaz Efendi," I said. "Who created us?"

The old man raised his eyebrows.

"Us?"

"The guests. The world. Everything." I did not know how to phrase it. "If guests come again and again, someone sends them. Or something. If entries appear by themselves, someone writes them. Who? God? Gods? The First? Who?"

Niyaz Efendi was silent for a long time. Then he rose, with difficulty, leaning on the table, and went to a shelf. Took down three books. Different ones. Laid them before me on the table.

"In the Order, guest," he said, "they have argued about this for eight hundred years. Three schools. Three answers. None has prevailed."

He laid his palm on the first book, thick, in a green binding, with golden tooling in Arabic script.

"The theologians," he said. "They say: the guests are messengers. Angels. Envoys of God. He sends them into the world to remind people of what people have forgotten. The guest does not remember himself because an angel clothed in human flesh must forget heaven, or the flesh would not endure." Niyaz Efendi paused. "A beautiful answer. Ahmet Baba was once in this school. Then he left."

He laid a hand on the second book, thin, in a black binding, without inscriptions.

"The philosophers," he said. "Followers of Ibn Arabi, Suhrawardi, of the Greeks, Plotinus, Plato. They say: the world is a breath. The First Intellect exhales the world as we exhale air. And each breath is the world anew. The same, but a little different." The old man paused. "Guests are those who remember the previous exhalation. A little. In fragments. They wander through the new world, recognising it and not recognising it. As you wander through this city." He looked at me. "They say: the guest is not created. The guest is the world's own memory of itself."

He laid a hand on the third book. A strange one. Not a book even, but a bundle of copper plates fastened with rings, covered with thin, precise drawings: wheels, levers, pipes, gears, water clocks, mechanical birds, figures of men with raised hands.

"And this," said Niyaz Efendi, and his voice dropped, "is the mechanics. The smallest school. It was founded in Baghdad, at the House of Wisdom, by a keeper who was a friend of the Banu Musa brothers, those who built machines. Mechanical birds that sang. Flute-players that played by themselves. Clocks that showed the stars." He stroked a copper plate. "The mechanics say: the world is a machine. An enormous one. More complex than anything imaginable. And someone built it."

"God?"

"No," said Niyaz Efendi. "Not God." He paused. "The mechanics say a strange thing, guest. A heresy. For it they were burned in three cities. They say: the machine of the world was built by a guest. Himself. At the end of time. When he had learned how. When he had built another machine, a small, clever one, that learned to think better than he. And that machine worked out how time is arranged. And built the world. Anew. From the very beginning." The old man looked at me. "And placed in this world a guest. Empty. Without memory. So that he should walk the whole way again. And build it again."

In the round room it was so quiet that I could hear, beyond the wall, below, the sea beating on the stones.

"It is a circle," I said.

"It is a circle," Niyaz Efendi agreed. "It has no beginning." He paused. "So say the mechanics. The theologians say, it has. God. And the philosophers say, the circle itself is the beginning."

"And you?" I asked. "What do you think?"

Niyaz Efendi looked for a long time at the three books before him. Green. Black. Copper.

"I think, guest," he said at last, "that for a hundred and two years I have been reading this book. And that I am an old man who will soon die." He raised his eyes to me. "And that it is all one to me who created the world, God, or breath, or a machine." He paused. "What interests me is something else. Who created the one who created."

"Reason will ask who created the creator."

I did not know where those words came from. Some professor. Some lecture hall. A grey beard. Chalk on his fingers.

I blinked. The vision passed.

* * *

Niyaz Efendi rang a small silver bell. Sumbul Agha at the door stirred, went out, and returned with a tray. Coffee. Black, thick, in tiny cups. Rahat lokum, pink, in powdered sugar. And, to my surprise, a saucer of dried apricots, exactly like those that Ferhat's grandmother passed me from behind the curtain.

"Sumbul knows what you like," said Niyaz Efendi. "Yesterday he was at Suleyman's. Buying silk." The old man tittered. "Suleyman does not know to whom he sold the silk. He thought it was merely a palace eunuch. Suleyman does not know much altogether. That is good. Wells must not know that they are wells."

"Suleyman is a 'well'?"

"His father was a well," said Niyaz Efendi. "The foreigner with the letter whom he saw as a boy, he is recorded here. Entry two hundred and thirty-nine." The old man sipped his coffee. "And Suleyman became a well himself. When he began to feed all the wanderers. We did not ask. He did it himself."

"And Karapet? The jeweller?"

"A well. Four generations."

"Hatice Hanim? The widow with the cats?"

"A well," said Niyaz Efendi. "Her husband was ours. A member of the Order. Thirty years." The old man set down his cup. "The chest in the cellar is ours. We put it there. Two hundred years ago. When the house belonged to a cloth merchant who was also ours." He paused. "The cats we did not place. The cats came by themselves."

"What is in the chest?"

Niyaz Efendi was silent for a long time.

"I do not know," he said at last. "Honestly, guest. I do not know. No one has opened it. Two hundred years. It cannot be opened. Not by key, nor crowbar, nor prayer. We tried." The old man looked at me. "In an entry two hundred years ago it is said: 'The chest will open when two meet.'" He paused. "Two of what is not said."

* * *

The door of the round room creaked.

Sumbul Agha turned so quickly that the bunch of keys at his belt rang like an alarm bell. His hand fell on his dagger.

In the doorway stood Kemal.

Barefoot. In a dusty shirt. With charcoal behind his ear. Out of breath, evidently he had run. He stood on the threshold and looked, not at me, not at the eunuch, not at the old man. At the shelves. At the thousands of books, scrolls, notebooks. In a ring. Without beginning and without end.

"How did you get in here?" I whispered.

Kemal did not answer. He looked at the shelves, and on his face was an expression I had never seen there. Not surprise. Not fear. Recognition.

Sumbul Agha took a step toward him, enormous, menacing, in green silk.

"Stop, Sumbul," said Niyaz Efendi quietly.

The eunuch stopped.

The old man slowly rose from behind the table. Went up to the boy. They were nearly of a height, the tiny hundred-year-old keeper and the twelve-year-old boy with charcoal behind his ear. Niyaz Efendi looked at him for a long time through two pairs of spectacles.

"You passed through three gates," he said. In Turkish, but I understood. "Past the guards. Past Sumbul. Through the third courtyard, where no one enters." He paused. "How?"

Kemal shrugged. Then raised a hand and pointed up. At the dome. At the place where, just beneath the vault, in a narrow window, sat a pigeon. White. With feathered feet and a fan tail.

"Akmal's dove," I said. "She..."

"Showed the way," said Niyaz Efendi. Not asking. He looked at the dove for a long time. Then again at the boy. "You see the underside," he said. "Yes?"

Kemal nodded.

Niyaz Efendi took off the upper pair of spectacles. Held them out to the boy.

"Look," he said. "At the shelves. Through them."

Kemal put on the spectacles. Huge, on his small face. Looked at the shelves. For a long time.

And suddenly smiled. Broadly. Brightly. As I had never seen him smile.

He pointed a finger at one shelf. High. Just beneath the dome. At the place where stood a small notebook in a blue cloth binding, quite new, not at all like the ancient scrolls around.

"What is there?" I asked Niyaz Efendi.

The old man looked where the boy pointed. For a long time. Then slowly took off the second pair of spectacles. Rubbed his eyes.

"There is nothing there," he said quietly. "That shelf is empty. Forty years." He paused. "What do you see, boy?"

Kemal took off the spectacles. Returned them to the old man. And said, the first long sentence I had heard from him:

"A blue notebook. With blots. It has not been written yet. But it is already standing there."

* * *

After this Niyaz Efendi was silent a long time.

Then he returned to the table. Sat. Laid his hands on the Defter. Looked at Kemal, still standing in the doorway, with the white dove, which had flown down onto his shoulder.

"Sumbul," he said. "Bring the boy apricots. And a cushion. Let him sit."

The eunuch, after long hesitation, obeyed.

"Guest," said Niyaz Efendi, turning to me. "I must tell you something. Not as a keeper. As an old man." He paused. "In the Order not all think alike. Not only about who created the world. About you."

"About me?"

"About guests," he corrected himself. "There are those who think as I do: guests must be recorded. Helped. Released. Not hindered. Let them go where they go. Let them arrive." The old man paused. "And there are others. Fewer. But stronger. They have money. Connections. People in the palaces of Europe."

"What do they want?"

"They split from us," said Niyaz Efendi. "Long ago. Three hundred years ago. In Venice. One keeper, a Venetian, decided that we were fools. That for eight hundred years we only record and do nothing. He said: the guest knows how to open the door. The guest himself does not remember, but the knowledge is in him. So he must not be released but held. Studied. To understand how he walks in a circle. And learn to walk it ourselves." The old man paused. "They called themselves Ordo Liminis. The Order of the Threshold. Almost like us." He smiled crookedly. "Only we are the guardians of the threshold. And they are those who wish to cross it."

A man in grey. With a cane. In a top hat. Smelling of bergamot. Whose face cannot be remembered. "We seek the same as you. Only from the other end."

"The man in grey," I said. "In the gallery of Hagia Sophia. Is he one of them?"

"One of them," Niyaz Efendi nodded. "We call him the Venetian. Though he is not from Venice. He is from Geneva. Or London. Or Vienna. He changes cities like gloves." The old man paused. "He came to Ahmet Baba. To Kyra Theodora. To the widow Hatice, twice. Tried to buy the chest. She drove him out. The cats helped."

"And how does he know about me?"

Niyaz Efendi looked at me for a long time.

"They too have a book," he said. "A copy of ours. Incomplete. The Venetian stole it three hundred years ago. Since then they write their own." He paused. "And in their book, guest, entries also appear. By themselves. The same as in ours." The old man tapped the Defter with a finger. "They have read about the city of two towers. About the machine that asks about the ocean. About the Russian general." He paused. "They know that you will come. They knew before you."

* * *

"Niyaz Efendi," I said slowly. "The city of two towers. The machine. The ocean." I faltered. "I do not know what that is. I have never seen a city of two towers. I know no machine."

"I know," said the old man. "It has not yet been. Or has already been, but not here." He paused. "The philosophers would say, it is the next exhalation. The mechanics, that it is where the machine is being built. The theologians, that it is a vision sent to you by God." Niyaz Efendi shrugged. "I do not know, guest. I only know that this entry is in your hand. And that it has a blot. And you have the habit of making a blot when you hold the pen back."

"How do you know about the blot?"

"Because," said Niyaz Efendi quietly, "in our book there are nine hundred and seventeen entries. And a hundred and twelve of them carry this blot. In different inks. With different pens. In different languages. Eight hundred years." He paused. "All in one hand."

I sat without moving. Kemal at the door stopped chewing his apricot. Sumbul Agha stood like a statue.

"Niyaz Efendi," I said. "Did I write them?"

The old man did not answer. He looked at me, long, endlessly long, through two pairs of spectacles, with huge, black, century-old eyes, and in those eyes was something I could not read. Not pity. Not fear. Not hope.

Patience. Ancient, endless patience of a man who has waited all his life, and has been rewarded, and understood that the waiting is only beginning.

"You will tell me that, guest," he said at last. "When you remember."

* * *

At parting he gave me three things.

The first, a sheet. Thin, old, yellowed parchment, folded in four. I unfolded it. A map. Drawn in ink, small, careful. Not one map but several, laid over one another, translucent. A city on two shores. A strait. A bay. Hills. And on each slightly different. On one, the dome of Hagia Sophia with a cross, without minarets. On another, with minarets, as now. On a third, with minarets and a cross at once. On a fourth, no dome at all, only ruins. On the fifth, above the city, above the strait, a bridge flung from one shore to the other, huge, impossible.

"What is this?" I asked.

"The city," said Niyaz Efendi. "As the guests saw it. Different. Each told his own. A keeper a hundred years ago drew them all, one over another." He paused. "The philosophers say these are different exhalations. The mechanics, different machines. The theologians, that the guests were raving." The old man smiled. "I think that somewhere there is a city where Hagia Sophia stands with cross and crescent together. And where the marble king has woken and made peace with the Sultan. And where the Russians did nail their shield to the gates after all, but no one remembers why." He shrugged. "A hundred and two years, and I am still a dreamer. Ahmet would laugh."

The second thing was a key. Small, copper, warm.

"To the chest?" I asked.

"No," said Niyaz Efendi. "To this room." He paused. "I am old. I shall soon die. Sumbul too is old. I have no pupils; the last died of cholera two years ago." The old man looked at Kemal. "Perhaps one day I shall have a pupil. One who sees the underside. And perhaps not." He laid the key in my palm. "If I am gone and you are here, come. Read. Write. Make your blot. Someone must."

And the third...

The third he did not put into my hands. He simply said it. Quietly. Looking me in the eyes.

"Guest. A Russian general will sail into this city. In a month, perhaps. With him a young man. With your face." He paused. "When you see him, do not be afraid. And do not run." A pause. "In an entry two hundred years ago it is said: 'The chest will open when two meet.' I thought it was of the two stones. For forty years I thought so." He shook his head. "Now I am not sure."

* * *

Sumbul Agha saw us to the gates, me and Kemal, with the white dove on his shoulder.

We walked through the third courtyard, past the roses and the fountain, through the second gate with the pencil towers, through the first courtyard, where goats grazed beneath the plane trees. The eunuch was silent. The keys at his belt jingled with every step.

At the first gate, where Ferhat was waiting, pale, worn out, leaping up to meet us with a face as though we had been gone a week, Sumbul Agha stopped.

"Guest," he said in his high, thin, boyish voice. "Sixty years ago, when they brought me here as a boy, the first night I wept. In the far corner of the third courtyard, by the wall above the sea. Alone." He paused. "And a man came to me. Young. Without a name. He sat down beside me. Said nothing. Simply sat. Until morning. And in the morning, leaving, he said: 'You will hold the keys of the threshold. Hold them firmly.'" The eunuch looked at me. "I hold them. Sixty years."

"What did he look like?" I asked.

Sumbul Agha looked into my face for a long time.

"I was a child," he said. "It was dark. I was crying." A pause. "But when you stood at the gates this morning for three hours, afraid to enter, I watched you from behind the cypresses. And I was eight years old again."

He turned and went back, into the depths of the empty palace, jingling his keys.

* * *

We walked home through the evening Sultanahmet, Ferhat, Kemal and I. Akmal's dove flew above us, circled, alighted on cornices and took off again.

"Well?" Ferhat asked at last, unable to hold back. "What was there? What did he say?"

I was silent a long time.

"He said that there are people who for eight hundred years have been recording those like me," I said at last. "That their records have travelled the world, from Alexandria to Baghdad, from Cordoba to Samarkand. That your grandfather is a 'well.' And Karapet. And Kyra Theodora. And the widow with the cats. That the chest in her cellar was placed there two hundred years ago. That there are others, from Venice, who wish to hold me. And that in a month a Russian general will sail into the city. With a man with my face."

Ferhat stopped in the middle of the street.

"With your face?"

"With mine."

Ferhat looked at me for a long time. Then at Kemal. Kemal walked beside us clutching a dried apricot in his fist and said nothing.

"Misafir," said Ferhat quietly. "Remember Grandfather said that everyone has a double? Somewhere in the world. Sometimes in another city. Sometimes in another time."

"I remember."

"I joked then," said Ferhat. "That I hoped my double could read Egyptian scribbles." He paused. "And now I am not amused."

We stood in the middle of the street. The sun was setting. From the minarets the evening call floated, one voice, a second, a third, until the whole city was singing.

And I suddenly thought, not with the mind, with something beneath the ribs, on the left, of the three books on Niyaz Efendi's table. Green, black, copper. God. Breath. Machine.

And of the question the old man had asked. Not who created the world. But who created the one who created.

And that perhaps the answer to this question is not in the past and not in the future. Not in Alexandria and not in the city of two towers. But there, where I am looking.

Kemal tugged my sleeve. Pointed upward.

Akmal's dove sat on the edge of a roof against the setting sun. White. Motionless. And beside her, for a single moment, in the blinding sunset light, I thought I saw a shadow. Tall. Taller than any shadow a pigeon can cast.

Kemal raised his hand and waved to it.

The shadow vanished. The dove took wing and flew home.

"He is going home too," said Kemal. In Turkish. Quietly. But I understood.

"Who?"

Kemal looked at me with his serious, un-childlike eyes.

"The one who will be," he said.
