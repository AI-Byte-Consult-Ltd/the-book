Chapter XII
A Night by the Temple Pond

The moon rose slowly that night, reluctantly, as though the weariness of the parched earth had been passed on to it too. Enormous, yellowish, with a blurred edge, it crept up from behind the eastern hills through a haze of dust heated by the day and never settled by evening - and its light lay on the roofs of the capital not as silver, as in good years, but as dull copper, like the glow of coals in a cooling hearth.

I awaited its rising at the window of my chambers, lighting no lamps - and when its edge at last tore free from the line of the hills, I took off everything that might betray the Pharaoh in me: the broad collar with lapis lazuli pendants, the golden bracelets, the headdress with the uraeus. I put on a plain linen dress, such as junior scribes wear, and threw over my shoulders a dark cloak of coarse wool which I had found in the chest of one of the servants.

Alone, Khemu had written. I knew that I was acting recklessly. I knew that Senusret, had he learned of my design, would have lain across the doors and not let me take a single step. But Khemu was risking far more than I - and if she asked me to come alone, it meant she had reasons which I had no right to dispute without hearing.

* * *

To get out of the palace proved easier than I expected - and this, I confess, alarmed me more than it pleased me. The guard at the small gate leading to the service courtyards was dozing, leaning on his spear, and did not even raise his head when I passed, trying to step as servants step who are sent in the middle of the night on urgent business. A second, at the exit to the city, called out to me:

"Who goes?"

"A scribe of the House of Life," I answered, not stopping and not turning my head. "Sent for a healer to a sick man."

"To which sick man?"

"To the one who will die while you ask questions."

The guard grunted - whether approvingly or irritably - and waved a hand. Already some paces off, I thought that tomorrow I should certainly tell Senusret how easily a stranger in a dark cloak can leave the palace of the Pharaoh in the middle of the night. And at once imagined how Senusret would ask how I knew.

The city slept - if one can call sleep that heavy, restless torpor into which a capital sinks in a year of famine. On the flat roofs, where in hot nights whole families slept, dark figures tossed and sighed. Somewhere a child wept - long, monotonously, with that special weeping in which there is no longer any hope that anyone will come. The dogs, which usually raised a bark at every passer-by, saw me off with only a lazy glance, too weakened to spend strength on alarm.

By the wall of one of the houses, right on the bare earth, three children slept, pressed against one another - the eldest, about ten, hugging the two younger, covering them with the hem of his own shirt. I stopped. My hand reached of itself for my belt, where a Pharaoh's purse usually hangs - and found only the coarse cloth of a scribe's dress. I had brought nothing. No gold, no bread. I stood over these children - a Pharaoh, a living god on earth, lord of the Two Lands - and could not give them even a piece of flat bread.

The eldest boy half opened his eyes and looked at me - without fear, without request, simply looked, as one looks at a shadow passing by.

"In the morning," I said to him quietly, not knowing why I said it, "bread will be given out at the palace gates. Come early with your brothers."

The boy answered nothing and closed his eyes again. I did not know whether he believed me. I myself did not know whether I should be able to keep this promise - but, leaving, I firmly resolved that tomorrow bread would be given out at the small gate, even if I had to carry it there with my own hands.

* * *

The temple lands began right beyond the market square - a high wall of mud brick, behind which rose the dark masses of the pylons. By day pilgrims crowded here, sellers of amulets, sellers of incense, but now the square was empty, only the wind chased across it dry palm leaves and scraps of papyrus left over from the day's trade.

The main gates of the Temple were locked, but Khemu evidently knew this - and I, as had been arranged in her brief message, went round the wall on the eastern side, to where gardens and service buildings of the temple servants adjoined it. There, in the thick shade of sycamores, I found a narrow wicket gate, almost indistinguishable in the dark. Beside it, on an upturned basket, sat an old man.

I froze. The old man raised his head - and I saw that his eyes were covered with a whitish film such as befalls those who have lived too long a life under the pitiless sun of the Valley.

"Someone has come," he said into the void, not raising his voice. "Someone in expensive sandals, though dressed, judging by the rustle, in cheap linen."

I involuntarily looked at my feet. The sandals. I had changed everything but the sandals - soft, of finely worked leather, with gilded straps, such as no scribe in the Two Lands wears.

"Do not be afraid," the blind man went on, tilting his head slightly, as though listening to my silence. "Old Meru asks no names. Meru has guarded this wicket forty floods, and so many secrets have passed through it that if he remembered them his head would long since have burst like an overripe melon. You are awaited at the pond. Go along the path by the wall, without turning aside. And step softer - in expensive sandals one walks loudly."

"How do you know that I am awaited?" I asked.

The blind man grinned with his toothless mouth.

"The girl has been sitting there since sunset. Sits and does not breathe - so afraid is she. Whom else would she await but one who comes in the night in another's dress and in his own sandals?"

I had already stepped toward the wicket when the blind man suddenly thrust across the passage his staff - dry, polished to a shine by his palms.

"Wait, master. Old Meru has a custom. He who passes through this wicket at night pays. Not in copper - copper is no use to me, I cannot see it. With a riddle."

"A riddle?"

"Guess it - you pass. Do not guess - you pass too," he giggled, "but Meru will know that you are a fool, and for fools it is dangerous to walk in the Temple at night. Listen."

He threw back his head, as though listening to something in the sky, and spoke in a chant, as old storytellers speak in market squares:

"'I am born each morning in flame. Each noon I am king over all kings. Each evening I die, and the belly of the heavenly cow swallows me. Twelve gates I pass in darkness, and behind each a serpent awaits me. And in the morning I am born again - and no one weeps that I died yesterday.' Who am I?"

I answered without thinking - as though the answer had lain on my tongue long before the question:

"Ra. The sun. The barque of the sun, which at night sails through the Duat, twelve hours, twelve gates, past Apep."

Meru froze. His toothless mouth slowly stretched into a smile - but in it there was no longer the former merriment.

"You answered not as a scribe, master," he said quietly. "A scribe would say: 'the sun.' But you named the gates and the serpent by name. Thus answer those to whom this riddle has been told from the cradle. Those who are themselves a son of Ra." He took the staff from the passage. "But old Meru has heard nothing. Old Meru is deaf as a stump. Go."

I passed by him, and already behind my back came a quiet, almost tender:

"And may the gods protect you, master. They, it is said, have no time for us nowadays. But perhaps for you they will make an exception."

* * *

The path along the wall led through a garden plunged in darkness. On the right stretched low buildings - storerooms, bakeries where by day bread was baked for sacrifices; they still smelled of cooled ash and leaven. On the left - the wall, and on the wall, on its very crest, something living showed black.

I stopped. On the crest of the wall, sharply outlined against the copper moon, sat a baboon. Huge, grey, with a mane falling to his shoulders like a priest's wig - he sat motionless, his paws on his knees, and gazed at the moon so intently as though conducting a silent conversation with it. On his neck a copper collar glinted.

A sacred baboon of Thoth. I had heard that several were kept in the Temple - they were fed better than many people, and at dawn, when the sun comes out from behind the hills, they raise their hands to it and cry out, greeting it, and the priests say that baboons are the first of all creatures to learn of the birth of light.

The baboon slowly turned his head and looked at me. In the moonlight his eyes gleamed - small, intelligent, human. He did not cry out. Did not bare his teeth. He looked at me from above long, very long - and then suddenly raised both paws, opening his palms, exactly as, they say, baboons raise them toward the rising sun.

But the sun was not rising. It was night. And he was raising his paws - to me.

I stood, unable to stir. The baboon lowered his paws, turned to the moon as if nothing had happened - and paid me no further attention.

The sacred pond of the Temple lay in the depth of the garden, surrounded by a stone parapet and old sycamores whose roots, thick as snakes, went under the facing slabs in search of water. In good years this pond was filled by underground springs to the very brim, and the priests performed their morning ablution in it before entering the sanctuary. Now the water stood scarcely a cubit from the bottom - dark, motionless, with a few drooping leaves of lotuses whose flowers had closed for the night and looked more like clenched fists. In it the copper moon was reflected, breaking into small shards where ripples from the night wind ran across the surface.

Somewhere in the reeds at the far edge frogs croaked discordantly, as if with effort. On a sycamore branch, right above the water, an ibis sat motionless - white, with a black head and a long curved beak - the sacred bird of the god of wisdom, and it seemed to me for a moment that it looked at me with the same silent question with which blind Meru had looked.

Khemu sat on the bottom step leading to the water, hugging her knees, so small in this huge, dark garden that I did not at once make her out in the shadow. Hearing steps, she leapt up so sharply that she nearly slipped into the water - and, recognising me, fell to her knees.

"Rise," I said in an undertone. "There is no Pharaoh here. Here is a scribe come for a healer - so I told the guard at the gates."

She rose, but did not lift her head.

"I did not believe you would come, my..." She faltered. "I do not know what to call you here."

"Call me as you wish. But tell me everything. You said that you saw columns."

Khemu at last raised her eyes to me - huge, dark, rimmed, in the custom of priestesses, with black paint, which had smeared from tears and now made her face like a mask of grief. She was quite young - I would not have given her more than sixteen floods - but in that look was something very old. So look people who have seen too much of what ought not to be seen.

"I was given to the Temple when I was eight years old," she began, and her voice, at first trembling, gradually steadied, as though by speaking she had at last allowed herself to cease to be afraid. "My mother was a potter's widow. She had nothing to feed me with, and in the Temple, they said, all are fed who serve the gods. I washed floors in the sanctuaries, carried water for ablutions, cleaned lamps. And then... then the dreams began."

"What dreams?"

"At first - trifles. I dreamed that the senior priestess would drop a jug - and next day she dropped it. I dreamed that a caravan with incense from Punt would come - and it came. I told no one. I thought it was merely coincidence. But once I dreamed that the mistress of the junior priestesses, old Tiyi, would die. I saw her dead - in her own bed, with open mouth. I woke in tears and told another girl. And three days later Tiyi died. Exactly as I had seen."

She fell silent. The frogs in the reeds hushed for a moment, as if listening too.

"The girl told the elders," Khemu went on more quietly. "And I was brought to the High Priest."

"To Nefer-Ka."

"Yes. He looked at me for a long time. Then he asked what else I dreamed. I told him. He ordered me to come to him every morning and tell him my dreams - all, to the last one. And forbade me to tell them to anyone else. Since then I became a junior priestess. I was taught letters, taught hymns, given good clothes and good food. And in return every morning I came to the High Priest and told him all that I had seen in the night."

I felt something go cold inside me. Nefer-Ka possessed a seeress. Nefer-Ka every morning learned what others were not given to know. How much of his famous, frightening foresight - how much of those occasions when he seemed to know in advance what I would say or do - how much of it was not wisdom but this girl's dreams?

"And did you tell him everything?" I asked.

Khemu lowered her gaze.

"Everything. Until last moon."

"What happened last moon?"

She was silent a long time, clasping and unclasping her fingers. The ibis on its branch shifted from foot to foot, and the faint rustle of its feathers sounded unexpectedly loud in the silence of the garden.

"I dreamed of columns," she said at last. "Enormous. So enormous that their summits were lost in darkness. A stone forest in which there is no sky. Between the columns people with torches walked - priests, many priests. And in the midst of them walked you, my..." - she faltered again - "you. You walked in the double crown, and your face was calm, but I knew - in dreams one knows such things - that you were afraid. The priests came closer, ever tighter. And then one of them raised a hand. The shadow of that hand fell on a column - long, broken. And it became dark. Quite dark. So dark that I woke with a cry, and my cell-mates decided a scorpion had stung me."

The cold beneath my ribs on the left became so sharp that I involuntarily pressed my palm to that place.

"Did you see the face of the one who raised his hand?"

"No." She shook her head. "He stood with his back to the torches. I saw only a silhouette. But I saw something else." She raised her eyes to me, and in them was real terror. "I saw what you had in your hand. You held in your palm two stones. Black and white. Smooth as river pebbles, only perfect, as though polished for hundreds of years. And when it became dark, they... glowed. Very faintly. Like embers beneath ash."

I slowly opened my palm, still pressed to my ribs, and looked at it. An empty palm.

"I have no such stones, Khemu. I never had."

"I know," she whispered. "Not yet."

"What do you mean - not yet?"

She moved closer and spoke so quietly that I had to bend to her to make out the words.

"In the treasury of the Temple, in the very depth, where only the initiated of the highest degree are admitted, there is a casket of black wood. I saw it once - I was sent to carry a lamp there when the senior keeper broke his leg. The casket was open. In it lay two stones. Black and white. Those very ones. I knew them at once - though my dream came afterward, much later. The old priestesses whisper that these stones belonged to the First Ones - those who descended from the Heavens. That they are the only thing left of them on earth. That he who possesses them..." She broke off.

"Finish."

"That he who possesses them can do what no one can. But what precisely - none of those I have spoken with knows. Even old Merit, who has served in the Temple longer than anyone, says only: 'This the High Priest knows. And no one else.'"

"Khemu," I said. "Have you ever heard what the old priestesses call these stones? Do they have a name?"

She nodded, not at once.

"Merit calls them the Two Eyes."

"The Eyes of Horus?"

"Yes, my... The Eyes of Horus." Khemu hugged her knees. "You know the legend, do you not? How Horus fought with Set for the throne of his father, for the throne of Osiris. Eighty years they fought. And in one of the battles Set tore out Horus's left eye and tore it into six parts, and scattered them all over the earth so that no one could ever gather them. But the right eye remained."

"And Thoth gathered the left," I said. "Piece by piece. And healed it. And returned it to Horus."

"Yes. The right eye of Horus is the sun. It burns by day, and is always whole. But the left is the moon. It is now full, now waning, now vanishes altogether - because it is that very eye which was once torn, and then gathered in parts. Every month the moon dies and is gathered anew." Khemu raised her eyes to the copper moon above the pond. "Merit says: the white stone is the Eye-Sun. It leads. And the black is the Eye-Moon. The eye which has already once been broken. It keeps. Because he who has been broken and gathered knows best how to keep."

One keeps, the other leads. The words passed through me with a chill, though I did not know why. As though I had already heard them. Or would hear again.

"And besides," Khemu continued, lowering her voice, "Merit tells another legend. A terrible one. It is told only to junior priestesses, so that they do not climb where they are forbidden."

"Tell it."

She looked around, as though the night itself might overhear, and began - in a chant, as tales are told that are passed from mouth to mouth:

"'There lived once a prince, the son of a Pharaoh, wise and learned, and there was no scroll in all the Two Lands which he had not read. And once an old priest said to him: "You read scrolls that are worth nothing. But there is a book which Thoth himself wrote with his own hand. Read in it the first spell - and you will hear what the birds in the sky are saying and the fish in the deep and the beasts in the mountains. Read the second - and you will see Ra in the sky with his gods, and the moon in her rising, and the stars in their forms, - even if you are dead and lying in a tomb." And the prince asked: "Where is this book?" And the priest said: "In the middle of the river at Coptos. In an iron casket. In the iron one - a bronze. In the bronze - a casket of sycamore. In the casket of sycamore - a casket of ivory and ebony. In it - a silver one. In the silver - a golden. And in the golden - the book. And around the caskets for three days' journey swarm snakes, scorpions and all manner of vermin, and around the casket itself is coiled a serpent which cannot be killed."'"

"And the prince went for the book," I said.

"He went. And killed the serpent. The serpent came alive. He killed it again. The serpent came alive again. And then he cut it in two and strewed sand between the halves so that they should not grow together. And took the book. And read. And heard the birds, and saw the stars." Khemu paused. "And then his wife and son died. Drowned in that same river. And then he himself. Because a book which a god has written does not belong to mortals. It can be taken. But for it one cannot pay."

"And what relation has that to the stones?"

Khemu looked at the water for a long time.

"Merit says," she said at last, almost soundlessly, "that the book was afterward returned. Put back, in the golden casket, in the silver, in the black, in the sycamore, in the bronze, in the iron. All but one. The casket of ivory and ebony was never returned to the river. It was carried off to the Temple." She turned to me. "The casket that stands in the treasury is of black wood, bound at the corners. I saw it. And when I held the lamp over it, it seemed to me that along the edge of the lid ivory was inlaid. A very thin strip. Yellowed."

"You think the stones are part of that book?"

"I do not know what I think," she whispered. "Merit says - a fairy tale. Everyone says - a fairy tale. But why then did the High Priest three times in one moon take the Most Ancient Epic from the Library? The keeper Amenemope told Iti, and Iti - me. Three times. What was he looking for there?"

I did not answer. I remembered Nefer-Ka's voice in the throne room: "Not yet. But I am patient."

I straightened and looked long at the water. The copper moon trembled in it, breaking into shards and gathering itself whole again.

The First Ones, who descended from the Heavens. The Most Ancient Epic, access to which only two in the whole Empire had - the Pharaoh and the High Priest. Stones of which the Pharaoh knew nothing, and the High Priest, it appears, knew all.

"Why did you stop telling him your dreams?" I asked. "This very dream."

Khemu did not answer for a long time.

"Because when I woke," she said at last, "I understood that if I told him about the columns, he would not be surprised. I do not know how I know this. But I know. He would not be surprised, because he already knows this dream. Because it is not a dream but... a design. And I grew afraid. For the first time in eight years in the Temple I was afraid not of what I might see, but of whom I might tell."

* * *

We both heard it at once: the rustle of steps on the path leading from the main sanctuary, and the uneven gleam of a torch on the trunks of the sycamores.

Khemu turned so white that it was visible even in the moonlight.

"Here," she breathed and pulled me by the sleeve into the reeds at the far edge of the pond, where the frogs instantly fell silent, sensing strangers.

We crouched in the thick growth, knee-deep in warm water smelling of mud. The sharp edges of the reeds scratched face and hands. Khemu pressed against me, trembling in every limb, and I felt how wildly her heart pounded.

The steps drew near. Onto the path by the pond came three. Two - temple guards with short spears, the third - in a light robe, with a torch in his hand. The light fell on a smoothly shaven head that flashed like polished stone.

Bakenren.

He stopped at the parapet, looked about - unhurriedly, as though making an ordinary night round - and made a sign to the guards. They withdrew a dozen paces and stood with their backs to the pond.

For a time nothing happened. Bakenren stood looking at the water, and the torch in his hand crackled, dropping sparks onto the slabs. Then from the opposite side of the garden, from the darkness where the light did not reach, a sound came - quiet, melodious, barely perceptible.

The jingle of bracelets.

I froze. Khemu, feeling my body tense, clutched my arm.

"You are late," said Bakenren into the darkness, not turning his head.

"To get out of the barracks unnoticed is not so simple as from the palace," answered a voice from the shadow. Young, a little lazy, with that special, word-stretching manner that belongs to noble youths of the Delta. I did not see the face - the speaker had stopped behind the trunk of a sycamore, outside the circle of light. "Especially now, when that old dog Senusret has posted his men everywhere."

"Senusret is no concern of yours," said Bakenren. "Your concern is that everything be ready for the festival."

"It will be ready. Two detachments of those that the Pharaoh left in the capital will go to guard the procession. Their commanders... understand from where this year the bread will come."

"Good." Bakenren paused. "The High Priest is pleased with you. Your father need not worry about his debt to the temple of Ptah. For now."

"For now," repeated the voice from the shadow, and in it for the first time sounded something like bitterness. "Always this 'for now.'"

"Such is the price of the 'third way,'" said Bakenren, and I was ready to swear that at these words he smiled. "Go. And henceforth do not jingle your trinkets when you come to a secret meeting. You can be heard from the pylon itself."

Again a melodious jingle - now receding, hurried. Bakenren stood a little longer by the parapet, looking at the copper moon in the black water, then called the guards and unhurriedly set off back along the path. The glow of the torch flickered on the sycamore trunks for some time, then vanished altogether.

We sat in the reeds, not stirring, until the frogs began to croak again.

* * *

"You heard," Khemu whispered when we at last got out onto the steps, wet to the knees, scratched by the reeds. "You heard, my... It was..."

"I heard," I said.

Khaemuas. Senusret had been right - and proved right far more quickly than we both had reckoned. The jingle of bracelets, the drawling manner, the father indebted to the temple of Ptah, the "third way" of which he had spoken at the council, glancing at Bakenren. All fitted. But it was not that which frightened me. Other words frightened me.

Everything must be ready for the festival. Two detachments to guard the procession.

"Khemu," I asked, "which festival is the nearest?"

She looked at me as though I had asked what colour the sky was.

"Opet, my... The festival of Opet. In two moons. When the statue of the god is borne along the river from the main sanctuary to the southern one. To Luxor."

To Luxor.

The ibis on the sycamore branch suddenly tore from its place and, heavily flapping its wings, flew away, south, toward the copper moon - and its shadow for a moment slid over the water of the pond, long and broken, like the shadow of a raised hand on a stone column.

* * *

"I shall take you away from here," I said. "Tonight. In the palace you will be safe."

Khemu shook her head - slowly, but firmly.

"No, my... If I vanish, he will understand. He will understand that very day. He will learn that I have stopped telling him my dreams, and understand why. But while I am here, while every morning I come to him and tell of jugs, caravans and sick old women, he thinks that I am still his. I am more needed by you here. Within."

"It is too dangerous."

"More dangerous than columns?" She tried to smile, and this smile on her tear-stained, paint-smeared face was the bravest thing I had seen in all the time of my reign. "I shall be careful. But I need someone through whom I can pass you word. Not through the guard, not through servants - there the Temple has ears everywhere."

"Iti," I said. "The youth from the Library. The one who brought me your note."

"Iti," she repeated, and her face for the first time all night softened. "Yes. Iti can be trusted. We have known each other since I came to the Temple. He used to bring the junior priestesses scrolls for study and secretly slip dates into the baskets - for all, but for me, for some reason, more often." She paused and added, looking not at me but at the water: "He is devoted to you, my... More than you think. And, perhaps, more than he himself understands."

I found nothing to answer. Somewhere in the depth of the garden the frogs began to sing again.

"Go," I said at last. "Return to your cell before you are missed. And Khemu..."

She turned.

"Thank you. For being afraid in time."

She bowed - low, in the temple fashion - and dissolved into the shadow of the sycamores so swiftly and soundlessly as though she had never been. Only wet footprints on the stone steps darkened for some time in the moonlight before the hot night wind dried them.

* * *

I was about to leave when from the shadow of the sycamores, from the side where Khemu had vanished, came a soft tapping - as if someone were walking leaning on a staff. I darted behind a thick trunk.

Onto the step by the pond came an old woman. Small, bent, in the white robe of a priestess, with a shaven, wrinkled head on which only a thin golden circlet held. Her face was so furrowed with wrinkles that it seemed moulded of dried silt. She leaned on a staff topped with a small cat's head - the staff of Bastet.

She stood by the water. Then slowly drew air in through her nose - once, twice - like a dog picking up a trail.

"It smells of a stranger," she said aloud, to no one in particular. Her voice was dry and rustling, like old papyrus. "It smells of expensive sandals, wet with sacred water." She paused. "And of lapis lazuli. Lapis lazuli such as is worn only in one house."

I froze behind the trunk of the sycamore, barely breathing. The lapis scarab - it still hung round my neck. I had forgotten to take it off.

The old woman turned - not to where I hid, but to the pond, to the copper moon in the water.

"The moon is copper tonight," she said in a chant. "Old people say: a copper moon - means that a son of Ra walks without guard. Old people say many things. Merit is old. Merit remembers nothing now." She tapped her staff on the step. "But if Merit remembered anything, she would tell the son of Ra this: the girl is brave. But the brave in this Temple are eaten first. Let the son of Ra look after his little bird. And let him not forget: the Eye which was once broken knows who broke it."

She stood a little longer, then heavily turned and, tapping her cat-headed staff, went off into the darkness - to the same place where Khemu had gone.

I came out from behind the sycamore only when the knock of the staff had died away completely. My hands trembled. She knew. She knew who I was - and did not betray me. More than that - she warned.

The Eye which was once broken knows who broke it.

I took the lapis scarab from my neck and clenched it in my fist so that the sharp edges bit into my palm.

* * *

Blind Meru still sat at the wicket on his upturned basket.

"You return," he said, not turning his head. "And your feet are wet. So you hid in the reeds. So there was someone else at the pond."

"You know much for a man who does not remember secrets," I said.

"I do not remember," Meru agreed. "I simply hear. To hear and to remember are different things, master. To remember is dangerous. But to hear... one has to hear when nothing else is left."

I stopped beside him.

"If you remembered, Meru," I asked quietly, "would you recall who has been going through this wicket in recent moons? Besides the girl?"

The blind man was silent for a long time, chewing with his toothless mouth.

"If I remembered," he said at last, "I would recall that one man goes here every seven nights. Jingles like a rattle on a goat's neck. And smells of expensive incense from the Delta. But I do not remember, master. Old Meru remembers nothing."

"Of course," I said. "Thank you for not remembering."

I opened my fist - the little lapis scarab on a plain thread, the only thing left on me of the Pharaoh and which I had forgotten to take off along with the other ornaments, still lay in my palm - and laid it in the old man's dry, wrinkled palm.

Meru felt the scarab with his fingertips, and his face for a moment changed - became serious, almost solemn.

"Lapis lazuli," he said. "Such is worn only in one house in the whole capital." He closed his palm. "Go, master. And may the gods protect you, since you so generously protect old men."

* * *

Senusret awaited me at the small gate of the palace - not on guard, not among the guards, but alone, leaning against the wall, arms folded on his chest. The guard who had been dozing here at the beginning of the night was not at his post. In his place stood two others - alert, spears at the ready, and both carefully looking aside.

"My Pharaoh," said Senusret in place of greeting, and his voice was so even that it frightened more than any shout. "The guard who let you pass will be flogged. The guard who did not notice you leaving will be sent to the southern outposts to Djehuty. And I myself, with your permission, shall until morning think of what old Paser would say to me."

"Paser would say," I answered, "that if the Pharaoh is summoned to see an omen, let him take Senusret and as many spearmen as possible."

"Paser is wise," said Senusret. "A pity that his counsels are heard but not obeyed."

I came closer to him, so that the guards could not make out a word.

"Do not flog the guard," I said. "He was deceived by a scribe hurrying for a healer. Anyone would have been deceived. And now listen to me carefully, Senusret. You were right about Khaemuas."

Senusret's face did not change, only his eyes narrowed.

"Proof?"

"My own ears. He meets Bakenren at the temple pond every seven nights. The Temple pays his father's debts to the temple of Ptah. And they are preparing something for the Opet festival. Two detachments of those of the capital will go to guard the procession - and their commanders, as Khaemuas said, understand from where this year the bread will come."

Senusret was silent a long time. The copper moon stood high now, and in its light his scar-scored face seemed hewn of the same stone as the palace walls.

"The Opet festival," he said at last. "The procession goes to Luxor."

"To Luxor," I confirmed.

"And you, of course, must lead it. The Pharaoh cannot fail to lead Opet. That would be an insult to the gods - and the people would never forgive it, especially now."

"Of course," I said.

We stood by the wall, looking at one another, and both, it seemed, thought of the same thing: that a trap laid with such patience cannot simply be avoided - one can only walk into it with open eyes, knowing in advance where the door will slam.

"Two moons," said Senusret. "We have two moons."

"We have two moons," I repeated. "And a seeress inside the Temple. And a young keeper of the Library. And old Paser with his legends. And you."

"That is not much, my Pharaoh."

"Not much," I agreed. "But it is more than there was yesterday."

Senusret for the first time all night permitted himself a smile - brief, only at the corners of his lips.

"Go to sleep, my Pharaoh. And next time, if you wish to stroll by moonlight, take at least me. I know how to walk quietly. And I, unlike you, have simple sandals."

I looked at my feet - at the soft, finely worked leather with gilded straps, wet from the water of the sacred pond - and, to my own surprise, laughed.

And when, already in my chambers, I at last lay down on my couch and closed my eyes, before me for a long time stood one and the same picture: the shadow of an ibis sliding over black water, long and broken - and two stones, black and white, lying in a casket of black wood somewhere in the very depth of the Temple, in the darkness to which I as yet had no access.

As yet.
