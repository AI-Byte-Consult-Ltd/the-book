Chapter XXII
The Rose Garden

The General took us away to his estate on the third day after the conversation in the study.

"Air," he said to my aunt at breakfast in a tone that admitted no objection. "The boy needs air. Not corridors, not the library, not railings. A garden. Roses. Horses. A week. Kostya will go with him."

My aunt was silent for a long time, looking into her cup. Then she raised her eyes to the General, and something passed between them - quick, wordless, like a signal between two sentries on different walls of one fortress.

"A week," she said at last. "And, Pavel Nikolaevich..." She faltered. "Let Stefan be near him."

The General slowly nodded. And I, sitting between them, felt as though I had just been agreed upon, in a language I did not know.

* * *

The General's estate lay forty versts from my aunt's castle, on a gentle hill above a river, and it began not with a gate but with a smell. We had not yet seen the house - only a birch avenue running uphill, and a dusty road, and the coachman Tikhon was still humming his endless song about the driver - when the wind changed, and into the carriage, through the opened windows, a fragrance poured so thick, so sweet, so unimaginable that Konstantin, who had been dozing all the way, opened his eyes and sat up straight.

"Mon Dieu," he said. "What is that?"

"Roses," I said.

"Roses don't smell like that, mon cher. Roses smell like Auntie's powder box. And this..." He drew in the air. "This smells as if someone had spilled a whole barrel of honey on the road and then set it on fire."

Tikhon on the box chuckled.

"Those are Bulgarian ones, young masters," he said over his shoulder. "The General's. Three thousand bushes here. A Bulgarian gardener looks after them like little children. Every day he talks to each bush. I've heard it myself. Talks and sings songs." Tikhon lowered his voice. "And at night, they say, he carries a lantern round the garden. In circles. The village women say he's a sorcerer. And I think - he's just homesick. For his own country."

The avenue ended, and beyond it the house opened to view - white, two-storeyed, with columns, with a broad terrace descending to the garden. And the garden...

I was not prepared for that garden.

It began right behind the terrace and went down to the river, in terraces, ledges, tiers - like an amphitheatre, like a frozen waterfall of flowers. Roses. Thousands of roses. White as snow on a mountain peak. Scarlet as blood on snow. Pink of every shade - from pale, almost transparent, like the morning dawn, to thick, dark as wine. Yellow. Tea-coloured. Cream. They twined up trellises, arches, the walls of arbours; stood in bushes as tall as a man; spread over the ground like a carpet. Between them ran paths strewn with white sand, little channels of water murmured, stone benches stood, wound with ivy.

And in the very middle, on a round lawn surrounded by a living labyrinth of clipped bushes, stood a snow-white arbour. And it was entwined from the ground to the very dome by a single rose bush - huge, ancient, gnarled, with flowers of such a deep, dark scarlet that from a distance they seemed almost black.

Under this bush, on his knees, with his back to us, in a canvas shirt, stood a man. And sang.

* * *

I did not understand the words. The language was like ours - and not like it: familiar roots, endings turned inside out, soft, singing, as if they were pronounced with a smile. The melody was slow, sad, with long drawn-out notes which now rose, now fell, like a mountain road.

The man sang, tying up a vine. Not turning round.

"Stefan," said the General. Quietly.

The singing broke off. The man slowly rose from his knees, brushed the earth from his palms and turned round.

I had seen him before - fleetingly, in my aunt's house, in the doorway of the drawing room, with a basket of white roses which he had dropped when Sventsitsky said "two worlds." I had seen him in the garden with a lantern, circling under my window. But never so close. And never by daylight.

He was fifty, perhaps more. Short, stocky, broad-shouldered, with heavy, knotted hands so densely scored by thorns that the scars on them formed a white net. His face dark, weathered, with thick black brows grown together at the bridge of the nose, and with a moustache, also black, streaked with grey, hanging to the chin. His hair short, grey at the temples. And his eyes...

His eyes were light. Unexpectedly light in that dark face. Grey, almost transparent, like water in a mountain stream.

And they looked at me.

As they had then, in the drawing room. Not in surprise. Not in fright. With an expression I had already once seen and could not forget: as though he were looking at a grave he had long sought and had at last found.

"Dobar den," he said. Quietly. To me. Not to the General.

And bowed - low, in the peasant way, pressing his hand to his chest. One bows like that not to guests. One bows like that to icons.

* * *

The General took Konstantin into the house - to show him the stables and a new English mare - and told me to "stroll about the garden, breathe." I was left alone.

No - not alone. Stefan walked after me. Ten paces behind. In silence. When I stopped at a bush he stopped too. When I walked on he walked on. He said nothing. He simply walked. As if on guard.

I endured it for some ten minutes. Then I turned round.

"Do you understand Russian?" I asked.

Stefan hesitated. Then nodded.

"Malko," he said. "A little. I speak - badly. I understand - well."

"Why are you following me?"

Stefan looked at me a long time with his light, almost transparent eyes. Then slowly, carefully choosing words, as if taking them one by one from a tight pocket, he said:

"The General said - watch. The mistress said - watch." He paused. "And I myself... I watch anyway. For a long time. Before them."

"A long time - how long?"

Stefan did not answer. He turned and went to the arbour. I followed him.

At the arbour he stopped and laid his palm on the trunk of the huge, ancient bush that entwined it. The trunk was as thick as an arm, knotted, covered with thorns, grey bark, lichen.

"This rose," said Stefan. "Do you know how old it is?"

"No."

"I brought it from the monastery. Near Tarnovo. A cutting. Small - like this." He showed two fingers. "Twenty-two years ago. The General was visiting us. In Tarnovo. I was young. I worked in the monastery garden. The General saw the roses and said: 'Will you come with me to Russia?' I said: 'I will. If I may take one rose.'" He stroked the trunk. "I took this one."

"Why this one?"

Stefan fell silent again. For a long time. Over the arbour bees droned, heavy, golden, drunk with nectar.

"Because there is a tale about it," he said at last. "At the monastery. An old one. My grandmother told it to me. And her grandmother to her."

"Tell it."

Stefan looked at me. Then at the house, at the terrace where no one was. Then at me again. And sat down on the stone bench by the arbour, patting the place beside him with his palm.

"Sit," he said. "A long tale. My Russian is short."

* * *

He told it slowly, mixing Russian words with Bulgarian, sometimes falling silent to find a word, sometimes showing with his hands what he could not find words for. But I understood. Strange - I understood nearly everything, even the Bulgarian words, as though they had been familiar to me once, long ago, and then forgotten, as the language of childhood is forgotten.

"Long ago," began Stefan. "Very long ago. When Tarnovo was still the capital. The tsar's city. When on Tsarevets - that is a hill, a fortress, big, above the river Yantra - stood the tsar's palace and the patriarch's cathedral. Before the Turks." He paused. "The Turks came. Took Tarnovo. Burned it. The Patriarch - Euthymius - they led away into exile. The kingdom ended. Five hundred years ago."

He raised his hand and pointed somewhere to the south, beyond the river, beyond the horizon.

"In the year when the Turks took Tsarevets," he continued, "a man came to the monastery. Young. A stranger. Not a Bulgarian, not a Greek, not a Turk. No one knew from where. He came at night, when the city was burning. All in dust. Without a name." Stefan looked at me. "He said to the monks: 'I do not remember who I am. I remember only that I must plant a rose here.' And took a cutting from his bosom. And planted it. By the monastery wall."

"And what happened next?"

"The monks took him in. He lived with them. A year. Worked in the garden. Did not say who he was. Did not remember." Stefan paused. "But he knew everything about roses. Everything. As if he had grown them all his life. And also - he knew languages. All of them. Spoke with Greeks - in Greek. With Turks - in Turkish. With merchants from Venice - in Venetian. And at night, in sleep, he spoke a language that no one knew."

Cold under the ribs. On the left.

"And after a year," said Stefan, "the rose bloomed. For the first time. Red. Dark. Almost black. Like this one." He touched the trunk. "And on the night it bloomed, the man left. No one saw how. In the morning - he was gone. Only by the rose, on the ground - two stones. Small. One white, one black."

I stopped breathing.

"And to the monk who was closest to him, before leaving he said..." Stefan closed his eyes, remembering. Then pronounced slowly, in Bulgarian, in a singsong, like a prayer: "'Kogato taya roza tsafne v chuzhda zemya, shte se varna.'" He opened his eyes. "'When this rose blooms in a foreign land - I shall return.'"

* * *

We were silent for a long time. Bees droned over the arbour. Somewhere by the river an oriole cried.

"And the stones?" I asked at last. My voice was alien, hoarse. "What became of the stones?"

"The monks kept them. For a long time. Two hundred years. Three hundred." Stefan shrugged. "Then - I don't know. Some say - the Turks stole them. Some say - a Russian officer took them, under Catherine, when the Russians first crossed the Danube. Some say - they went away themselves. Stones, they say, do not like to lie long in one place."

"And the rose?"

"The rose lived. Five hundred years." Stefan stroked the trunk again. "It grew old. But it bloomed. Every year. And every year the monks took cuttings and planted them in other places. In other monasteries. Near Tarnovo, at Preobrazhenski, at Kilifarevo, at Arbanasi. They said: let it grow everywhere. So that he will find it. Wherever he returns."

"And you brought a cutting here."

"I brought it here." Stefan looked at me. "Twenty-two years ago. I thought it a tale. Grandmother's tale. Just a beautiful rose." He paused. "Twenty-two years it grew here. Every year it bloomed in June. As it should."

"And?"

Stefan slowly rose from the bench. He went round to the other side of the arbour - to where the trunk of the rose went into the ground - and drew aside a leafy shoot. Under it, right at the root, was a young sprout. Thin, green, fresh. And on it - one bud. Opened. Dark scarlet, almost black.

"It is not June now," said Stefan quietly. "Now it is the end of summer. The roses have finished. All of them. In the whole garden. Only this one..." He touched the bud with the tips of his fingers, carefully, as one touches a sleeping child. "This one bloomed two weeks ago. At night. By itself. I came in the morning - and it was blooming."

"Two weeks ago," I repeated.

"Two weeks ago," said Stefan, "you were found by the railings. In the left wing." He looked at me with his light, almost transparent eyes. "I am not a clairvoyant. I am a gardener. But I can count."

* * *

From the terrace came Konstantin's voice:

"Mon cher! Where are you? The General orders everyone to dinner! There's duck with apples, and if you're late, I'll eat your portion and won't repent!"

Stefan instantly stepped away from me, lowered the leafy shoot, covering the sprout. His face became again impenetrable, peasant-like, silent.

"Go," he said. "Later. Later I will tell more."

"Stefan..."

"Later," he repeated. And, after a pause, added quite softly, in Bulgarian, as though not to me but to the rose: "Dobre doshal."

I did not know those words. But I understood them. "Welcome." Or - "welcome back."

* * *

Dinner was long and noisy - the General, contrary to his habit, was talkative, Konstantin made everyone laugh with tales of regimental pranks, and besides ourselves there were two more guests at table whom I had not expected.

The first was Father Serafim - not a Russian priest but a Bulgarian monk who had come from Tarnovo itself. Tall, thin, with a long grey beard, in a black cassock faded by the sun to the colour of dust, and with eyes lively, quick, mocking, not at all monkish. He had been travelling in Russia for half a year collecting money for a Bulgarian school at the monastery - the General, as it turned out, was his chief benefactor.

The second was a young man of about twenty, a Bulgarian, a student - Lyuben - thin, pale, in a shabby student's frock coat, with burning eyes and hair so long that the General, seeing him, winced and muttered something about nihilists. Lyuben studied in Odessa, and had come here "to visit a compatriot" - that is, Stefan. He spoke Russian almost without accent, fast, hotly, waving his hands, and kept knocking over his glass.

"Father Serafim," said the General, pouring the monk wine, "tell the young people how things are with you there now. About the Exarchate."

The monk sighed, stroked his beard.

"The Exarchate, Your Excellency, is our victory and our misfortune," he said. His voice was low, singsong, with a soft Bulgarian accent. "For forty years we fought to serve God in our own language. Greek bishops served in our churches in Greek, our books they burned, our schools they closed. Forty years! And now the Sultan has signed a firman - and we have our own church. Our own exarch. Our own books." He paused. "And the Patriarch of Constantinople has declared us schismatics. And now the Greeks curse us, the Turks laugh at us, and we..." He spread his hands. "We pray in our own language. For now."

"For now," repeated Lyuben, and his eyes flashed. "Precisely - for now, Father! A church is good. But a church is not freedom. While the Turk sits in Tarnovo, while the Turk sits in Plovdiv, in Sofia, while our girls..."

"Lyuben," said Stefan quietly. He sat at the end of the table, hardly eating and not having spoken a word all through dinner. "Not at the table."

Lyuben broke off, reddened, bent over his plate.

The General gave him a long, heavy look.

"Young man," he said slowly, "I have served the Sovereign for thirty years. I have been in Constantinople more often than you in your own bath. I know what is going on in the Balkans. I know that in Bucharest sits your committee, and that from there men cross the Danube with weapons and leaflets. And I know that two years ago in Sofia a man died on the gallows whom your people call the Apostle." Lyuben flinched. The General continued evenly: "I do not ask you to tell me anything. I ask one thing: do not bring into my house what may cost the lives of people who serve here. Stefan has been my gardener for twenty-two years. If anything happens to him because of your leaflets - I will find you. Wherever you are. Do you understand me?"

It became very quiet in the dining room. Lyuben went pale to blueness. Father Serafim lowered his eyes.

And Stefan... Stefan suddenly raised his head and looked at the General - straight, without any deference, as an equal at an equal. And said in Bulgarian something short and quiet.

The General held that look. Then slowly nodded.

"What did he say?" Konstantin asked the monk in a whisper.

Father Serafim was silent.

"He said: 'Pavel, for twenty-two years I have guarded your roses. For twenty-two years you have guarded me. Do not fear for me. Fear for the boy.'"

Konstantin looked at me. I looked at my plate.

* * *

After dinner, to clear the air, Father Serafim offered to tell "something merry." Merry, in the Bulgarian way, turned out to be a tale about Cunning Peter - a village rogue who cheated everyone: the Turkish pasha, and the greedy priest, and the rich chorbadzhi, and even the devil himself.

"Once," the monk told it, and his eyes laughed, "the pasha sent for Cunning Peter and said: 'They say you can lie so that everyone believes. Lie to me - and I will give you a horse. And if I do not believe - I will cut off your head.' Cunning Peter scratched the back of his head and said: 'Effendi, I would gladly lie. But I have no bag for lies with me. It stayed at home. Give me a horse - I will ride for the bag.' The pasha gave him a horse. Cunning Peter rode off." The monk made a pause. "And never came back. The pasha waited a week. Then understood. And laughed so that he fell off the divan. Said: 'Now that is a lie! The best! He lied to me - and I believed - and was left without a horse!'"

Konstantin laughed till he cried. The General smiled into his moustache. Even Lyuben smiled.

"And do you have riddles?" Konstantin asked when he had finished laughing. "Bulgarian ones?"

"Gatanki?" Father Serafim brightened. "We have! Many! Here is an old one. It is set to children when they are taught letters. 'Byalo pole, cherno seme. Koyto go see - toy go znae.'"

"'A white field, a black seed,'" translated Lyuben. "'Who sows it - knows it.'"

Konstantin pondered. The General smiled, clearly knowing the answer. I looked at the monk.

A white field. A black seed. A white stone and a black. A white sheet of paper and black signs on it.

"A letter," I said. "White paper, black ink. Whoever wrote it knows what is in it."

"Bravo!" Father Serafim clapped his hands. "Yes, a letter!"

"But another may read it too," objected Konstantin. "If he knows the letters."

"If he knows the letters," the monk agreed. And suddenly stopped smiling. "And if the letter is written in letters that no one knows except the one who wrote it?" He looked at me. "Then only the one who sowed knows what will come up."

I felt that in the inner pocket of my coat it suddenly became heavier - though there was nothing there but the blue notebook.

* * *

In the evening, when the heat had subsided and long blue shadows floated over the garden, Stefan called me to the greenhouse.

Konstantin went with me - the General had said "not a step," and Konstantin took the order with such seriousness that he followed me, it seemed, even to the privy. Stefan looked at him, hesitated - and nodded: let him.

The greenhouse stood at the far end of the garden, beyond the pond - a long glass building on a brick foundation, misted from within, smelling of damp earth, rotting leaves and something spicy, tropical. Inside, in the warmth, grew plants such as I had never seen: palms in tubs, lemon trees, some fleshy cacti, lianas with enormous leaves, and - in the farthest corner - a small, neat altar built of stones, on which stood a clay lamp and lay a dried wreath.

"Martenitsa," said Stefan, pointing to the wreath woven of red and white woollen threads. "Baba Marta. The first of March. We tie red-white threads on the wrist so that spring will come healthy. Later, when we see a stork or a flowering tree, we take it off and hang it on a branch." He touched the wreath. "Here are no storks. I hang it here."

"Red and white," said Konstantin.

"Red and white," nodded Stefan. "Blood and snow. Life and death. One without the other - impossible."

He went to a shelf by the wall, took from it a tin box - old, dented, with a peeling picture on which roses had once been drawn - and opened it.

Inside, on faded red velvet, lay a small glass vial - thin, narrow, with a ground stopper sealed with wax. In it a thick, almost transparent liquid glowed golden.

"Rose oil," said Stefan. "From Kazanlak. From the Valley of Roses. There - between two mountains, the Balkan and the Sredna Gora - roses grow. Fields. Versts. They are gathered at dawn, while the dew is on. By women. By hand. Every flower." He raised the vial to the light. "To make this much oil - three thousand roses are needed. Three thousand. This vial is my whole family. Mother gathered. Sister gathered. I gathered when I was a boy."

"Why are you showing it to me?" I asked.

Stefan was silent a long time. Then he put the vial back in the box and took out from under the velvet something else. Small, thin, folded in four.

Paper. Very old, yellowed, almost rotted at the folds.

He unfolded it - slowly, carefully, as one unfolds the wings of a dead butterfly. On the paper was a drawing. Faded, but still distinguishable: a dark scarlet rose. And beneath it - two small circles. One filled in. The other empty. And between them a wavy line.

And still lower - a line of writing. Not Cyrillic. Not Greek letters. Long, flowing, curving lines running one into another without breaks.

Konstantin beside me drew breath noisily.

"This is from the monastery," said Stefan quietly. "This was drawn by that man. Five hundred years ago. The monks kept it together with the stones. The stones went - the paper stayed. When I was leaving for Russia, the abbot gave it to me. Said: 'Take it with the rose. The rose will find to whom to give it.'"

He held out the paper to me.

"The rose has found," he said.

* * *

I did not take it. I stood and looked at the paper in his outstretched hand, at the flowing lines, at the two circles and the wavy stroke between them - and could not move. I did not know these signs. I was sure I had never seen them. And yet, looking at them, I felt what I had felt when Sventsitsky said "two worlds" - as though a key were entering a lock I had never seen but which had all this time been within me.

"I cannot," I said. "It is not mine."

"It is yours," said Stefan. Simply. As one speaks of the weather.

"How do you know?"

Stefan looked at me a long time. Then he laid the paper on the table, beside the box. Came up to me. Very close. And took my right hand in his - heavy, calloused, scored by thorns.

Turned it palm upward.

"That man," he said, "my grandmother said: on his palm there was no line of life. Smooth. Like a stone the water has rolled." He ran his thumb over my palm. "Twenty-two years I have looked at hands. The General's, the gentry's, the peasants'. All hands are different. All with lines." He raised his eyes to me. "Yours is the first without."

I snatched my hand away. Looked at it myself. At my own palm.

I had never looked at it so. Never. It had never occurred to me.

Smooth. Where on Konstantin - I had seen his hands a thousand times - a long curved line ran from the wrist to the fingers, on me there was nothing. Smooth skin. Like a child's. Or like a stone.

Konstantin looked at my palm. Then slowly raised his own - and looked at it. Then again at mine.

"Mon cher," he said very quietly. "I... I never noticed."

"Neither did I," I said.

* * *

We came out of the greenhouse when it was already quite dark. The paper I did take after all - Stefan put it into my hand and closed my fingers around it so firmly that refusal was impossible. Now it lay in the blue notebook, between pages, beside the drawing of two circles that I had not drawn, and the words "He is near. Wait," which I had not written.

At the pond Lyuben caught up with us - out of breath, with a stack of papers under his frock coat which he was carefully hiding.

"Stefan!" he whispered. "Bay Stefan, I need..." And, seeing us, broke off.

"Go into the greenhouse," said Stefan. "It is warm there. Hide what you carry under the martenitsa. No one will touch it." He looked at Konstantin and me. "They saw nothing."

"We saw nothing," said Konstantin gravely. And, when Lyuben had run off, added, looking at Stefan: "The General knows, though. About the greenhouse."

"The General knows," nodded Stefan. "The General knows everything. The General pretends not to know. With you Russians that is called - friendship."

"And with you?"

Stefan was silent.

"With us it is called - roses," he said. "Some people grow them, others smell them. And no one asks who watered."

* * *

At night I did not sleep. I stood at the window of my room on the second floor of the General's house and looked at the garden. The moon was almost full, and the garden lay silver under it, quiet, motionless - only the roses smelled so that one's head spun.

And in the garden below, a light was moving. A lantern. Slowly, in a circle. Round the arbour. Round the ancient, gnarled bush on which a single dark scarlet, almost black bud bloomed.

Stefan was walking round his rose. Circle after circle. Like a sentry. Like a monk round an altar.

Someone knocked softly at my door. I opened. Konstantin - in a dressing gown, with tousled hair, with a candle in his hand.

"You're not sleeping," he said. Not a question.

"I'm not sleeping."

He came in, stood beside me at the window. We silently watched the light in the garden.

"Mon cher," said Konstantin after a long time. "May I look again? At your hand?"

I held out my hand. Palm up.

He took it. Brought the candle near. Looked for a long time. Then ran his thumb over the smooth skin - slowly, from the wrist to the fingers, where on all people in the world the life line runs.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing at all."

He did not let go of my hand. He held it in his, warm, and looked out the window at the light.

"You know," he said at last, very softly, "Strauch says that the lines on the palm are just folds of skin. That they mean nothing. That it is superstition." He paused. "I believe him. I want to believe him. But if you have no life line..." His voice wavered. "Mon cher, then I don't know how to hold you. Do you understand? If there is no line - what is there to hold on to?"

I did not know what to answer. I looked at Stefan's light in the garden, circle after circle, round the black rose that had bloomed out of season.

"Hold on to the hand," I said at last. "As long as you hold it - I am here."

Konstantin looked at me. Then at our hands. Then let go - slowly, as if reluctantly, as if his fingers would not obey. And turned to the window so that I would not see his face.

"Good night, mon cher," he said, already from the door. In an almost ordinary voice. Almost.

The door closed. The candle went away down the corridor.

And in the garden below, Stefan's light stopped by the arbour. And stood for a long time motionless. Then it rose - higher, higher - as though the gardener had lifted the lantern above his head, toward the rose itself, toward the single dark bud.

And it seemed to me - this was, of course, a trick of the moonlight - that the bud, in answer to the light, opened faintly. By one more petal.
