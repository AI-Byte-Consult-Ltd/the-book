Chapter IV
Oriental Sweets

Smiling - as best I could, because my hands for some reason still trembled slightly, and something vague and prickly had not settled in my chest, to which I could not have given a name or a cause - I held out my hand to the old men for a handshake.

The old men on the carpet stirred, half rose, and shook my hand with that stately unhurriedness that marks people in no rush in this life, and said something in reply, accompanying the words with a brief bow and a touch of the palm to the chest. The young men behind them looked at me with undisguised curiosity - as one looks at a rare bird that has strayed by mistake into someone else's garden.

There were three of them. One, about fourteen, slender, supple, with a mop of curly hair and eyes in which imps danced, all but jumped up and down with impatience. The second, the youngest, about twelve, quiet, with a pensive, almost girlish face, held a piece of charcoal in his hands and, it seemed, had been drawing something right on the flagstones of the yard before I came. And the third - a broad-shouldered, sullen lad of about seventeen, with the first dark down above his upper lip - looked at me as one looks at a man who is about to steal something.

It became finally clear: we spoke different languages. The discovery did not exactly surprise me - after this morning I seemed to have unlearned the art of being surprised by such trifles - but it made me take from my pocket once more the scrap of paper with the incomprehensible inscription, the one thread I still had left to hold on to.

I held the sheet out to the one of the old men who seemed to me the most welcoming - the one whose smile wrinkled his whole face, right up to the eyes. Grandfather took the paper carefully, with both hands, unfolded it, smoothed it with fingers gnarled by age and sun, read it - and, after a moment's silence longer than mere reading required, turned to the curly-haired youth and whispered something in his ear.

The youth leapt up so swiftly that I barely managed to follow his course with my eyes: across the yard, to the house, up the wall - there was no other way to describe it, because he literally vaulted into the second floor through an open window, not troubling to look for a door. From the house at once came a clatter of crockery, someone's exclamations, the hurried patter of bare feet on a wooden floor - and a sleepy, indignant voice, clearly woken against its will.

We were left five - I, two old men and the two remaining youths - and all that time I was studied with the same mixture of kindliness and avid curiosity with which one studies a rarity at a fair. The sullen lad kept repeating some phrase - judging by the intonation, a question, and an unfriendly one - but, I confess, I let a good half of his efforts pass by my ears, watching spellbound the acrobatic feats of the boy who had run off.

"I do not understand a word of what you are saying," I said with all possible politeness, perfectly aware that my politeness was now running into a blank wall of incomprehension, "just as I do not understand what is written on this paper."

The sullen lad heard my speech, frowned still more and said something to the old men - sharply, defiantly, pointing at me with his chin. The second old man, lean, with a stern face that seemed hewn from stone, answered him with a single short word, and the lad, flushing, fell silent, though he went on boring into me with his eyes.

A moment later, like a jack-in-the-box, the curly-haired one flew back out of the house with a large tray in his hands, nearly missing his footing on the top step of the porch, for which he at once received from the elder grandfather a short but expressive rebuke. He set the tray down in the centre of our improvised table - the carpet itself - and we all, including me, sat down around it on cushions, while the young men settled a little way off, as befitted the unwritten but, evidently, very strict etiquette of that house.

The tray proved a work of art in itself - old metal darkened with time, with a chased pattern, plainly handed down in that family for more than one generation. On it stood three thin, almost transparent glass tumblers, an elegant silver teapot of artful carving with Indian elephants along the whole spout, and several plates, at the sight of which my stomach, having had nothing since morning but two rings of bread and a couple of figs, contracted with redoubled force.

On the first plate lay sweets such as I had never tasted before - or perhaps was seeing for the first time, though the sense of recognition that had pursued me all morning stirred for an instant even here, as soon as I glanced at those small rolls dusted with powdered sugar in every imaginable colour: red, yellow, lilac, with nuts and without. The second plate overflowed with sliced fruit - melon, watermelon, something resembling an apple, peaches, cherries, apricots, it seemed, and something else to which I could not have given a name even if my life depended on it. The third, smaller, was filled with almonds, cashews and walnuts.

The elder to whom I had first addressed myself filled my tumbler with his very first movement - and the air at once filled with the thick, tart aroma of black tea, so rich that I involuntarily closed my eyes, inhaling it as though it might help me remember something, anything, of my former, lost life. Then he filled the tumblers for himself and for the second grandfather and with a gesture - brief, but full of unfeigned cordiality - invited me to begin.

I took one of the rolls - pink, flecked with green pistachios - and bit into it. It proved soft, chewy, faintly springy, and in my mouth spread the taste of rose, honey and something floral, as though I had bitten not into a sweet but into the very garden in which it had grown. I must have betrayed my amazement too openly, because the younger of the youths burst out laughing and shouted something, pointing a finger at me, while the elder grandfather, smiling into his beard, pushed the whole plate toward me.

"Lokum," he said, tapping the edge of the plate with a finger. "Lokum."

"Lokum," I repeated with my mouth full, and both old men laughed.

For some time we passed in the silence of tea-drinking, broken only by the clinking of tumblers and the distant sounds of street life: somewhere a pedlar was crying his wares, somewhere a dog barked, somewhere around the corner a ship's whistle moaned again, long and mournful.

* * *

He appeared on the porch - barefoot, in a hastily pulled-on shirt, his face crumpled from sleep and the imprint of a pillow on his cheek, yawning so wide that he seemed able to swallow the whole yard. Twenty or so, perhaps a little more, slender, swarthy, with short black hair sticking out in all directions from sleep, and lively, quick eyes which, the instant they rested on me, woke completely.

The curly-haired brother who had brought the tray darted out from behind him and began to chatter something, pointing now at me, now at the note in grandfather's hands. The newly woken one listened, ruffled his hair, came down the steps, bowed to the old men and, sitting down closer to me, uttered a phrase which I, to my own amazement, at last understood, though with a distinct, sing-song Eastern accent:

"Forgive my appearance. I was loading ships in the port all night and had only just lain down when my brother burst in through the window and said that grandfather needed a man who understands a foreigner." He smiled, and that smile instantly erased from his face the traces of a sleepless night. "My name is Ferhat. The one you addressed is my grandfather, his name is Suleyman. The other elder is Yusuf, my uncle. This rascal who races up walls is my brother Akmal. The one with the charcoal is Kemal, the youngest. And this grumpy one" - he nodded at the sullen lad - "is our cousin Orhan. Pay no attention, he looks at everyone like that. Even cats. I work in the port, so I understand your language a little - sailors and merchants from all over the world speak all languages at once there, one has to learn at least a bit - but I speak it, I am afraid, not as well as I would like. What is your name? Where have you come from and where are you headed?"

I almost laughed with relief - for the first time in that long, tangled morning I had a chance to explain my absurd situation to someone - and at once spoiled the whole impression by honestly admitting that I could answer none of the three questions properly.

"I do not remember," I said. "Neither how I came to be in this city, nor where I was going, nor from where. I do not even remember my own name. And the funniest thing is - I do not even understand in what language I am speaking with you now."

Ferhat gave me a long, thoughtful look, then turned to his grandfather and began whispering something quick, plainly conveying my words. Suleyman glanced at me over his tumbler - not with distrust, rather with that particular attention with which one regards a curious but harmless riddle - and answered his grandson briefly and weightily.

But before Ferhat could translate, Orhan sprang from his place and began to speak - loudly, hotly, waving his arms. I did not understand the words, but I understood everything else: in his every gesture, in every glance he cast at me, one thing could be read - I do not believe it.

Ferhat answered him - at first calmly, then more sharply. Orhan would not be stilled. Kemal, sitting with his charcoal, drew his head into his shoulders. Akmal, on the contrary, leaned forward, watching the squabble with obvious relish. And then Yusuf - that same old man with the stone face - said a few words quietly, without raising his voice, and Orhan broke off mid-word, as though he had run into a wall.

"Forgive me," said Ferhat, turning to me, and his face flushed slightly. "My brother says... He says that a man who does not remember his name is either ill or lying. And that there have been many strangers in the port of late who look out for what lies badly." Ferhat hesitated. "He is young and foolish. Forgive him."

"He is not foolish," I said. "In his place I should probably think the same. Tell him that I understand him. And that if I had wanted to steal something, I would hardly have begun by sitting down to tea with the owners."

Ferhat translated. Orhan listened, snorted, but in his eyes, it seemed to me, there flickered something like reluctant respect. He sat down again - a little farther off than before - and folded his arms on his chest.

"And what did Uncle Yusuf say?" I asked. "Why did he fall silent so suddenly?"

Ferhat grinned.

"Uncle said: 'In this house a guest is judged not by his name but by how he drinks tea. This one drinks well.'"

* * *

As if in confirmation of Yusuf's words, a resonant, familiar voice rang out at the gate:

"Misafir! Hoş geldin, misafir!"

I turned. In the gateway, blocking it almost entirely, stood the red-bearded shopkeeper - the same who that morning had treated me to bread and figs. In one hand he held a small bag of something fragrant, with the other he waved a greeting, and his face, from beard to brows, shone with the pleasure experienced by people who are the first to bring an amusing piece of news.

"Hasan Amca!" Akmal leapt up to meet him.

The shopkeeper came into the yard, bowed to the old men, handed Suleyman the small bag - judging by the smell, of cinnamon - and at once, without waiting for an invitation, sat down on the edge of the carpet, which creaked piteously. He began to speak - quickly, resonantly, pointing at me now and then, imitating with his hands something like grabbing and twisting a wrist. Akmal dissolved into laughter, Kemal smiled, even Yusuf barely perceptibly moved an eyebrow. Orhan listened, his face growing longer and longer.

"What is he telling?" I asked Ferhat.

Ferhat was laughing so that he could barely speak.

"Hasan Amca says that this morning you caught Mehmet by the hand. Little Mehmet, who picks pockets all along our street and whom no one has ever managed to catch - neither the guard, nor Hasan Amca himself, nor even his own grandmother." Ferhat wiped his eyes. "He says you caught him so quickly, as if you knew in advance where he would reach. And that now the whole street will know that it is best not to meddle with the foreigner in velvet boots."

Hasan finished his tale, slapped his knees and added something loudly, looking straight at Orhan. The latter turned crimson to the roots of his hair and stared at the ground.

"And what was that?" I asked.

"That," said Ferhat with evident pleasure, "was him asking Orhan whether it was not he who last spring tried to catch Mehmet three days running and in the end fell into a gutter."

Orhan muttered something, got up and, looking at no one, went into the house. Hasan saw him off with a good-natured chuckle, then turned to my note, still lying in Suleyman's hands, and, squinting, peered into it over the old man's shoulder. His brows drew together for an instant. He said something to Suleyman - under his breath, no longer laughing. The latter nodded.

"Hasan Amca traded with Persian merchants for many years," Ferhat explained. "He says the handwriting here is no common one. He says if grandfather needs help to read the rest - let him call him."

The shopkeeper rose, making the carpet groan again, gave me a mock bow - "Misafir!" - and departed, whistling, leaving behind a cloud of cinnamon and a feeling as though a small festive hurricane had just rolled across the yard.

* * *

Taking advantage of the lull, I took out of my pockets everything I had found on myself that morning: the two stones, black and white, the blank sheet of paper folded in four, the strange coins - and carefully laid the finds out on the carpet before me, as cards are laid out for a fortune-teller in the hope that someone at least would manage to read in them more than I had managed to read myself.

The first to reach for the coins was not Suleyman, but Yusuf. He took one, then another, held it up to his very eyes, rubbed it on his sleeve, weighed it in his palm, then in both palms in turn - with that unhurried concentration with which, no doubt, he had for many years weighed other men's debts and his own profits. At last he spoke - drily, briefly, as if dictating into a ledger.

"Uncle Yusuf says," Ferhat translated, "that this is not Ottoman money. Nor Persian. Nor Frankish, nor English, nor Russian, nor Greek. In his life he has held in his hands the coins of thirty countries, perhaps more. But such as these he has never seen." Ferhat paused, listening to the continuation. "He says the metal is good. The minting very fine. Too fine. They are not minted thus for trade, but..." Ferhat faltered, searching for the word. "For remembrance. Like a medal."

Yusuf laid the coins back on the carpet - in an even row, as if setting out counters on a board - and did not touch them again.

Suleyman set his tea aside and moved closer, examining the rest of my treasures with the air of a man to whom such a task was no novelty. He unfolded the blank sheet of paper, looked through it against the light, felt its density - as one tests the authenticity of a document - and returned it to the carpet, having found, it seemed, nothing remarkable but its very emptiness.

He lingered longest over the stones. He turned them in his knotted old fingers, held them up to his eyes, rubbed the smooth surface with his thumb - not with awe, not with recognition, but with the purely craftsman's, appraising interest of a man who in a long life has seen many a curious trinket brought by the sea and by wanderers from every corner of the world.

"Beautiful stones," Ferhat translated his grandfather's words. "Well polished. He does not know where from, but says - the work is fine, not natural. Perhaps amulets. Perhaps merely the toy of some child from a wealthy family."

I confess that so prosaic an explanation for some reason brought me relief - far more relief than any mysterious revelation would have brought. At least something that morning might turn out to be merely stones, and not another insoluble riddle.

Kemal, the youngest, the quiet brother with the charcoal, who had been sitting apart all this time, suddenly moved closer and, glancing shyly at his grandfather, stretched out his hand toward the white stone. Suleyman nodded. Kemal took the stone, laid it on his palm and looked at it for a long time without moving. Then with quick, sure movements he drew something with charcoal right on the flagstone of the yard - and, flushing, drew back.

I looked at the drawing. They were two circles - one black, filled in with charcoal, and one white, left empty - and between them a line. Not straight, but curved, wavy, like a river. Or like a road.

"What is this?" I asked Ferhat.

Ferhat asked his brother. Kemal, not raising his eyes, muttered something.

"He says," said Ferhat after a slight pause, "that the stones wanted him to draw it." He laughed awkwardly. "Kemal is like that. Always drawing. He says that things prompt him. Pay no attention."

I nodded. But for some reason I could not for a long while take my eyes from the two circles on the flagstone and the wavy line between them.

* * *

Last of all Suleyman took the scrap of paper with the inscription - the very one that had brought us to this conversation - unfolded it, read it, and this time lingered longer than usual, silently moving his lips as though checking himself.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat, having heard the explanation, "that an address is written here. In Istanbul. Not far from the new Sultan's Palace."

Istanbul. I repeated the word to myself, tasting it as I had lately tasted the rose sweet - and waited for it to open something in me. To unlock some little door. Nothing unlocked. The word was beautiful, round as a dome - and utterly empty for me. Like my own name, which I did not know.

"So this city is called Istanbul?" I asked.

Ferhat looked at me with such amazement as though I had asked what the sun is called.

"You do not remember even that?"

"I do not."

He translated to his grandfather. Suleyman was silent for a long time, looking at me, and in his gaze for the first time in the whole conversation appeared something new - not suspicion and not pity, but a peculiar, aged gravity.

"Grandfather says," said Ferhat quietly, "that this city has many names. Some call it Istanbul, others Constantinople, others Tsargrad, others simply the City, because there is no other like it. He says: since you do not remember a single one of its names, perhaps it is the city itself that has decided by what name to present itself to you."

Suleyman continued, and Ferhat translated, lagging slightly:

"Grandfather himself has been in those parts - long ago, in his youth, when he traded in silk. He knows the streets there. He can take you there, show you the place. But only in three days. On that day silk merchants gather there from the distant provinces, and grandfather has business there anyway - to see old acquaintances. And before then, he says, there is no hurry. If the note has waited for you so long, it will wait three days more."

"In three days," I repeated, trying to get used to the thought that my investigation of my own identity was being postponed by a full three days.

"You can stay with us all that time," Ferhat went on, with that simple, everyday generosity behind which stood not a drop of calculation or doubt. "There are rooms enough in the house. No one will trouble you. You will have your own room, with access to the library and a small balcony. You look rather tired - as though you had come from afar, and you do not even know where you are going next. Rest will certainly not hurt you."

I meant to object - whether from politeness or from a vague, inexplicable feeling that I ought not to abuse another's hospitality - but I had no time to utter a word, because Ferhat, evidently considering the question already settled, went on to set out the arrangements in a businesslike way:

"As for me, I shall be at work these three days - I leave early, come back late, so we shall hardly see each other. But everyone in the house will be glad to help you, even if you do not understand each other in words. My mother and grandmother, by the custom of our country, you will not see - but they will cook, and I hope you will like our Ottoman cuisine. My younger brother Akmal can show you the neighbourhood, if you wish."

At these words Akmal - the very one who had run for the tray - broke into a smile so sincere and broad that I at once wanted to agree to this excursion, without even knowing where it would lead me.

"And if you prefer to stay at home," Ferhat added with a sly twinkle in his eyes, "grandfather will gladly teach you to play backgammon. Only mind: he plays not for amusement but in earnest, and shows no mercy to novices."

"One difficulty," I said. "How will your family address me if I have no name?"

Ferhat translated. The old men exchanged glances. Yusuf shrugged - as if to say, not my affair. And Suleyman, after thinking, smiled that same smile which wrinkled his face to the eyes, and uttered one word.

"Misafir," said Ferhat. "Grandfather says: until you remember your name, you will be Misafir to us. It means 'guest.' He says it is a good name. Better than many real ones."

"Misafir," I repeated. And, to my own surprise, I felt it grow a little warmer in my chest from that simple word.

* * *

Suleyman, hearing, it seemed, that the conversation had turned to backgammon, gave a short, pleased chuckle and rapped a knuckle on the wooden board lying right there at the edge of the carpet. Then, without waiting for my consent, he opened it, tipped out the counters and began to set them up - white on his side, dark on mine.

"Right now?" I asked Ferhat.

"Grandfather says: the best way to know a man is to play with him. Words lie. Dice - never."

Suleyman held out the dice to me. I took them - two small bone cubes, yellowed from countless throws - and froze, not knowing what to do with them.

"Throw," Ferhat prompted.

I threw. Five and three came up. Ferhat, leaning toward me, began to explain how the counters move - but before he finished the first sentence, my hand had already taken a dark counter by itself and moved it five points. Then another - three. Confidently, quickly, without thinking.

Ferhat broke off mid-word. Suleyman raised his eyes to me.

I myself looked at my hand as though it belonged to someone else. I did not know the rules of this game. I was sure that I did not know. And yet my hand knew them - just as in the morning my feet had known the way along an unfamiliar road.

Suleyman slowly, very slowly nodded and threw his dice. We played a game. I lost - utterly, hopelessly, as befits a novice - because my hand, having made the first few confident moves, suddenly seemed to forget what it knew, and after that I moved the counters at random, stumbling on every throw. But when the game ended, Suleyman did not laugh as one laughs at a defeated novice. He looked long at the board, then at me, and said something to Ferhat - quietly, almost thoughtfully.

"Grandfather says," translated Ferhat, "that the first five moves you played as old masters play. And then - like a child. He says he has never seen such a thing. As though someone else played for you first, and then left."

I did not know what to answer. I caught myself smiling for the first time that morning - yes, perhaps, in all that unthinkable day - through my anxiety rather than despite it. Perhaps it was the tea, still hot and spicy in my hands, or perhaps the simple, undeserved warmth with which people entirely strange to me were ready to receive me - having lost my name, my memory and, it seemed, all notion of my own life - under their roof, without any questions or conditions, save perhaps a readiness to play a game or two of backgammon.

"Thank you," I said, hoping that my intonation would convey gratitude better than any words could. "I do not know how I shall repay you for this kindness, but I promise at least one thing: the next game of backgammon I shall not lose without a fight."

Ferhat burst out laughing - he understood my joke, it seemed, without difficulty, his port practice had not been in vain - and his laughter was at once taken up by his brothers, who had probably not made out a single word of what I said, but unerringly caught the very spirit of merriment. Even Orhan, looking out of a second-floor window, smirked crookedly, against his will - and at once ducked back.

"Now is the time to rest," said Ferhat at last, rising and brushing off his knees. "And I, with your permission, shall sleep out what I did not sleep. Follow my brother, he will show you your room."

Akmal leapt up with the same swiftness with which he had earlier raced for the tray and waved to me, inviting me to follow. I rose, feeling the weariness of that day - a weariness not so much of the body as of the spirit, too long trying to contain too much that was inexplicable all at once - settle on my shoulders with a new, almost tangible weight.

Already at the very door of the house I looked back. Suleyman and Yusuf had returned to their game as though nothing had happened, rapping the counters on the board with the same unhurried concentration with which they had done so, no doubt, for more than one decade. Kemal bent over his drawing on the flagstone, finishing something with charcoal. The world around them - the narrow lane, the motley coverlets on the ropes, the distant steamer's whistle - seemed suddenly so simple, so clear in its everydayness, that for a moment I envied those people with the whitest envy of which a man was capable who did not even remember whether he had ever known the feeling before.

Perhaps, I thought, climbing after Akmal up the steep wooden staircase smelling of wax and old wood, the greatest luxury in the world is to know for certain who you are and where your home is. A luxury of which I had, it seemed, been deprived - for today, at least - but which, looking at these people who had received me with such ease and generosity, I suddenly longed desperately, painfully, to regain.

And meanwhile - meanwhile I had at least a name. Misafir. Guest.

For a start, I thought, that was not so little.
