Chapter XXXVI
The Farewell

The ten days before departure passed as, probably, the last days before a wedding or a funeral pass, in a bustle that exists so that one need not think of anything.

The house hummed. Dunyasha from morning till night washed, ironed, starched, folded, refolded, folded again, my shirts, my frock coats, my neckcloths, and every time she folded another cravat she sighed as though laying it in a coffin. Mitka lugged trunks down from the attic, blew the dust off them, hunted for keys that no one could find, and told the kitchen that the master was going to the Turks, and the Turks, they say, have forty wives each and all in masks. The cook, Marfa, baked. Baked with such fury as if she meant to feed the whole embassy at Constantinople a year ahead: pies with cabbage, pies with sturgeon spinal marrow, curd tarts, rusks, shortbreads, gingerbreads, and wrapped it all in linen cloths and packed it in baskets, which Ignatiy Savvich, with icy dignity, carried out to the lumber room, saying that in Odessa there was bread.

"In Odessa there is no bread," Marfa snapped. "In Odessa there are rolls. And a roll is not bread. A roll is frippery."

Auntie was silent. All ten days.

She came out to breakfast, to dinner, to supper. Poured the tea. Asked whether I had slept well. Spoke with Agafya Tikhonovna about accounts and with Ignatiy Savvich about the cellar. Received visitors, Kutasova, Shelestova, the Colonel, who came "to say goodbye" every day, because every day a new reason was found to say goodbye once more. She was as straight, as calm, as flawless as ever.

Only her hair she wore differently now. Not in a tight knot at the nape, fastened with a tortoiseshell comb, but plaited in a simple braid thrown over her shoulder. As though, having given me the key, she had given away something more, and there was no longer anything to hide.

* * *

On the third day Stefan came.

He came neither in a carriage nor on horseback, but on foot. Forty versts. From the General's estate. From dawn to dusk. I saw him from the balcony, a small, stocky, stooped figure on the avenue, in a linen shirt, in worn boots, with a knapsack on his back and with something he carried before him in both hands, carefully, as an icon is carried in a procession.

A pot.

Large, clay, heavy, bound in wet sacking. And in it, a rose. Not a cutting, no. A bush. Young, but already sturdy, an elbow high, with dark, glossy leaves and three buds, dark crimson, almost black.

I ran down to the porch. Stefan stopped on the lowest step. His face was grey with dust, sweat on his brow. But his eyes, light, almost transparent, looked at me calmly.

"Dobar vecher," he said.

"Stefan. You walked forty versts?"

"Walked," said Stefan. "A rose cannot be taken in a carriage. It shakes. The roots tear." He paused. "A rose must be carried. In the arms. Like a child."

"Why?"

Stefan gave me a long look.

"You are going to Tsargrad," he said. "The General wrote." He held out the pot to me. "Take her."

"To Constantinople? A rose?"

"From the big bush," said Stefan. "The one by the arbour. The Tarnovo one." He paused. "I layered her off three years ago. Raised her apart. I did not know why." He set the pot on the step. "Now I know."

* * *

We sat on the bench by the porch, I, Stefan and the rose between us. Dusk was falling. Over the garden the first stars were lighting. Dunyasha brought Stefan kvass and bread; he drank the whole jug at a draught, without stopping, and broke off the bread and put it in his knapsack.

"You must rest," I said. "Stay the night. Ignatiy Savvich will give you a room."

"No need of a room," said Stefan. "I will be in the garden. By the rose. As at home." He paused. Then said slowly, choosing his words, as always, as though taking them one by one from a tight pocket: "Master. I did not tell you the whole tale. Then. In the orangery."

"Not the whole?"

"Not the whole," Stefan nodded. "About that man. Who came to the monastery when Tarnovo burned. Who planted the rose. Who left, leaving two stones." He paused. "The monks wrote more afterward. In the chronicle. I saw it. As a boy. The abbot showed me."

"What does it say?"

Stefan looked at the rose for a long time.

"It says," he said, "that when that man left, he said not one thing but two. The first you know: 'When this rose blooms in a foreign land, I shall return.'" He paused. "And the second he said only to the abbot. Quietly. And the abbot wrote it down." Stefan raised his eyes to me. "'When I return, the rose will go with me. To where I came from. And there it will bloom again. For the third time.'"

"For the third time?"

"In Tarnovo the first," said Stefan. "Here the second." He nodded at the pot. "And the third, there. Where you are going." He paused. "I do not know what it means. I am a gardener. I know only that a rose must be carried in the arms. And watered. And talked to. Every day." He stroked the leaves. "She understands. She understands everything. Roses remember."

"Stefan," I said. "In Constantinople, they say, by the Patriarchate there grows a rose. Also from Tarnovo. You said so."

"It grows," said Stefan. "The monks took it. Three hundred years ago." He paused. "When you see it, set this one beside it. Let them talk. They have not seen each other for a long time."

* * *

He lived in the garden three days. Slept on the ground by the arbour, wrapped in his coat. By day he dug, pruned, tied up Auntie's roses, which, he said, "had gone quite wild without a master." He spoke to hardly anyone. With me too.

But on the third night, when I went out to the garden, unable to sleep, he was sitting by the arbour in lantern light, writing something. In pencil. In a small notebook, on his knee. Slowly. Carefully. With the tip of his tongue out, as children write.

He saw me. He did not hide the notebook.

"A letter," he said. "To Lyuben. In Bucharest." He paused. "You are travelling through Odessa?"

"Through Odessa."

"In Odessa," said Stefan, "there is a Bulgarian house. On Preobrazhenskaya. Our people live there. Merchants. Teachers. Students." He tore a leaf from the notebook, folded it in four. "Pass it on. To whoever opens. Say, from Stefan of Tarnovo. They will understand."

I took the leaf. It was heavier than a sheet of paper should be.

"What is in it?"

Stefan looked at me.

"You do not want to know, master," he said. "The General knows. The General permitted." He paused. "There will soon be war. Not now. In a year. Perhaps two. The Russians will cross the Danube. To the Balkans. To Tarnovo." His voice trembled. "Five hundred years we have waited. Five hundred." He clenched his fist. "In this letter: where the Turkish detachments stand near Tarnovo. How many. Where the stores are. Where the fords across the Yantra are." He paused. "My brothers found out. At risk. Passed it to me. Through Lyuben. I pass it on."

"Why to me?"

"Because you are a guest," said Stefan simply. "A guest nobody searches. A guest everybody feeds." He smiled, for the first time since I had known him, broadly, truly. "So we say. And the Turks too."

* * *

On the sixth day Vera Anatolievna came alone.

Without the Colonel, who, by her account, lay with gout cursing the fate that did not let him go with the General. Without a companion. Without a maid. She came into the small drawing room, where I sat over a map of the Black Sea, quiet, in grey, with a dragonfly brooch on her collar, with a beaded reticule, and sat down opposite me without removing her gloves.

"I promised," she said. "Remember? To give you the drawing. When you would know what to do with it."

"I do not know what to do with it, Vera Anatolievna."

"I know," she said. "But you are going to where it is drawn." She opened the reticule. "I have thought. Ten nights. And decided: let it be with you. There. Perhaps there it will be of more use to you than to me here."

She took out a watercolour wrapped in tissue paper. The same one: a young man in a red turban in a narrow lane by the sea, with a scrap of paper in his hand, with my face. Laid it on the table.

And then took out a second sheet.

"This I never showed you," she said quietly. "I showed it to no one. Fifteen years. Not even to Mishenka."

She unfolded it. Also a watercolour. The same lane. The same houses, stone below, wooden above. The same washing on the lines. And the same young man in a red turban, standing in the same place, in the same pose, only the face was different. Not lost. Empty. Looking through.

"This is a week later," said Vera Anatolievna. "The second time. When he did not recognise me." She paused. "I drew quickly. Five minutes. Then he left. And at home, in the evening, by lamplight, I looked at the drawing, and saw."

"What?"

She stretched out a gloved finger and pointed. Behind the young man's back, by the wall of the house, in shadow, barely visible, scarcely sketched, like a chance stroke of diluted grey paint, there was something. Tall. A head taller than a man. Faceless. Motionless.

"I did not draw this," said Vera Anatolievna. Her voice was level, but I saw her hand trembling in its glove. "I swear to you. I drew the wall. The washing. Him. I did not draw anyone behind him." She paused. "But it is there. On the paper. In my paint. With my brush."

We looked at the grey smudge by the wall.

"For fifteen years I have been telling myself it is a smudge," said Vera Anatolievna. "A chance stroke. The brush trembled." She raised her eyes to me. "Take both. Please. I no longer need to tell myself it is a smudge."

* * *

On the seventh day there was a thunderstorm. On the eighth, Kutasova with Lizonka.

Nadezhda Porfirievna came in first, in lilac, with a lorgnette, with the face of a general leading troops to the assault, and announced from the threshold that they were here "literally for a minute, to say goodbye to the lieutenant." Lizonka followed, pale, red-eyed, in a white dress, clutching something small in her hand.

Konstantin, seeing them from the window, tried to escape through the back door. The General intercepted him in the corridor, I saw it, took him by the shoulder, turned him round and pushed him toward the drawing room with the words: "Lieutenant, you are an adjutant. An adjutant does not flee from the enemy. An adjutant accepts battle."

The battle lasted half an hour. Kutasova talked. Of the weather. Of the Turks. Of how dangerous the sea is. Of her cousin in Odessa, who would be happy to receive the lieutenant. Of how beautifully Lizonka embroiders: "Lizonka, show him!"

Lizonka, red to the roots of her hair, stepped toward Konstantin and held out what she had in her hand.

A handkerchief. White, batiste, with lace at the edge. And in the corner, embroidery. Small, careful, unskilled but earnest: a rose. Dark red. And beneath it a letter. "K."

"So that you... so that you do not forget," Lizonka whispered. "There. In Turkey. That here... that here someone..." She faltered and did not finish.

Konstantin stood with the handkerchief in his hand. He had a face I had never seen on him: bewildered, embarrassed, almost frightened. He looked at the handkerchief. Then at Lizonka. Then, for some reason, at me, standing in the doorway.

"I thank you, Elizaveta Andreevna," he said at last. Quietly. Very seriously. "I shall not forget." A pause. "I promise I shall carry it. And that I shall return it to you. When I return."

Lizonka flushed and ran from the drawing room. Kutasova after her, glancing back triumphantly at Auntie.

When they had gone, Konstantin sat a long time on the windowsill in my room, turning the handkerchief with the rose in his fingers.

"Mon cher," he said at last. "I am a fool."

"Why?"

"Because I promised to return it," said Konstantin. "And now she will wait. And I do not know whether I shall return." He looked at me. "If you do not return, neither shall I. Understand? I could not return without you. And she will wait."

"Kostya..."

"Never mind," said Konstantin. Put the handkerchief in the pocket of his tunic, the inner one, on the left. Where one keeps what is dear. "Never mind, mon cher. We shall return. Both." He jumped down from the sill. "And if not, let Lizonka marry Tryaskov. He will write her verses. On unrequited love. He's good at that."

* * *

On the ninth day Agafya Tikhonovna came to me herself.

At night. With a candle. With the bunch of keys at her belt, which this time did not jingle, she held them down with her hand.

"Master," she said from the threshold. "Forgive me for coming at night. By day it's impossible. By day there's the mistress."

"What has happened, Agafya Tikhonovna?"

The old woman came in. Closed the door. Set the candle on the table. And took from under her apron a bundle. Black. Soft. Dusty.

Crape.

"From the portrait," she said. "I took it down. Today. Myself." She paused. "Fifty years I straightened it every morning. And this morning I came and thought: what for?" She laid the crape on the table. "The master is going away. Let the portrait look. Let it see him go."

"You took the crape off the portrait," I said slowly. "So now it can be seen. The face."

Agafya Tikhonovna was silent for a long time.

"It can, master," she said at last. "Only don't go. There's no need." She raised her faded eyes to me. "I looked today, in the daytime. For the first time in fifty years. Straight. In the light." Her voice trembled. "There's no face there. None at all. The paint has gone. As if someone scraped it off. Only the canvas is left. Empty. White."

"Empty?"

"Empty, my dear," the old woman whispered. "And fifty years ago, when I came as a girl, there was a face. I remember. I peeped under the crape in secret, when the mistress wasn't looking." She crossed herself. "There was a face. Young. Handsome. Sad." She paused. "And now there isn't."

"When did it vanish?"

Agafya Tikhonovna looked at me. Long. As she must have looked at that portrait for fifty years.

"I don't know, master," she said. "Under the crape you can't see." A pause. "But I think it did not vanish. I think it went away."

"Where to?"

The old woman did not answer. She took the candle. At the door she turned.

"And another thing, my dear. The name. From the plate. The one they scraped out." She paused. "I swore on the icon to the late mistress not to say." A pause. "But the late mistress said: do not say until he leaves." She looked at me. "And you are leaving."

I stopped breathing.

"Agafya Tikhonovna..."

"Not now, master," she said. "In the morning. On the porch. When you get into the carriage." She paused. "I shall whisper it. And you, don't ask again. I'll say it once. I shall not be able to more."

And went out, carrying the candle.

* * *

The last supper was quiet.

No charades. No toasts. No guests but the closest. Auntie. The General. Evdokia Gerasimovna, in a plain dark dress, without a hat, without a plume. Konstantin. Doctor Strauch. Vera Anatolievna; the Colonel had sent a note that the gout "would finish him, but not before he saw Tsargrad." And Stefan, on the edge of a chair, in a clean shirt, silent.

Marfa served everything she had baked in ten days. There was no room on the table. No one ate.

The General was silent for a long time. Then pushed aside his plate.

"For thirty years I have been leaving this house," he said. "On campaigns. On embassies. To wars." He paused. "Every time Artemia Aristarkhovna saw me off. And every time she said the same thing. 'Pavel Nikolaevich, come back. You have not told all yet.'" He looked at Auntie. "And I came back. Thirty years. Because I had not told all."

"You have not told all now, Pavel," said Auntie quietly.

"Not all," the General agreed. "There is one story I have told no one. Not even you." He paused. "Perhaps now?"

Auntie nodded.

* * *

"Siam," said the General. "You know I love Siam. More than any land." He paused. "Fifteen years ago I lived there two months. In a monastery. In the mountains. In the north, on the Burmese border." He looked at the candle. "There was an old monk there. The abbot. They said he was a hundred years old. He hardly spoke. He sat under a bodhi tree, the kind under which, they say, the Buddha found enlightenment, and looked at the valley. For whole days."

"What did you talk about with him?" asked Strauch.

"About God, Karl Fyodorovich," said the General. "I asked him: who created the world? I was curious. I had read the Bible. The Koran. Indian books. Chinese. Everywhere someone created it. God. Gods. Brahma. The Celestial Emperor." He paused. "And the monk laughed. Quietly. And said: 'No one.'"

"No one?" Evdokia Gerasimovna repeated.

"No one, Dunya." The General smiled at her. "He said: no one created the world. The world is a wheel. It turns. Always. Without beginning and without end. Birth, death, birth, death. Worlds appear and vanish like bubbles on water. And appear again. All is a circle." He paused. "I asked: and who turns the wheel? The monk said: 'Those who ride on it. Each by his own desire. While they desire, the wheel turns.'" The General looked at me. "I asked: can one get off? He said: 'One can. If one remembers who one was before sitting on the wheel.'"

I sat motionless. The candle on the table before me trembled.

"And then," said the General, "the monk said something to me that I did not understand for fifteen years. He said: 'You seek faces, Russian. I see it. All your life you look at faces. One day you will find the one you seek.' A pause. 'And he will tell you that it was not he who was seeking you. It was you who sought him. From the very beginning. You simply do not remember that you yourself called him into this world.'"

In the dining room it became very quiet.

"What does that mean, Pavel?" Auntie whispered.

"I do not know, Artemia," said the General. "Fifteen years I have not known." He paused. "The monk died a week after I left. They wrote to me. Said he sat under the tree and died. Smiling." The General raised his glass. "When I learn what it means, I shall return and tell you. As I promised."

* * *

"And I shall put it otherwise," Doctor Strauch said suddenly.

All turned to him. Strauch sat turning an unlit cigar in his fingers and looking at his plate.

"I am a German," he said. "I am a physician. I have read Darwin." He raised his eyes. "Mr. Darwin says that no one created the world. That everything came by itself. Stones by themselves. Stars by themselves. Life by itself. From mud, from water, from light. Millions of years. Slowly. Step by step. Ape, man. Fish, lizard, bird." He paused. "No God. No wheel. Simply time. A great deal of time. And chance."

"That is heresy, Karl Fyodorovich," said Evdokia Gerasimovna gently.

"That is science, Evdokia Gerasimovna," said Strauch. "Though sometimes it seems to me they are the same." He paused. "But I will tell you this. Mr. Darwin explains where man came from." He looked at me. "But he does not explain where came from a young man who has no life line on his palm, who speaks Egyptian in his sleep and writes lines that match a scroll from Khiva." Strauch laid the cigar on the table. "For that, Mr. Darwin has not a single chapter."

"Perhaps he will write one yet," said Konstantin.

"Perhaps," Strauch agreed. "Or perhaps someone else will write it." He looked at me again. "In a hundred years. When machines will think."

I started.

"Why did you say, machines?"

Strauch shrugged.

"I do not know," he said. "It came to mind." He paused. "Professor Sventsitsky told me that on the day you do not remember you spoke to him of a man who builds a machine. Which answers him from a glowing window." Strauch looked me in the eye. "Perhaps that is your chapter. The one Darwin lacks."

* * *

Stefan, who all this time had sat silent on the edge of his chair, suddenly raised his head.

"And among us," he said slowly, "among the Bulgarians, grannies say it so." All turned to him. Stefan blushed but went on. "God did not create the world alone. With Him was another. Without a name. God said: 'Dive to the bottom of the sea. Bring earth.' He dived. Brought a handful. God took it and made the earth. Mountains, rivers, fields." Stefan paused. "And the other hid a little earth in his mouth. For himself. To make his own world. Later. Some day."

"And did he?" asked Konstantin.

"I do not know," said Stefan. "Grannies say the earth in his mouth grew and grew. He spat it out. And there were mountains. The Balkans." He smiled. "That is why we have mountains. And you have a plain. With you God made alone."

Konstantin laughed. The General smiled into his moustache. Even Auntie's lips twitched slightly.

"And who was that other?" I asked.

Stefan looked at me. With his light, almost transparent eyes.

"Grannies do not know, master," he said. "Grannies say he still walks. On the earth. Looking for where to plant his handful." He paused. "They say he has no name. Because God did not give him one. God said: 'You will find a name for yourself. When you make your own world.'"

* * *

The morning was grey, windy, cold. Over the garden clouds ran low. Stefan's roses, tied to the trellises, swayed in the wind.

At the porch stood two carriages. In the first, the General and Konstantin. In the second, I, the trunks, Marfa's baskets and the pot with the rose, bound in wet sacking, which Stefan had set at my feet so that it would not shake.

Everyone saw us off.

Ignatiy Savvich, in full livery, straight as a pole, with a face on which not a muscle stirred. Marfa, in tears, clutching yet another basket of pies that no one had taken. Dunyasha, sobbing into her apron. Mitka, on the roof, where he could see better. The coachman Tikhon, on the box of the second carriage, in a new cap, gloomy as a cloud: "I'll get you to the station, master. And after that, the locomotive. The locomotive is not a horse. You can't talk to it."

Evdokia Gerasimovna stood by the first carriage. The General leaned toward her. Said something. She nodded. Took from her finger a ring, plain, silver, thin, and laid it in his palm. He closed his fist. And did not open it for a long time.

Vera Anatolievna stood by the steps. In grey. With the dragonfly brooch. She did not come up. She only raised a gloved hand and held it raised a long time.

Doctor Strauch stood by a column. With his unlit cigar. He came to the second carriage. Held out his hand to me.

"Twelfth," he said. "Come back. I should so like to write it in."

"I shall try, Karl Fyodorovich."

"Germans do not try," said Strauch. "Germans do." And stepped away.

Stefan stood by a rose bush at the start of the avenue. He did not come up. He was looking not at me but at the pot with the rose at my feet. His lips moved. He was speaking to her. In Bulgarian. Quietly. I could not hear the words.

And Auntie...

Auntie stood on the top step of the porch. Straight. In a dark dress. With her braid thrown over her shoulder. With a string of pearls. She did not weep. She looked at me, long, unwaveringly, as if she wished to memorise every feature, and I understood that she would not come down. That if she came down she would not be able to let me go.

I climbed up to her myself.

"Auntie."

She said nothing. She took my face in her hands, cold, ringed, and held it. A long time. Then kissed my forehead.

"Have you the key?" she asked.

"I have."

"Good." She released me. "Go."

And turned away. Before I could answer.

* * *

Agafya Tikhonovna stood at the step of the second carriage. Small, dry, in her cap, with the bunch of keys at her belt. As I climbed in she bent to me, so close that I smelled incense and wax, and whispered.

One word.

A name.

I did not ask again. I could not ask again. My voice was gone.

It was not my name. Not the one by which I was called in that house. Another. Strange. Ancient. Which I had never heard.

And which I knew.

Not with the mind. With something deeper. As one knows a melody heard before birth. As one knows the smell of a house in which one has not lived. As one knows a face never seen, but which comes in dreams.

Agafya Tikhonovna straightened. Crossed me. Stepped back.

"Off we go!" cried Tikhon.

The carriages started.

I looked back. The house. The porch. Auntie on the top step, with her back to us. The General's betrothed by the column. Vera Anatolievna with her hand raised. Strauch with his cigar. Stefan by the rose bush. Dunyasha. Marfa. Ignatiy Savvich. Mitka on the roof waving with both hands.

And the gallery window. At the end of the left wing. Tall, narrow. Where the portrait hung, without crape. Without a face.

In that window, for a single moment, as the carriage turned into the avenue, someone stood. Tall. Grey. Motionless.

And watched me go.

* * *

The train to Odessa took two days.

I had never travelled by locomotive, or did not remember having done so, and for the first hours I sat at the window like Timosha at... like a child at a confectioner's window. Fields. Forests. Villages with crooked huts. Churches with green domes. Stations, stone, wooden, with bells, with buffets, with ring-rolls and tea from a samovar, and at every station the locomotive puffed, whistled, let off steam, and passengers poured out onto the platform to stretch their legs, and crammed back into the carriages.

In our carriage, first class, blue, with velvet sofas, travelled strange people.

A Greek merchant from Taganrog, stout, moustached, in gold rings, who all the way ate olives from a jar and told Konstantin that in Constantinople he had three shops, and in each a wife. "Not the Turkish way, no!" he assured. "The Greek way! Each thinks she is the only one!"

A Polish nun, small, severe, in black, with a rosary, who was going to Odessa and from there to Jerusalem, to the Holy Sepulchre, on foot, "if God grants." She spoke to no one but me. And only once. On the second day, at a station near Kiev, when everyone had got out, she came up to me and said in Polish, quietly, and I understood: "You are like the one I saw in a dream at Czestochowa. Before the icon. He stood between two doors." And crossed me. And did not approach again.

And a blind old kobzar, bearded, grey, in a white svita, with a bandura on his back and a boy guide, who was travelling third class but at one of the stations near Vinnytsia entered our carriage; the boy had mistaken the door. The General would not allow him to be turned out. Bade him sit. Poured him tea. And asked him to play.

* * *

The kobzar sang for a long time.

Not a song, a duma. Long, slow, sorrowful, almost on a single note, with runs on the bandura like the lapping of water. I did not understand all the words, the Little Russian accent, the archaic turns, but I understood the main thing.

About captives. Cossacks taken prisoner by the Turks. Chained. Sitting on galleys, on Turkish ships where men row, shackled to the benches, day and night, for years. In the middle of the Black Sea. On the way to Tsargrad.

"Oh, hey, on the Black Sea, on a little white stone..."

They row and look north. Where home is. Where the steppe is, the Dnieper, the native cottage, mother on the threshold. They cannot return. They are chained. But they look. Every day. And pray to God, not for freedom. That at least a wind from the native side might blow in their faces. Once.

"Oh, strike, God, upon the sea, the quiet, the Black..."

The kobzar sang with his blind eyes closed. The boy guide sat at his feet and wept without a sound. The Greek merchant stopped eating olives. The Polish nun prayed. Konstantin turned to the window.

And the General sat motionless, clutching in his fist Evdokia Gerasimovna's ring.

When the duma ended, the kobzar opened his white, sightless eyes. Turned them to me. Straight at me, though no one had told him where I sat.

And said, in Little Russian, slowly:

"You, lad, row too. I sense it. Chained. Between two shores." He paused. "Only you do not look north. You look back." A pause. "And you need to look forward."

"Now you are looking back," the boy at the dervish's gate had said.

I had never seen that boy. Had never heard those words.

But they were. In me. Like the kobzar's duma.

* * *

Odessa greeted us with sun.

A white city on a high shore. Broad, straight streets lined with acacias. Houses light, stone, with balconies, with columns, with stucco, as if brought from Italy. Noise. Shouting. Laughter. Dozens of languages, Russian, Greek, Italian, Yiddish, Armenian, Turkish, Bulgarian, all mingled, at every corner. The smell of the sea. Of salt. Of fish. Of coal. Of acacia.

And the staircase.

Enormous, from the boulevard on the shore down to the port, stone, broad, endless, two hundred steps, said the General. From above, only the landings are visible. From below, only the steps. The builder's trick. A deception of the eye.

"Like life," said Konstantin, standing on the top landing and looking down at the port, at the masts, at the smoking funnels. "From above it seems all stops. From below, all steps. And in fact, both."

"Lieutenant," said the General, "you are becoming a philosopher. It is dangerous in an adjutant."

* * *

The steamer was to leave in the evening. Until the evening there was time.

The General went to the office of the Russian Company to see to papers. Konstantin with him. I was left alone. With the pot of the rose in my arms, Stefan had told me not to leave her for a minute, and with the letter in my pocket. The letter for Preobrazhenskaya. For the Bulgarian house.

I found it quickly. Two-storeyed, with green shutters, with a sign above the door, in Bulgarian, in Cyrillic, "School." I knocked. A young man opened, swarthy, black-moustached, with burning eyes, very like Lyuben whom I had seen at Stefan's.

"From Stefan of Tarnovo," I said.

The young man turned pale. Looked about. Pulled me inside. Took the letter. Unfolded it. Read. Raised eyes to me, wet.

"Do you know what is here?" he asked in Russian.

"I do."

"And you brought it."

"I brought it."

He looked at me for a long time. Then at the pot with the rose in my hands. Then again at me.

"Is this from Tarnovo?" he asked quietly. "The rose?"

"From Tarnovo."

The young Bulgarian slowly sank to his knees. Right on the floor. And kissed a leaf of the rose.

"Five hundred years," he said. "Five hundred years." He raised his face to me. "My grandmother said: when a rose from Tarnovo goes south, it means soon. It means they will come. Will free us." He laughed through tears. "You are the one who carries the rose. Do you know what that means?"

I did not know. I knew nothing.

But I nodded.

* * *

On the way back, on Deribasovskaya, noisy, crowded, with cafés, shops, cabmen, street vendors, I stopped at a shop window.

A clockmaker's workshop. Small. Below, in a half-basement, seven steps down. Behind the glass, clocks. Dozens. Hundreds. On the walls, on shelves, in the window. With pendulums. With cuckoos. Pocket watches on chains. And all were ticking.

I do not know why I went down. My feet went by themselves.

The door was upholstered in felt. Above it a copper bell. New. Shining. Without a crack.

I pushed the door. The bell jingled.

And all the clocks in the workshop, all at once, all the hundreds, stopped.

The silence was so complete that I heard my own heart beating. The pendulums stood still. The cuckoo froze in its window with its beak open.

At a table by the window sat an old clockmaker. Grey, in a skullcap, with a loupe in his eye. He raised his head. Looked at me. His face went white.

And beside him, on a stool, sat a boy. About ten. Thin, curly, in large, too large for his face, spectacles with thick lenses, behind which his eyes seemed huge, like an owl's. With tweezers in his hand. An apprentice.

The boy looked at me. Without blinking. With his huge owlish eyes.

I opened my mouth. And said, not knowing where the words came from:

"Excuse me. I am off again."

And went out.

The bell jingled. The door closed.

And behind my back, in the basement, all at once, all the hundreds, the clocks started.

I stood on Deribasovskaya among the crowd, with a pot of rose in my arms. And through the noise of the street, through the cabmen's shouts, through the laughter, through the jingle of harness bells, I heard below, in the workshop, the boy in the huge spectacles ask the old man:

"Grandfather, who was that?"

And the old man answered, quietly, hoarsely:

"I do not know, Lyovushka. Remember him. Perhaps you will meet him again."

* * *

The steamer was called the *Grand Duke Konstantin*.

"It is a sign," announced Konstantin, seeing the name on the hull. "This is my steamer. I was named after it. Or it after me. No matter." He patted the iron rail of the gangway. "It will carry us."

She was large, two-funnelled, with enormous paddle wheels on her sides, with masts on which furled sails hung, white, with a red stripe. On deck, bustle. Sailors. Porters. Passengers. Greeks, Armenians, Turks, Jews, Russians. Pilgrims, to Jerusalem, through Constantinople. Merchants. Officers. Ladies with lapdogs. Ship's boys.

I went up the gangway with the pot of the rose in my arms. The General ahead. Konstantin behind.

And on the upper deck, at the very rail, leaning on it, stood a man.

Tall. In a grey coat, cut in the European fashion. In a top hat. With a cane. He was smoking a thin cigar and looking at Odessa, the white city on the high shore, the staircase, the acacias.

He did not turn as we passed.

But Konstantin, walking behind me, suddenly stopped. Sniffed.

"Mon cher," he said softly. "Do you smell it?"

"What?"

"Bergamot," said Konstantin. "Cologne. Expensive. Parisian." He looked at the man in grey. "I have smelled him before. Today. On the stairs. When you went to the Bulgarian house and the General and I went to the office." He paused. "This man followed you. All the way to Preobrazhenskaya."

The man in grey did not turn. He finished his cigar. Threw the stub into the water. And slowly, unhurriedly, tapping his cane on the deck, went off into the first-class saloon.

"General," said Konstantin. "Who is that?"

The General looked after the man in grey for a long time. His face was stone.

"I do not know, Lieutenant," he said. "But in the steamer's passenger register his name, I saw it when I was arranging the papers, is written in letters I could not read." A pause. "Long ones. Crooked. One into another."

* * *

The steamer cast off at dusk.

Odessa receded, slowly, reluctantly, a white city on a high shore, the lights on the boulevard, the lights on the staircase, the lights in the windows. Then only the lighthouse. Then only the sea.

The Black Sea. Nocturnal. Dark as ink. With white crests of waves flaring in the glow of the side lights. With the wind fresh, salt, cold.

I stood at the rail. With the pot of the rose. The General beside me. Konstantin on the other side.

"Two days," said the General. "The day after tomorrow in the morning, the Bosphorus."

"General," I said. "That monk. In Siam. He said: 'You simply do not remember that you yourself called him into this world.'" I paused. "Called whom?"

The General looked at the dark sea for a long time.

"I thought I knew," he said at last. "Thirty years I thought so. I thought it was about God. That I was seeking God." He paused. "And now I think it is about you."

"About me?"

"For thirty years I looked at faces," said the General. "In Samarkand, in Peking, in Havana, in Sitka, in Melbourne. I thought I was seeking someone." He turned to me. "But perhaps it was not I who sought. Perhaps it was you who called me. Through all this. Through all these lands. So that I should bring you the scroll. So that I should bring you to where you must come." He paused. "Perhaps that is what the monk meant. That I called you, and you called me. And no one remembers who was first."

"A circle," I said.

"A circle," the General agreed. "It has no beginning." A pause. "So the monk said."

"And the gypsy woman at the stones," I said, not knowing where the words came from, "said: 'This one has one.'"

The General looked at me. Long. Very long.

"What gypsy woman?"

"I do not know," I said. "I have never seen a gypsy woman at stones."

* * *

At night I did not sleep.

I stood at the rail holding the pot with the rose. Konstantin slept in the cabin; I could hear his breathing through the open door. The General too. The deck was empty. Only the watch sailor on the bridge. And the wind. And the sea.

And the name.

The name that Agafya Tikhonovna had whispered to me at the step of the carriage.

I repeated it to myself. Over and over. I did not utter it aloud; I could not. I only thought it. Word by word. Sound by sound.

It was old. It was strange. It was mine.

And the longer I repeated it, the more clearly I understood that it was not the only one. Behind it stood others. As behind one reflection in a mirror, others, endless, receding into depth. Other names. In other languages. In other cities. In other centuries. An Egyptian one, short, guttural, sounding like a blow on bronze. A Turkish one, "Misafir," guest. Another, simple, modern, which I could not recall but which someone wrote to me on a glowing screen and I did not respond. And more. And more.

And in the very depth, behind them all, behind all the reflections, one. The very first. The most ancient.

Which I could not hear. Could not remember. Only sensed, as one senses warmth from a stone that is not in the hand.

The rose in the pot in my hands stirred in the wind. One of the three buds, dark crimson, almost black, slowly opened. Right here, at night, on the deck, in the middle of the Black Sea.

And one petal tore away. Flew. Down. Into the dark water.

I watched it fall. Watched it vanish in the white foam astern.

"When I return, the rose will go with me. To where I came from. And there it will bloom again. For the third time."

In the east, above the edge of the sea, the sky began to grey.

In a day, the Bosphorus.
