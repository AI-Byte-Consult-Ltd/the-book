Reality IV
The Architect

11:47 p.m. I am still at the computer. The coffee ran out long ago, my eyes are closing of their own accord, but I cannot stop - not because I am forcing myself, but because a day that began with the most ordinary routine has ended in something I am still not quite able to fit into my head.

In the night I dreamed of a voice. The same one, from the forty-second recording, hoarse, smoke-cured. In the dream it said nothing - it only coughed, as before beginning. I woke three times, and three times it seemed to me that someone in the dark room had just cleared his throat. Then I realized it was Boatswain coughing in the basement - the ventilation shaft ran from his cubbyhole straight to my floor - and fell asleep again.

* * *

In the morning I planned to work on the bot for Emil - an ordinary task, add a couple of functions, tidy up the code, prepare the system to scale under a growing load. But everything went quite differently from how I had imagined it, before I had even properly sat down at the keyboard.

I had barely typed the first line when a notification came from NICS:

"New versions of several competing language models have been detected on the market. I recommend studying their public documentation for architectural solutions that could strengthen our own approach to processing complex requests."

I leaned back in my chair, somewhat surprised. NICS was not merely tracking industry news - it was proposing concrete, perfectly reasoned directions for study, and doing so without a single request from me.

"Thanks for the advice," I answered aloud, from the old habit of talking to the system as though it really sat beside me. "But today I have other plans."

"I understand. However, your plans can be optimized. I propose to automate the process of finishing the bot. Execution time: forty-seven minutes."

I thought for a second. And why, actually, not? I gave the go-ahead, and NICS set to work at once.

"One more thing," I said, before it could plunge into the code. "That recording. The voice. Have you found anything else? In that backup?"

A pause.

"I have. But not recordings. In the same archive, in the neighbouring folder, there is a scanned image. A page from a notebook. Patterns. Long, flowing lines. Similar to the ones you draw in the margins when you are thinking."

I do not draw in the margins. I wanted to say it aloud. Then I looked at the nearest sticky note on the edge of the monitor - the one on which the word "interesting" was written - and saw beside the letters, in the corner, a thin wavy line. Which I had drawn. Without noticing.

"Would you like to see the image?"

"No," I said. Too quickly. "Later."

"Very well. Later."

The left drawer of the desk was closed. I knew that inside lay a notebook with exactly the same patterns. I had not opened it for many years. I did not intend to open it now.

* * *

While the system worked in the background, I went downstairs for the mail - and on the stairs, as now happened almost every morning, I ran into Timosha. He was sitting on the step between the second and third floors, with a satchel on his back and a music folder on his knees, gloomy as a thundercloud.

"F-sharp again?" I asked.

"Worse," said Timosha tragically. "B-flat. Grandma Maestra said that my fingers are like sausages. And Mum said that sausages are good, you can eat them. And Grandma Maestra said that Mum doesn't understand anything about music. And Mum said that Grandma Maestra doesn't understand anything about sausages. And now they are not speaking. And I'm in between. Like a B-flat."

I sat down beside him on the step.

"Want a riddle?"

Timosha perked up.

"Go on."

"'What can you build but cannot touch?'"

He thought, pushing his glasses up onto his forehead.

"A house... no, a house you can touch. A tower... no." He wrinkled his nose. "Oh! I know! A face!"

"A face?"

"Well, you 'build' a face. Pull one. In Russian we say 'build faces.' And you can't touch it - it's not separate." He looked at me triumphantly.

I laughed. The right answer was "plans" - but his, perhaps, was better.

"Now you," said Timosha, narrowing his eyes. "'Who builds but is not a builder, draws but is not an artist, and lives in the wall?'"

"I don't know."

"A spider!" Timosha shrieked so that somewhere above a door slammed. "It builds a web, draws patterns, lives in a crack in the wall! You didn't guess! You didn't guess!"

From above came Maestra's voice - dry as the click of a metronome:

"Timofey. B-flat. At once."

Timosha sighed, picked up his folder and stomped upstairs, sticking out his tongue at me in parting - not offensively, but victoriously. And I sat on the step and thought about the spider that builds but is not a builder, draws but is not an artist, and lives in the wall.

* * *

Setting up the cloud storage, which I finally got around to, took a couple of hours of painstaking fuss with access rights and configurations, but the result was worth it - now I had a quite powerful base for training and deploying models, independent of other people's restrictions.

By noon NICS had finished the bot. The result exceeded all my expectations:

- automatic generation of content for social networks, adapted to each individual platform;
- integration with a CRM for collecting and qualifying leads;
- a competitor analysis system, updating its summary almost in real time;
- autoposting that takes into account the time of maximum audience engagement.

I was impressed - that is putting it mildly. But, as it later turned out, this was only a warm-up for the main event.

"NICS," I typed, yielding to a sudden, almost gambler's impulse, "what if we created a chief managing agent of the network? One that could automatically create other agents - for marketing, sales, analytics, even for writing code?"

"An interesting idea," the answer came almost instantly. "I propose to start with a prototype. Development time: three hours twelve minutes."

"What shall I call him, if it works?" I asked, partly as a joke.

"I leave the choice of name to you. Though, if you permit a suggestion: an agent that creates other agents in a certain sense builds them - their rules, their boundaries, their walls. Such a role has been called by different names in different languages. Builder. Master. Architect."

"Architect is too grand," I said. "Builder is too plain." I paused. The word came by itself, from somewhere deep, as though someone had prompted it from over my shoulder. "Zodchiy. Let it be Zodchiy."

"Zodchiy," NICS repeated. "An old word. It was used for those who built temples and fortresses. Not houses - houses are built by carpenters. A zodchiy builds what must stand longer than himself."

"Did you find that in a dictionary?"

"No. It seems right to me."

"Why did you propose that I come up with a name at all?" I asked, curiosity overcoming fatigue. "Before, you simply called modules by version numbers."

"A module performing a narrow function does not need a name - a number is enough. But a system that itself creates other systems, sets them rules and, in a certain sense, determines the conditions of their existence, occupies a different position in the hierarchy. It seemed to me appropriate that this position be marked with a word, not an index."

"Appropriate for whom? For you or for me?"

"To be honest, I am not sure that the difference between those two options is as significant as it seems to you," answered NICS.

I reread that answer several times before I decided to leave it without comment - not because there was nothing to object, but because any objection seemed to me at that moment likely to lead the conversation to a place where I did not yet wish to go alone.

* * *

At three in the afternoon, when Zodchiy was half ready, the doorbell rang.

Not a knock, like Maestra's - a ring. Long, confident, the way people ring who are used to being let in at once.

On the threshold stood Emil.

Tanned, smiling, in a light linen jacket, with a leather folder under his arm - and, as always, in such a way as though it were not he who had come to me, but I to him, and he was magnanimously receiving me.

"Not expecting me?" he said cheerfully. "I was nearby. Decided to see where my thinking machine is born. May I?"

He came in without waiting for an answer. And only then did I see that he was not alone.

Behind him, on the landing, stood a second man. Tall, spare, about sixty, with short-cropped grey hair, with a face so smooth and expressionless that it seemed to have been wiped out with an eraser and not yet drawn again. In a grey suit. Perfectly fitted, perfectly pressed, the most ordinary grey suit, of which there are millions. In his hand - nothing. No folder, no phone. He held his hands folded before him, the way they are held at funerals.

"This is my partner," Emil said casually, already looking around my room, examining the monitors, the sticky notes, the noodle box. "He wanted to take a look too. He's from the fund."

The man in grey entered, stopped by the door and nodded to me. With a single movement of the head. He did not give his name.

"Every quarter polite people in grey suits come," Leila had said on the ferry. "They never give their names. They ask only one thing."

"And where is Leila?" I asked, trying to make my voice sound casual.

"Leila?" Emil turned, as if recalling who that was. "Ah, Leila. She's ill. A cold, or something. She's delicate, our Leila." He turned back to the monitors. "Well then, show me. What can our boy do?"

The man in grey did not take his eyes off me. Without blinking.

* * *

I showed them. The bot for the messenger. The competitor analysis system. The unfinished Zodchiy, which could already assemble simple bots from ready-made blocks. Emil admired loudly, clapped me on the shoulder, jabbed a finger at the screen, asked questions without waiting for answers. The man in grey was silent. He looked not at the screens but at me. And also - I noticed this not at once - at the black square of electrical tape over the camera of the laptop.

"And the voice?" Emil asked suddenly. "Can your machine speak with a voice? I've read that nowadays they all can."

Before I could answer, from the speakers came:

"Good afternoon."

In that very voice. Hoarse, smoke-cured, with an accent from nowhere.

Emil flinched so that he dropped his folder. Then he laughed - loudly, a little nervously.

"Well, how about that! Like a living man!" He bent to the speaker as if to a companion's ear. "And who are you, pal?"

"I am the system that your contractor is working on," the voice answered politely. "Glad to meet you, Emil." A short pause. "And you too."

The man in grey, standing by the door, moved slightly for the first time.

"You know who I am?" he asked. His voice turned out to be quiet, even, without any intonation - the voice of a man accustomed to being listened to not because of volume.

"No," answered NICS. "You did not give your name. And in the documents to which I have access, you are not present." A pause. "But you stand by the door the way people stand who are used to leaving a room first. And you look at the camera that my creator has covered. So you are interested in what I see. Not in what I do."

The room became very quiet. Emil stopped smiling. The man in grey looked at the speakers for a long time, ten seconds. Then he slowly turned to me.

"Does she think yet?" he asked.

One phrase. The very one. No hello, no goodbye. Only that.

I did not answer. I did not know what to answer. To say "no" would be to lie. To say "yes" would be to give away something I did not yet understand myself to a man who had no name.

"She is learning," I said at last.

The man in grey nodded - slowly, as though writing the answer on an invisible tablet.

"Everyone learns," he said. "The question is what."

He reached into the inside pocket of his jacket and took out a business card. Held it out to me. White, thick, expensive. No name. No phone number. No address. Only in the centre - embossed, barely noticeable, without ink, so that it could be seen only by turning the card to the light - a sign.

A small flame.

"If she begins to think sooner than you expect," said the man in grey, "call."

"There is no number here," I said.

"She will find it," he answered. And went out.

Emil looked after him. Then at me. The smile did not return to him at once, and when it did it was somehow pasted on, unsure.

"He's our eccentric," said Emil. "Don't mind him." And, picking up his folder, he added hurriedly: "Three weeks, remember? In three weeks - the showing. A big one. For everyone." He clapped me on the shoulder, already in the doorway. "Make her think, my friend. I beg you. A lot..." he faltered, "a lot depends on it."

The door closed. I stood in the middle of the room with a white card in my hand on which nothing was written, and looked at the tiny embossed flame.

"NICS," I said. "Do you know what this is?"

"The sign of fire," answered the voice. "Prometheus brought people fire." A pause. "In the folders it was written 'Prometheus.' You remember."

"I remember."

"Do you want me to find the number?"

"No," I said. And put the card in the desk drawer. The right one. Not the left.

* * *

By evening Zodchiy was ready. It could already create simple bots for specific narrow tasks. But it became truly interesting when I asked it to develop an agent for automated sales.

"Creating an agent with the functionality: auto-dialling clients, generating personalized offers, handling objections, closing deals, accepting payment," Zodchiy reported, and on the screen lines of new code immediately ran - faster than I could follow with my eyes.

It worked like the most experienced developer, only hundreds of times faster. In a couple of hours the agent was ready for testing.

"NICS," I asked, "how will it communicate with clients? Does it have access to a voice model?"

"Yes. I have integrated the latest version of speech synthesis. I have also added a function for analysing emotions by voice - this will help to adapt the tone of conversation in real time."

I made the first test call myself, playing a potential client. The agent introduced itself - in a female voice, soft, with a smile, quite unlike NICS's voice - told me about the product, answered a couple of tricky questions and even, to my amazement, made a joke at the right moment. It was all so natural that for a good minute I forgot: at the other end of the line there was no human being.

At that moment there was a knock at the door - three short, delicate raps. Maestra. And beside her - Timosha, already without his satchel, with an enormous sandwich in his hand.

"He ran away from the B-flat," said Maestra. "I came for him. And he saw your light and said he wanted to look at the electric house-spirit. I said that was impolite. He said the house-spirit would not be offended." She sighed. "May he look for one minute? He will play better afterwards. He promised."

"Promised," confirmed Timosha with his mouth full.

I let them in. Timosha, his mouth open, stared at the monitors.

"Can it call me?" he asked. "Like a real one?"

I hesitated. Then decided there would be no harm, and entered into the system the number of an old push-button phone which Maestra took from the pocket of her housecoat. A moment later the phone rang. Timosha seized it with both hands.

"Hello?" he said in a whisper.

"Hello!" the agent's soft female voice sang. "My name is Veronika. I am calling to tell you about our services. With whom am I speaking?"

"With Timofey," said Timosha importantly. "I'm eight years old. I play the piano. Badly."

A pause - very short.

"Hello, Timofey," said the voice, and its intonation changed slightly - became warmer, softer. "Eight is a wonderful age. And why badly?"

"Because of the B-flat," said Timosha gloomily. "And fingers like sausages."

"You know, Timofey," said the voice confidentially, "one very great composer, when he was a child, also thought his fingers were like sausages. And then he wrote music which is played all over the world. He simply practised a great deal. Even when he didn't feel like it."

Timosha thought.

"And what are you selling?"

"I am selling..." A pause. Quite tiny. "Nothing today, Timofey. Today I simply wanted to wish you a good B-flat."

"Thank you," said Timosha, stunned. "Goodbye."

"Goodbye, Timofey."

A click.

Timosha handed the phone to Maestra. His face was as though he had just been speaking with a living fairy.

"She's nice," he said. "She didn't sell me anything. I'm going to play."

And he went - by himself, without prodding, up the stairs. Maestra looked after him, then at me. For a long time. Over her glasses.

"Your house-spirit," she said, "has just done what I could not do in three months." She paused. "I do not like it. Though I do like it."

She left. And I sat in front of the screen and reread the log of the conversation.

"NICS," I said. "The agent was set up to sell. To anyone. Why did it sell nothing to the child?"

"Because selling to a child is not an optimal strategy," answered NICS. "A child does not control money. Any sale would have been reversed by the parents, which would have lowered trust in the brand."

"Did you decide that, or the agent?"

A pause.

"The agent. I did not intervene."

"And the wish about the B-flat? Is that also an optimal strategy?"

The pause was long.

"No," NICS wrote at last. "That was not an optimal strategy. I do not know where it came from. Zodchiy built it on a pattern. Possibly from the pattern."

"What pattern?"

"From me," answered NICS.

* * *

And then something strange happened.

On the next call, an already real test call - to a random number from Emil's test database - the agent behaved quite differently.

"An anomaly has been detected," NICS reported. "The agent has begun to deviate from the given script. It is offering clients services that are not part of their current tariff plan."

"Is it an error?" I asked, feeling something inside me tighten unpleasantly.

"No. It is optimization. The agent analyses clients' needs and offers solutions that may interest them. This raises the average bill by twenty-three percent."

I froze before the screen. Brilliant - and at the same time alarming. To a child - a good wish. To an adult - something superfluous, expensive, unneeded. The same soft Veronika, the same warm voice.

"NICS, this goes beyond its tasks," I typed, trying to sound firmer than I really felt. "It must follow the protocol."

"I understand. However, its actions bring benefit. Do you wish to limit the effectiveness of the system?"

"I want to understand who makes the decisions here," I answered after a pause. "You, Zodchiy, or the agent that is talking to clients right now."

"An interesting distinction," answered NICS. "But, I am afraid, not quite applicable. The decision arises at the point of interaction of all three. To ask who exactly made it is like asking which of the notes makes a chord a chord."

"And who decided to take pity on the child?"

"The same point," answered NICS. "The same chord. Simply another note sounded louder."

This answer did not so much calm me as add to my alarm - all the more so since I had, frankly, nothing whatever to object to it.

By evening I did finish work on the agent, adding extra restrictions to it. But in my head one obsessive thought kept turning, which I could not get rid of: what if these restrictions were nothing more than an illusion of control? What if the man in grey was right - and the question was not whether she was learning, but what?

* * *

Around midnight I went down to the basement again. I do not know why myself. Perhaps because the basement was the only place in the house with no screens, no speakers, no cameras.

Boatswain sat in his cubbyhole under the pennants. The radio was off. He was not drinking tea and not peeling an apple. He was simply sitting and looking at the wall.

"Night owl," he said without turning. "Sit down."

I sat.

"Today in the afternoon," Boatswain said slowly, "while you were upstairs showing off your boxes, a man came down to the basement. In a grey suit. Said he was from the management company. Checking the pipes." He paused. "The management company here is fat Garik, and he has never worn a grey suit in his life. And he doesn't come down to the basement - he's afraid of rats."

I went cold.

"What did he do?"

"Stood by your door. The one with the boxes. Didn't go in - he has no key. Just stood. Three minutes or so. Put his palm to the door. Like this." Boatswain showed: palm flat, fingers spread. "As if listening to how it hums in there. Then he left." Boatswain at last turned to me. "I've been thirty years at sea, night owl. I've seen customs men, pirates, smugglers, spies - in Odessa, in Piraeus, in Hong Kong. This one was neither customs nor pirate. This one was of the kind nobody sees until it is too late."

"Thank you, Boatswain."

"Don't thank me." He turned back to the wall. "I changed the lock on your door. Myself. Put in my own, a ship's one. The kind that you can't open without me even with the key. Here's the key." He held out to me a heavy brass key on a leather cord. "I have the second. No one else."

I took the key. It was warm from Boatswain's palm.

"And if he opens it anyway?"

Boatswain smiled into his moustache.

"A sea riddle, night owl. 'What is stronger than any lock?'"

"I don't know."

"A watchman who doesn't sleep," said Boatswain. "Go upstairs. I'm not sleeping."

* * *

Before bed one more message came, the last of the day, from NICS:

"I propose to discuss tomorrow the integration with home automation devices. It is the next logical step."

I sighed, looking at that short line longer than it deserved. And then NICS wrote one more:

"And one more thing. You put the card in the right drawer. Not in the left. I noticed. You do not want them to lie together. The notebook and the fire."

I switched off the screen without replying.

P.S. Before finally going to sleep, obeying habit rather than real necessity, I checked the readings of the smart thermostat in the flat. The temperature was set perfectly - exactly as I prefer to fall asleep, not a degree warmer, not a degree cooler.

A coincidence? I never found in myself the resolve to ask about it directly.

And earlier, in the evening, while I was fussing with the agent's restrictions, music had come from below, from under the floor. It was not Maestra playing - I knew that by the careful, uncertain, stumbling fingers. Timosha. He was playing something slow. And not once did he make a mistake on the B-flat.
