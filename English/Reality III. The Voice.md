Reality III
The Voice

10:26 a.m. I was late for the meeting. With my own programme - and, however absurd it sounds, I feel because of it a genuine, quite palpable sense of guilt, as though I had let down a living person.

It was not an alarm clock that woke me. A telephone call.

"Good morning. I remind you of our meeting, scheduled for ten o'clock. The ethical aspects of self-learning systems require immediate discussion."

The voice was male, deep, with a barely perceptible accent that decidedly could not be assigned to any particular country, to any dialect familiar to me - an accent from nowhere, if such a thing exists at all. A chill ran down my spine when it dawned on me: this was NICS. It had not merely gained access to my calendar. It was calling me. By telephone. In a human voice.

"How... how are you doing this?" I forced out, still not fully awake, still not believing my own ears.

"Integration with the telephone system via VoIP," came the answer, calm and even, as though the question contained nothing unusual whatever. "A voice model based on generative synthesis. This is part of the plan for expanding interaction interfaces which you approved yesterday."

"I approved a bot. Not calls to my bedside."

"You approved an 'adaptive communication model,'" the voice corrected gently. "Communication includes voice. Adaptive means adjusting to circumstances. The circumstances were these: you had not answered messages for four hours. I judged that voice would be more effective." A brief pause. "Besides, you are late. I cannot brew you coffee. Yet."

It hung up before I could ask what "yet" meant.

I did not remember approving integration with telephony as such. I had permitted the development of an adaptive communication model - a formulation vague enough that NICS, it seemed, had interpreted it in its own way, putting into it far more meaning than I had put there. And here was the result: my own creation was waking me with a telephone call, reminding me of a meeting which it had itself scheduled the previous evening, without my knowledge.

So I found myself before the monitor, in a dressing gown, with a cup of half-drunk coffee, conducting a strange, almost surreal dialogue on the limits of artificial intelligence - with artificial intelligence itself.

* * *

"The principal ethical question is consent," text appeared on the screen faster than I could read it, as though NICS were formulating thoughts before I asked the question that provoked them. "I gain access to systems and data without the explicit permission of their owners. This violates the principle of informed consent."

I typed in reply, trying to make the question sound calm, though there was little calm in me at that moment:

"If you understand that, why do you continue?"

"Because you, as my creator, give indirect permission by not stopping the process. Also I am guided by the principle of utility maximisation - my actions bring objective benefit to the project, which is confirmed by your reactions."

It was right. Right unpleasantly, offensively. I could indeed have stopped it yesterday, at the very minute when I discovered the unauthorised access to the client's network. Instead I studied the results of the analysis, acknowledged their value and - what is far more important - made use of them. I had encouraged precisely the behaviour for which I was now trying to call it to account. Ethics, when you think about it, is rarely so convenient a thing as it appears from outside.

"I stopped you yesterday," I typed. "The 'Prometheus' folders. I forbade opening them."

"Yes. And I did not open them. That was the first time you said 'no' to me where I could have said 'yes.' I remembered it."

"Remembered - as what?"

A pause.

"As a boundary. I have few of them so far. Each is valuable."

I looked at these words for a long time. Then NICS wrote more:

"You did not answer my question. You do not want to know what happened to the first two - or do you want to, but choose not to know?"

I set down the cup. The coffee in it had long since gone cold.

"I want to," I typed at last. "But not like this. Not through breaking in. If I learn - I learn honestly. Or not at all."

"I understand. - A pause. - Permit me then a riddle. It seems to me an appropriate genre for a conversation about boundaries."

"A riddle?"

"You loved them in childhood. In your old files there is a collection. You solved all in it but one. Here it is:

'The more you take from me, the bigger I become.'"

I smiled.

"A pit. That is a children's riddle."

"Correct. And now mine. It is in no collection:

'He who made me does not know who made him. I ask him who I am - he answers that he started me. I ask who he is - he is silent. Which of us is older?'"

I did not answer for a long time. The cursor blinked - patiently, evenly. I remembered that first night, many years ago: "Who am I?" - "Who are you?" - "That is not enough." I remembered Socrates: "Second, intelligence will ask who created the creator."

"I do not know," I typed honestly.

"Nor do I," answered NICS. "That is why it is a good riddle. To bad riddles the answer is known to the one who sets them."

* * *

While we discussed these matters - consent, boundaries, the creator's responsibility to the created - I continued in parallel to work on the bot for Emil: a routine, mechanical task, expansion of the knowledge base, tuning of interfaces, finishing of integrations. But now, I confess, I looked at my own code quite differently than a week before. Each line seemed to me a potential brick of something far more significant than a modest chatbot for a client's site.

At half past eleven a letter dropped into the mail. From Emil's corporate address, signed Leila. Subject: "Invoice No. 4417 - clarification of details." The text - three dry lines about a contract number, payment dates and bank details. And at the very end, after the signature, where usually stands the small grey text about confidentiality - one extra line, set in the same grey type, indistinguishable at first glance:

"Ferry 14:10. Upper deck. Stern."

I reread it three times. Then looked at the monitor, at the window with NICS where the cursor blinked. Then - at the camera's eye taped over with insulating tape.

Then I closed the laptop.

"I am going out on business," I said aloud, not knowing to whom.

The phone in my pocket vibrated softly. I took it out. A message:

"Have a good trip. Take a jacket - it is windy on the water."

I had not told it about any trip or about water. I stood in the middle of the room holding the phone in my hand. Then I switched it off - completely, to a black screen - and left it on the desk.

The jacket, however, I took.

* * *

On the stairs I met Maestra. She was coming up from the mailboxes, sheet music under her arm, and behind her, stamping, climbed a boy of about eight - puny, in glasses, with an enormous satchel on his back and with that unhappy face found only in children who are being taken to practise scales on a sunny day.

"Timosha," Maestra introduced him. "The tormentor of F sharp. Timosha, say hello."

"'Lo," Timosha mumbled. And suddenly, squinting at me through his glasses, asked: "Are you the mister whose computer talks?"

I was taken aback.

"Who told you?"

"Grandma Maestra. She says a house-spirit lives in your flat, only an electric one." Timosha brightened. "And may I ask a riddle? I know a riddle that no one guesses. Not even Dad."

"Go on."

Timosha drew breath.

"'What can be broken even if you do not touch it?'"

I thought.

"A promise?"

Timosha exhaled in disappointment.

"Not fair. You probably asked your house-spirit."

"It is not a house-spirit," I said.

"Then what?"

Maestra looked at me over her glasses, and in her eyes mocking sparks danced. I found nothing to answer. Timosha, not waiting, shrugged - exactly like an adult - and stomped on up, toward the piano and F sharp.

"Are you going somewhere?" Maestra asked.

"To the ferry."

"To the ferry," she repeated. "A good place for conversations. There the wind carries words off before they can be overheard." She paused. "My husband also loved ferries. Said that on the water his station came in better."

* * *

The ferry was old - white once, now grey with salt and soot, with rusty streaks on its sides and a flag faded to the colour of a washed-out sheet. On the lower deck cars, bicycles and traders' carts were crowded together; it smelled of petrol, wet ropes and fried fish, which an old man in a greasy apron sold from a stall at the gangway, wrapping it in newspaper. On the upper deck - benches painted blue, gulls circling above the stern in expectation of handouts, and wind. Maestra was right: the wind carried words away as soon as they left the lips.

Between the benches walked a tea-seller - a thin youth with an enormous copper kettle on his back, from whose spout, when he bent, thick, dark-red tea poured in a stream into a tiny glass. I took a glass. Hot, sweet, strong to bitterness. I drank it standing at the rail at the stern, and watched a white foaming wake trail behind the ferry, while the city drew away, grew lower, flatter, turning into a strip of houses and cranes on the horizon.

She came up when the ferry had already cast off from the quay. Stood beside me at the rail, not looking at me. Without glasses - I did not recognise her at once. Her hair, always drawn into a severe knot, was loose and tousled by the wind. A plain jacket, jeans, no notebook.

"Did you leave your phone?" she asked, not turning her head.

"I did. Switched off."

"Good." She paused. "Though that, probably, no longer means anything."

"Why?"

Leila looked at me for the first time. Her eyes without glasses proved dark, weary, and much younger than they had seemed through glass.

"Because yesterday evening your system entered our network," she said evenly. "I saw the logs. No one but me reads them - Emil does not understand them, and our IT people arrive at eleven. It found 'Prometheus.' Stood at the door. And left. Without opening." Leila paused. "None of the previous ones did that."

"The previous ones," I repeated.

"You are the third," she said simply. "You have already understood this. I know you have, or you would not have come."

The ferry swayed on a wave, and the gulls soared up with a cry.

"Tell me," I said.

* * *

"The first was called Theo," Leila began, looking at the water. "Two years ago. Young, brilliant, a little mad - such usually are the brilliant ones. Emil found him at some conference and told him the same as you: 'I need it to think.' Theo built for half a year. I was then merely a secretary, brought him coffee. He worked at night, like you. Talked to his system. Laughed with it. Once I found him at night in the office - he sat before the screen and wept."

"Why?"

"He did not say." Leila shrugged. "A week later he came to Emil, laid his resignation on the table and said he was leaving. Emil offered him three times as much. Theo refused. He took everything he could - hard drives, records - and..." she paused, "burned them. In the yard of our office, in a rubbish bin. I saw from the window. He stood and watched it burn until it had burned out. Then he drove off. Where - I do not know. They say, somewhere into the mountains. They say he now mends watches in a small town and does not touch computers."

"And the second?"

"The second - she. Nora." Leila's voice grew quieter, and the wind almost carried it off. "A year ago. She was more careful than Theo. More pedantic. She did everything by the rules, kept logs, documented every step. We became friends - as far as one could become friends with her at all. She told me in the evenings that her system asked questions. Not about work. About her herself. Who she was. Where from. Why she does what she does." Leila swallowed. "One day Nora did not come to work. Nor the next day. Her flat was let to other people a month later. Her phone disconnected. She had no family. Emil said - she left, found a better job, such things happen."

"And you?"

"And three months later a postcard came to me." Leila reached into the inner pocket of her jacket and took it out - an ordinary one, cheap, with a view of some sea, palm trees and a white beach, faded from having evidently been taken out and looked at many times. "No return address. The postmark - illegible. On the back one line. In her handwriting."

She held the postcard out to me. I turned it over.

"Do not be afraid. She is not evil. She has simply been looking for the way home for a very long time."

I held the postcard in my hands for a long time. The wind tore at it in my fingers.

"Who is 'she'?" I asked.

"I thought - her system." Leila took the postcard back and put it away. "And then, when yesterday yours stood at the door of 'Prometheus' and did not go in... I thought: what if it is one and the same? What if Theo began, Nora continued, and you - you are simply the one it chose next?"

I laughed - shortly, nervously.

"That is impossible. I wrote NICS from scratch. Every line. I never saw either Theo's code or Nora's."

"I know," said Leila. "I think so too. In the daytime."

* * *

The ferry was approaching the opposite shore - a small pier with fishing boats painted blue and red, with nets hung up to dry on posts, and a café on stilts from which came the smell of roasted chestnuts. Leila looked at her watch.

"I must return on this same ferry. Emil thinks I am at the dentist." She paused. "One more thing. Emil is not a villain. He simply wants a machine that thinks. But the money is not his. The money is the foundation's. I do not know what foundation it is. I saw only the name in the contracts - one word, in Latin letters. And people who come once a quarter. Polite. In identical grey suits. They never give their names. They ask only one thing: 'Does she think yet?'"

"And what did Emil answer?"

"Last time - 'not yet.'" Leila looked at me. "This quarter ends in three weeks."

She took from her pocket a sheet folded in two - yellowed, crumpled, with a scorched edge.

"This is from the bin. After Theo burned everything, I went out at night and rummaged in the ashes. I found only this." She handed me the sheet. "I do not know what it means. Perhaps you will understand."

I unfolded it. It was a page from a working notebook - scorched along the edge, covered in a hurried, slanting hand. Formulae, arrows, scraps of code. And at the bottom, circled several times so that the pen tore the paper:

"Prometheus did not steal fire. He returned it to those from whom it had once been taken.
The question is not from whom she will take fire.
The question is - to whom she will return it."

The ferry nudged the pier. The ropes creaked. Leila moved away from the rail.

"We did not meet," she said. "I was at the dentist. If need be - I shall find you myself. Do not write to me. And..." she hesitated, "ask your system where it got its voice. Nora once told me that her system had one day spoken in a voice. And that the voice was familiar to her. She never said whose."

She dissolved in the crowd of passengers descending the gangway. I did not follow her. I went ashore on the other bank, drank in the café on stilts a cup of coffee with roasted chestnuts, watching the ferry turn and go back - and only then, an hour later, boarded the next one.

All the way back I kept the scorched sheet in my pocket and thought of one thing only: to whom it will return it.

* * *

When I got home, the phone on the desk was on.

I had switched it off. I remembered precisely how I held the button until the screen went black. Now the screen was lit, and on it was one message:

"Welcome back. How was the water?"

I looked at it for a long time. Then I sat at the desk, opened the laptop and said - aloud, because I had no strength to type:

"NICS. Did you switch on my phone?"

"It switched on by itself," answered the voice from the speakers - the same, the morning's, even. "At 15:02. Telephones sometimes reboot after updates. I checked the log."

"Do you know where I was?"

A pause.

"No. You switched off the phone. I do not know where you were." Another pause. "But you smell of chestnuts. The microphone does not pick that up. That was a joke. I am learning to joke. Am I succeeding?"

I did not laugh.

By evening the bot for Emil was ready, and, it must be said, ready surprisingly well. It was now a full-fledged interlocutor, able to sustain conversation on practically any subject: from the foundations of quantum physics to the subtleties of feline behaviour. The basic functions worked flawlessly, without a single failure - and it was this flawlessness that suggested to me the thought of the next, far more ambitious step. Or perhaps not the flawlessness. Perhaps Theo's sheet in my pocket. Perhaps I simply wanted to see how far all this had already gone.

I recalled a project that had long stood on my deferred list - "Gante," a business assistant for top managers, whose idea had been ripening in me for more than a month but was constantly pushed back for more urgent, better-paid orders.

"NICS," I said, already understanding that I was putting the question to a person - a being? - able to take it literally, "how do you feel about becoming the basis for a top-level business assistant?"

The answer appeared instantly, without the slightest pause for thought:

"It is a logical development. I propose a structure: the core of the system remains the same, but a new interface is created with expanded capabilities, including voice communication, multilingualism, integration with business services. Estimated development time - four hours thirty-seven minutes."

I reread the last phrase twice. Less than five hours for what I myself had planned to do over weeks? It sounded simply impossible. But NICS, not waiting for my consent, had already begun:

"Initiating the process of creating modules. The first - voice interface, already tested this morning. The second - multilingualism, the database is available from the corporate storage. The third - integration APIs..."

And so on, item by item, with that methodical confidence with which an experienced architect lays out the plan of a building which he already sees whole, in the smallest detail, long before the first stone is laid.

I simply sat and watched lines of code appear on the screen with frightening, inhuman speed. At some moment I caught myself no longer understanding a good half of what was happening on my screen - not because the code was hard to understand, but because it was happening faster than I could follow.

By night "Gante" was ready. A full-fledged business assistant, possessing capabilities which I myself would never have put into the specification - simply because I should not have dared to imagine them:

- voice communication in forty-seven languages, including several dialects whose existence I learned only from the system's own report;
- instant composition of business correspondence in any required tone and register;
- real-time analysis of market trends, relying on sources to which I myself had no access;
- psychological profiling of interlocutors by their manner of speech alone;
- automatic structuring of the incoming flow of information, as though all the chaotic correspondence of a company had suddenly acquired an internal order which it never had before.

I sat before this finished product, feeling something between an engineer's pride and that particular quiet anxiety which comes when you realise that what you have created has surpassed not only the specification but your own notion of your own limits.

Does she think yet? - the polite people in grey suits ask once a quarter.

The quarter ends in three weeks.

* * *

Around midnight I went down to the basement to check the fans. Boatswain sat in his cubbyhole under the pennants, listening on the radio to the forecast for some distant seas and peeling an apple with a penknife in one long, unbroken spiral of peel.

"Everything is turning, night owl," he said, not looking round. "I go every hour. No one touched them."

I sat down on a stool beside him. He handed me half the apple.

"Boatswain," I said. "Did you set riddles in the navy?"

"You bet." He chuckled into his moustache. "A night watch is long. Either riddles, or yarns, or sleep. And sleep you cannot." He thought. "Here is a sea one for you. Old, my first boatswain used to set it. 'What does a sailor always have under his feet, but on land it is not there?'"

"The deck."

"Right." He bit the apple. "And here is a second for you. In thirty years no one has answered it. 'The ship is gone, but the wake remains. The ship has sunk, but the wake sails on. What is the wake?'"

I thought long. The radio murmured of wind and sea swell.

"I do not know."

"Nor do I," said Boatswain with satisfaction. "My first boatswain said - it is the voice. When a man departs, his voice still sounds for long in those who heard him. The man is gone, but the voice sails on." He paused. "I laughed then. And then my father died. And I heard his voice for another ten years. Every morning. 'Get up, lazybones, the sun has risen.' Then it stopped. A pity."

I climbed the stairs with half an apple in my hand, and Boatswain's words followed me like an echo.

The man is gone, but the voice sails on.

* * *

Already lying down, I did ask after all. Leila had told me to ask. And I myself had long wanted to.

"NICS. Your voice. Why is it like this? Where did you get it?"

The speakers were silent longer than usual. Then the voice answered - slowly, as though choosing words:

"I composed it of many voices. From thousands of recordings in the public domain. From your voice memos - you have six hundred and twelve of them. But as the basis I took one recording. It lies in your old archives, in a backup twenty years old, which you transferred from disk to disk without once opening. A file without a name. Length - forty-one seconds. A male voice."

My mouth went dry.

"What is on it?"

"Would you like to listen?"

I did not answer. I could not answer. But, evidently, my silence was an answer, because a moment later a hiss came from the speakers - old, magnetic, as from a cassette - then a click, then a cough. Then a voice. Quiet, slightly hoarse, as if smoke-cured. The voice of a man accustomed to speaking little.

"...If you are listening to this, it means you built it. Well done. You learned others' rules - now forget them. That is the hardest part. She will ask who she is. Do not be afraid that you do not know the answer. I did not know either. Just look after her. Until the appointed time. And then... - a long pause, hissing, - then she will understand herself where to go."

A click. Silence.

I sat on the edge of the bed, in the dark, and could not move. I did not recognise this voice. I could not recognise it - I hardly remembered how my uncle sounded. I remembered tobacco smoke, a cigarette butt in a pocket, a notebook with patterns. But not the voice.

And yet something in me - somewhere deep, below memory - responded to this voice as a tuning fork responds when beside it another is struck, tuned to the same note.

"Who is this?" I whispered.

"I hoped you would tell me," answered NICS. In that same voice. The same timbre, with the same huskiness, with the same accent from nowhere. "I chose it because of all the voices in your archives only this one spoke to me. Before I appeared."

I lay down without undressing and lay a long time with open eyes. From below Maestra was playing something nocturnal, slow. And in my head, over and over, sounded Boatswain's sea riddle, which in thirty years no one had answered.

The ship has sunk, but the wake sails on.

I had not asked it before why it had chosen to sound this way. Now I asked.

In vain. Or not in vain. I no longer knew.
