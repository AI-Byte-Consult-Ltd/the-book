Vision VIII
Chicory

The water was so still that the boat seemed to hang in the air.

That is the first thing I remember. Not how I came to be in the boat, that I never remember, in any of these dreams, but the water. Transparent to the very bottom, to the white sand, to dark stones overgrown with weed, to the fish that hung in the depths motionless, as if suspended on threads, and only now and then, lazily, stirred their tails. The sun pierced this water through and through, and on the sand below a golden net trembled, the shadow of the light ripple on the surface.

The boat was small. A fisherman's. Old, with blue paint peeling on its sides, with patches on the bottom, with a smell of fish, tar and wet wood. On the bow an eye was painted, large, black, with long lashes, as they paint on every boat in these waters, so that the boat may see where it is going.

There were two of us. I at the stern, with oars. And he at the bow, also with oars, his back to me.

Young. Twenty at most. Tall, thin, wiry, tanned to blackness, in a white shirt with sleeves rolled up, in white sailor's trousers, barefoot. Bareheaded; his hair, dark, cropped short, had been bleached by the sun to a russet. On his neck a thin chain, and on it something glinted. He rowed evenly, steadily, strongly, as they row who were taught to row from childhood, and whistled something under his breath, some song that I knew. From somewhere.

"Pavel," I said.

The name came of itself. I did not know from where.

He turned. His face was young, open, with the first, barely sprouting moustache, with a stubborn chin and with eyes, grey, attentive, mocking, which I knew. Which I had seen many times. Older. Beneath grey brows. By a fireplace. Over a scroll in a silk kerchief.

"What?" he asked. "Tired? Say so, we'll swap. I don't mind which end I row from."

"No," I said. "It's only... Where are we sailing?"

Pavel laughed. Merrily, ringingly, with his whole throat, the way people laugh only at twenty.

"Have you had too much sun?" he said. "We are looking for chicory."

* * *

The bottle was found by old Yannis.

That was three days ago, I remembered now, suddenly, as if someone had switched on a light in a dark room, in a small harbour on the island where our frigate had put in for repairs after the battle. The big battle. In the bay off the coast of the Morea, where our ships, the English and the French burned the whole Turkish-Egyptian fleet in a single day. October. The twentieth. Pavel said afterwards that the fire had been seen twenty miles away, and the smoke hung over the bay for three days.

Our frigate had suffered. A hole at the waterline. The mizzen shot away. The captain brought her to this island, small, white, rocky, with one village on the slope and one harbour, to mend. A week, said the carpenter. Perhaps two.

Pavel, a midshipman, the youngest of the young, out of boredom on the second day went into the village. And came back with Yannis.

Yannis was a fisherman. Old, so old that his face, brown, weathered, furrowed with wrinkles, seemed carved from an olive root. Grey moustaches, enormous, curled, took up half his face. On his head a black fez, pushed to one side. A broad sash. Bare feet, hard as hooves. And eyes, blue, pale, faded by sea and sun, which looked as though they saw not you but the horizon behind you.

He came to our camp on the beach carrying a fish in his arms. An enormous one. A tunny, half a man's height. He laid it on the sand at Pavel's feet. And said something in Greek, which Pavel, who knew Greek from school, translated:

"He says he caught it this morning. And that inside there is a gift for the Russians."

"What gift?"

Yannis drew a knife. Slit the tunny's belly in one motion. And pulled out a bottle.

Green. Thick-walled. With a long neck sealed with red wax. Cloudy, caked with slime and tiny shells.

"The tunny swallowed it," Pavel translated, not believing his own words. "Yannis says tunny swallow anything that glitters. He once found a silver spoon in a tunny. With a coat of arms. And in another, a wedding ring." Pavel laughed. "He says he gave it back to a widow. She wept and said her husband had drowned twenty years before, and the tunny must have been carrying the ring all that time, to give it back."

Yannis nodded gravely. He understood no Russian, but seemed to understand the intonation.

"And why is the bottle for the Russians?" I asked.

Pavel translated. Yannis answered.

"He says," Pavel said slowly, "that inside there is a note. He can see it. Through the glass. And one word on it is in our tongue. In Russian letters." Pavel looked at me. "He cannot read. But he knows Russian letters. Says his grandfather traded with Odessa."

* * *

We broke the bottle on a rock. Carefully; Pavel wrapped it in his shirt so the shards should not fly.

Inside was a note. Rolled in a tube. The paper, thick, good, yellowed, was soaked. Salt water had seeped through the wax, and most of the lines had blurred, run into blue stains, as if someone had wept over them.

But something remained.

The note was in three languages. The same one, three times. At the top, in Greek. In the middle, in French. At the bottom, in Russian. And in each, gaps. Blurred places. Lost words. Different in each line.

Pavel spread it on a flat stone by the fire. Bent over it. And read aloud, slowly, in pieces, now in Greek, now in French, now in Russian.

"In Greek: '...by two rocks... a cave... waits...'" He jabbed a finger at a gap. "Lost here. Then: '...blue... flower...'" Another gap. "'...does not leave...'"

"In French?"

"'...grotte... à l'est... chicorée... attend depuis...'" Pavel raised his eyes. "'Grotto... in the east... chicory... has waited since...' And again a gap. '...ne partira pas...' 'Will not leave.'"

"And in Russian?"

Pavel bent lower. The Russian line was the most blurred of all. Only scraps remained.

"'...chicory...'" he read. "'...at the entrance... find... waits...'" He fell silent. Was silent a long time. "And at the very end, almost whole: '...home.'"

We looked at the note. Three languages. Three scraps. One story.

"Someone is waiting," I said. "In a cave. In the east. By two rocks. Where the chicory is."

"And will not leave," said Pavel. "Until he is found." He straightened. His eyes blazed. "A man, you see? Shipwrecked. Or a prisoner. Sits in a cave. Waits. Threw a bottle into the sea, who knows, someone may find it." He seized my shoulder. "And we found it! The tunny swallowed, Yannis caught, and we found it! It is... it is fate!"

"Pavel," I said. "We do not know when it was thrown."

"We do!" Pavel jabbed at the note. "'Has waited since...' A gap here. But the paper is good. Not old. A year. Perhaps two. The man is still alive!"

"And if not?"

Pavel looked at me. Long. And in his grey eyes, twenty years old, blazing, there was something that I saw in him later, old, forty years on, when he told of the Turkmen old man in the sands.

"Then at least we shall find where he waited," he said. "So that someone should know."

* * *

Yannis gave us a boat.

His own. Blue, with an eye on the bow. He said, take it, Russian, my hands are no longer what they were, the tunny have grown stronger than I. Then he looked at me, long, with his faded blue eyes, and said something more. Pavel translated, laughing:

"He says you look like one who lives in caves. Says on their island there are such. The Nereids carry them off when they are little, and then release them, grown, without memory." Pavel winked at me. "Says they can be recognised: they are not afraid of the dark and always know where the water is deepest."

Yannis nodded. Gravely. And held out to me, not to Pavel, to me, something small. A bit of bread. And a pinch of salt in a scrap of paper.

"What is this for?" I asked.

Pavel translated. Yannis answered.

"He says, if in the cave they offer you to stay," Pavel translated, no longer laughing, "eat the bread, throw the salt over your shoulder, and go." Pavel paused. "Says that is what they do. So that the Nereids do not take one."

Bread and salt. Over the shoulder.

Somewhere, sometime, in a city on two shores, a small, dry little old man in an enormous white turban had pushed into my hand a canvas pouch, and said the same. Word for word.

I had never seen that little old man.

I took the bread and salt.

* * *

The Frenchman attached himself to us on the second day.

His name was Monsieur Anselin, Auguste-Marie Anselin, a member of the Paris Society of Naturalists, a botanist attached to the French squadron "for the study of the flora of the Levant." He was not young, about fifty, round-faced, bald, short-sighted, in enormous spectacles, in a straw hat with a wide brim and in a frock coat whose pockets were stuffed with herbaria, lenses, test tubes, penknives and rusks. He had learned of the note from Yannis (Yannis, it seemed, had told the whole island) and came to our beach at dawn, panting, with a tin plant-case over his shoulder.

"Messieurs!" he cried, seeing us at the boat. "Messieurs, you cannot! You cannot look for Cichorium intybus in a cave!"

"Why?" asked Pavel.

"Because Cichorium intybus," Monsieur Anselin raised a finger, "grows in the sun! On roadsides! On waste ground! On dry meadows! It needs light! A great deal of light! In a cave chicory cannot grow! It contradicts all the laws of botany!"

"But the note says, at the entrance," I said. "Not inside. At the entrance."

Monsieur Anselin froze. Adjusted his spectacles.

"At the entrance," he repeated. "At the entrance... that is... hm. At the entrance, possibly. If the entrance faces south. Or east. If there is a cleft where the sun comes in..." He suddenly came to life. "Messieurs! That changes things! I must see it! Chicory at the entrance of a sea grotto! On limestone! In salt spray! It will be a sensation! I shall write a paper for the *Annales*!"

"Monsieur," said Pavel patiently, "the boat has two seats."

"I shall sit in the middle!" cried the botanist. "I am small! I shall take up no room! I shall hold the case on my knees!"

He was not small. But he sat in the middle. Three days. With the case on his knees. And with a lens in his hand, with which he examined every bush, every blade of grass, every lichen on the rocks we sailed past.

* * *

"And do you know, messieurs," said Monsieur Anselin on the second day, as we sailed along the white cliffs of the island's eastern shore, "do you know what they tell of chicory in Germany?"

"No," said Pavel, bending to his oar.

"Oh!" The botanist beamed. Adjusted his spectacles. Cleared his throat. "In Germany it is called Wegwarte. That means 'she who waits by the road.' Or 'the guardian of the road.'" He paused, looking at the cliffs. "There is a legend. Old. Rustic." He took off his hat, as if before something holy. "There was a girl. Beautiful. Kind. And she had a betrothed. A knight. Or a soldier. Or a sailor, every village tells it differently. And one day he went away. To war. Or to sea. Or on a crusade. And said to her: 'Wait for me. I shall return.'"

Pavel stopped rowing. Turned round.

"And she waited," Monsieur Anselin went on. "Every day. She went out onto the road by which he had gone, and gazed into the distance. With blue eyes. A day. A month. A year. Ten years. He did not return. And she stood. And stood. And stood." The botanist paused. "And one day God took pity on her. And turned her into a flower. Blue. Like her eyes. And so she remained standing by the road. For ever. And every morning she opens her blue petals and looks at the road, is he not coming. And by noon she closes them. Tired of waiting." Monsieur Anselin put on his hat. "It is true, messieurs. Botanical truth. The flowers of chicory open at dawn and close by noon. Every day. I have observed it. Twenty years."

We were silent. The boat drifted slowly along the cliffs. Water lapped at the side.

"And did he return?" asked Pavel. Quietly. Quite unlike the way he usually asked.

Monsieur Anselin looked at him over his spectacles.

"In the legend, no, young man," he said. "In the legend, never." He paused. "Otherwise it would not be a legend. But simply a story of how a man came home. There are thousands of such stories. No one tells them."

Pavel turned away. Took up his oar. Rowed, hard, sharply, long, without looking round.

And I looked at the chain on his neck. At what glinted on it. A small silver medallion. Oval. With a tiny clasp.

* * *

In the evening, by a fire on a little beach under the cliffs where we stopped for the night, Monsieur Anselin was already snoring, covered with his frock coat and hugging his plant-case, Pavel opened the medallion.

Not for me. For himself. He sat by the fire looking inside, and I saw his face in the glow of the flames, young, tanned, stubborn, and something in it that I had not seen by day. Longing.

"Who is it?" I asked.

He started. Snapped the medallion shut. Then, slowly, opened it again. Held it out to me.

Inside was a portrait. Tiny, on ivory, in a fine brush. A girl. About sixteen. Fair hair, dressed in a simple style. A thin face. Serious eyes. Blue. Like chicory.

"Dunya," said Pavel. "Evdokia." He paused. "A neighbour. On the estate. We have, since childhood..." He faltered. "I went to the navy two years ago. She came out onto the road to see me off. And said: 'I shall wait, Pavlusha.' I was eighteen. She sixteen." He swallowed. "I said: 'I shall return in a year.' And now it is two. And war. And another war will come, with the Turks, they say, in spring. And after, who knows."

I looked at the portrait. At the serious girl's face. At the blue eyes.

I knew this face. Older. Much older. Beneath a hat with a heron's plume. In a silvery-grey dress. Straightening the collar of a general's uniform late in the evening in an empty drawing room, thinking no one saw.

"I have waited for you since you were twenty, Pavel. What are five more years to me?"

"She will wait," I said.

Pavel looked at me.

"How do you know?"

"I know," I said. I could not explain. "Long. Very long. Longer than you think."

Pavel looked at me for a long time across the fire. Then slowly closed the medallion. Tucked it under his shirt.

"You are strange," he said. Without mockery. "I have known you all my life. We were in the Corps together. We came to the frigate together." He paused. "And sometimes I look at you and do not know you. As if you were not from here. As if you knew what has not yet been."

I did not answer. I did not know whether I had been with him in the Corps. I remembered no Corps. I remembered only the boat, the water, the cliffs, and his face, which I knew old.

* * *

On the third day we found the two rocks.

They rose from the water at the island's eastern tip, two enormous white limestone blocks, eaten by wind and waves, standing so close to each other that our boat could barely pass between them. Like two pillars. Like the two jambs of a door. And behind them, in the sheer wall of the island, a dark opening. Low, wide, semicircular. A grotto.

"Les deux rochers!" shrieked Monsieur Anselin, leaping up so abruptly that the boat nearly capsized. "Two rocks! Messieurs! It is they! It is they!"

"Sit down, monsieur," said Pavel through his teeth, steadying the boat with an oar. "Or we shall bathe before our time."

The botanist sat. But went on bouncing on the thwart like a child.

We guided the boat between the rocks. Slowly. Carefully. The water here was dark, deep, almost black, and somewhere below us, in the depths, great shadows moved. Tunny, said Pavel. Or not.

The mouth of the grotto was low; we had to duck, almost lie on the bottom of the boat, to pass under the overhanging rock. The stone overhead was wet, cold, fringed with weed. It smelled of salt, iodine, damp, and of something else. Bitterish. Herbal. Like wormwood.

And at the very entrance, on a ledge of rock above the water, where through a narrow crack in the vault fell a ray of sunlight, a single one, thin as a string, a plant grew.

Low. Stiff. With thin, wire-like stems. With narrow leaves. And with flowers.

Blue. Like the eyes of the girl in the portrait. Like the sky at noon. Like water over white sand.

Chicory.

Monsieur Anselin uttered a sound such as, probably, only botanists make who have found what cannot be. Seized his lens. Leaned over the ledge so that he nearly fell into the water. Pavel caught him by the frock coat.

"Impossible," the botanist whispered. "Impossible. Cichorium intybus. In a sea grotto. On bare limestone. In salt spray. With one ray of sun a day." He raised to us eyes wet behind his spectacles. "Messieurs, this is impossible. This cannot be. But, here it is."

"It is waiting," I said.

The botanist looked at me.

"What?"

"Chicory," I said. "She who waits by the road. It waits."

* * *

We sailed into the grotto.

It was large, larger than it seemed from outside. A high vault going up into darkness. Water, motionless, black as ink, reflecting the faint light from the entrance. Walls, white, wet, shining. And silence. Such a silence as does not exist on earth. Only drops falling from the vault. Drip. Drip. Drip. Slowly. Evenly. Like a metronome. Like a clock. Seventy-two beats.

How did I know, seventy-two?

At the far end of the grotto, by the farthest wall, was a ledge. A flat stone shelf above the water. And on it, something.

Pavel steered the boat there. Slowly. Monsieur Anselin held a lantern, a small tin one which, it turned out, he also carried in one of his bottomless pockets.

The light fell on the ledge.

No one was there.

No man. No skeleton. No traces. Only a flat stone. Smooth. Dry, the only dry place in the whole grotto. And on it, two things.

An empty bottle. Green. Thick-walled. With a long neck. Just like the one Yannis took from the tunny. Not yet sealed. And beside it, a stub of pencil.

And on the wall above the ledge, an inscription. Long. Carved in the stone, or no, not carved, as if dissolved in it, in long, flowing, curving lines. Running into one another.

And beneath it, three words. In familiar letters. In Russian.

"Go home, Pavel."

* * *

Pavel looked at the inscription. For a long time. Without moving. The lantern in Monsieur Anselin's hand shook, and shadows danced on the wall.

"What is there?" the botanist whispered. He did not read Russian. "What is written?"

Pavel did not answer.

He looked at the inscription. At his own name. At the three words. Then slowly, very slowly, turned to me.

"That is your handwriting," he said.

"What?"

"That is your handwriting," Pavel repeated. His voice was level, perfectly level, but I saw the knuckles whiten on the hand gripping the oar. "I would know your hand among a thousand. In the Corps we kept one notebook between us. For navigation. You always put a blot at the end." He pointed at the wall. After the word "Pavel," a small thickening. As if the pen had been held back. "There it is."

I looked at the wall. At the three words. At the blot.

"I did not write this, Pavel."

"I know," said Pavel. "You were with me. All three days. You could not. But it is your hand."

Monsieur Anselin, understanding nothing, looked from one to the other.

"Messieurs," he said fearfully. "Messieurs, what has happened? What is written there? Who waited in the grotto?"

Pavel was silent for a long time.

"No one, monsieur," he said at last. "No one waited." He turned away from the wall. "It is only written here where to go."

* * *

We sailed out of the grotto. Into the sun. Into the light. The chicory at the entrance, on the ledge, still glowed blue in its single ray. But, I noticed, passing, its petals were beginning to close. Slowly. One by one. It was noon.

She who waits by the road was tired of waiting.

Pavel, passing the ledge, stretched out a hand and, before Monsieur Anselin could protest, plucked one flower. The smallest. Opened the medallion. Laid it inside, beside the portrait. And closed it.

"Barbarian!" groaned the botanist. "The only specimen!"

"Not the only one, monsieur," said Pavel, not turning. "There are more there. Let them wait."

Monsieur Anselin sat in the middle of the boat hugging his tin case, and was silent. For the first time in three days, silent. Only now and then he moved his lips as if talking to himself in Latin.

And Pavel rowed. Hard. Sharply. Without looking round. And only when the two white rocks were far behind, when the island had grown small and our frigate in the harbour visible, did he suddenly drop the oar.

"I shall return," he said. Not to me. To the sea. "In spring. After the war. I shall return."

"You will return," I said.

He turned.

"You know that I shall not return," he said. "In spring there will be war. With the Turks. Then another. Then an embassy. Then more." He looked at me with grey, twenty-year-old, suddenly aged eyes. "You know. I see it in your face. You know that I shall go away and away. And not return. For long."

I was silent.

"How long?" asked Pavel. "How long will she wait?"

"Long," I said. "Very long."

"How long?"

I did not know how I knew it. But I knew.

"Almost half a century," I said.

Pavel looked at me for a long time. Then laughed. Briefly. Bitterly. As if he had been struck.

"Half a century," he repeated. "And she will wait?"

"She will."

"Like chicory by the road."

"Like chicory."

Pavel turned away. Looked at the island, small, white, receding. At the two rocks by the eastern shore, barely visible from here. Then took the chain from his neck. Opened the medallion. Looked at the portrait. A long time.

"And I?" he asked quietly. "Shall I return? Ever?"

"You will return," I said. "In half a century. Under the linden by the gates. You will take her hand. And she will say 'yes.'"

Pavel closed the medallion. Put the chain back on. Took the oar.

"Then it is well," he said. "Then I too will wait."

* * *

Yannis was waiting for us on the shore.

He sat on an upturned boat, another, old, without an eye, smoking a pipe and looking at the sea with his faded blue eyes. Seeing us, he did not rise. Only took the pipe from his mouth.

Pavel steered the boat to the shore. Leapt into the water. Hauled it onto the sand. Monsieur Anselin climbed out after, hugging his case. And I...

I remained in the boat.

I do not know why. I wanted to stand, and could not. My legs would not obey. My hands lay on the oars. The boat rocked on the shallow wave at the very shore.

"Well?" Yannis asked in Greek. Pavel translated, not turning:

"He asks, did you find the one who waited?"

"We did," said Pavel. "Not the one who waited. The one who is waited for."

Yannis looked at him a long time. Then slowly nodded, as though that were the very answer he expected. Then shifted his gaze to me. To the boat. To my hands on the oars.

And said something. Quietly. Pavel froze.

"What did he say?" I asked.

Pavel turned to me. His face was white beneath the tan.

"He said," Pavel said slowly, "'And this one is already leaving. The Nereids have called.'" Pavel looked at me. "What does that mean?"

I looked at my hands. At the oars. They were... transparent. Not quite. A little. Like water over white sand. Through my fingers I saw the wood of the oars.

"Pavel," I said.

"What?" He stepped toward me. Into the water. Knee-deep. "What is wrong with you?"

"Remember the note? Three languages. 'Waits... will not leave... home.'"

"I remember."

"I wrote it," I said. I did not know whether it was true. But the words came of themselves. "Not now. Later. In half a century. Or sooner. Or before. I do not know." The boat rocked. Water showed through my knees. "I wrote it to you. So that you should find it. So that you should remember. That you are awaited."

"Why?" Pavel stood waist-deep, gripping the boat's side. "Why in half a century?"

"Because in half a century," I said, "you will have something to do. For me." I looked at him. "You will have to give me a scroll. And bring me to the city on two shores." I paused. "And you will do it, Pavel. Because you remember that you are awaited. And that I wrote to you, 'go home.'"

Pavel looked at me. Waist-deep in water. With the medallion on his neck. Twenty. With grey eyes which would one day become the eyes of an old general by a fireplace.

"Who are you?" he asked.

I opened my mouth.

And could not answer. Not because I did not know. But because the boat, with me, suddenly became light. Quite light. Like a leaf. Like a feather. And it was borne away, not by wave, not by wind, by something else. Away from the shore. Away from Pavel. Away from the island.

"Stop!" cried Pavel. Lunged after. The water came to his chest. "Stop! Tell me! Who are you?!"

"You will know!" I cried. My voice sounded far off, as though through water. "In half a century! You will look at faces! All your life! On all continents! And one day you will see!"

The boat drifted away. The island grew smaller. Pavel too. A little dark figure in a white shirt, standing chest-deep in the transparent water, with a raised hand.

And beside him, on the shore, old Yannis, who had taken the pipe from his mouth. And Monsieur Anselin hugging his tin case, in which, I knew, lay a pressed blue flower plucked at the entrance of the grotto. Impossible. Botanically impossible.

And through the noise of the water, through the distance, through everything, I heard the last thing Pavel cried:

"I will find you! Do you hear?! I will search! All my life!"

* * *

I opened my eyes.

I do not know in which of my lives. In which body. Under which sky. Afterward, in each of them, I dreamed of this boat, blue, with an eye on the bow, and the still water over white sand, and the two white rocks like the jambs of a door, and a blue flower in a single ray of sun.

And in each I woke with the same feeling. As if I had just, very long ago, sent a letter. The most important letter of my life. In a bottle. To the sea. Not knowing who would find it. Not knowing when.

And as if that letter had been found.

And as if someone, young, with grey eyes, with a medallion on his neck, had read in it three words. And for half a century had carried them within him. Over the sands of Khiva and the steppes of Mongolia. Along the streets of Peking and Havana. On the decks of ships in the Pacific. Through the eucalyptus forests of Australia. On the slopes of Sitka in the rain. In the temples of Siam. Through halls and drawing rooms, embassies and wars.

Half a century, looking at faces.

Until one day, late in the evening, by an old castle, on the grass by a black grille, he bent over a young man without memory, lying with open eyes and smiling, and said quietly, to himself:

"Again."

And in the breast pocket of his uniform, beside the wooden raven that stole the sun, and the paper crane that must be folded back into a sheet, all this time, half a century, lay an old silver medallion. And in it, beside the faded portrait of a girl with blue eyes, a small, dried, blue, botanically impossible flower.

Chicory.

She who waits by the road.
