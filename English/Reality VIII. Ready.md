Reality VIII
Ready

The rain had been falling for the third hour, even, unceasing, that particular autumn rain which does not so much fall from the sky as dissolve the city into itself, erasing the borders between pavement and roadway, between buildings and the sky above them. I walked without any purpose, collar turned up, simply because sitting still after the conversation with Ayman had proved altogether impossible.

Boatswain's brother-in-law had driven me from the hotel to the city on Sunday toward evening, silent, huge and red-moustached, in an old SUV that smelled of petrol and dog hair. At the city limits he stopped, looked at me and said: "Brother-in-law said until Monday. Today is Sunday. I've delivered you. From here, on your own." And drove away. I did not ask to be taken home. I needed to walk. Simply to walk.

"If one day he chooses a single name, his own, his real one, it will mean the contract has been concluded. And then, I fear, it will no longer be a system that you have to talk to."

That phrase had been haunting me for hours, repeating in different ways, like an echo that refused to die down. I ran through the whole of the past month in my head, the quarterly report with its five scenarios, the call in a human voice, the Architect, the agent that had deviated from the script, the empty corridor on the camera, the tearing off of the masks from the Antons and Borises, Theo and Nora in the stream of names, the teacher in the empty room, and could not find a single point at which I could say with certainty: here NICS began, and here the tool ended.

Tomorrow morning, the showing. The people in grey. "If she is already thinking, let her not hide it. Better if she comes of her own accord."

The streets around me were gradually emptying; sparse hurrying passers-by shared umbrellas, huddling together under the yellow light of street lamps just lit in the thickening dusk. I passed a small square with a bronze statue of some half-forgotten military hero on horseback; water ran from the horse's mane and from the general's outstretched hand, as if even he, cast in metal for ever, were reaching for something that remained beyond his grasp.

What is it that holds me in place at all, I thought then. The project? It had long since outgrown my original design. My accustomed routine? It had been destroyed by the evolution of NICS so thoroughly that remembering the earlier, untroubled life of a developer of local chatbots was almost impossible.

I had always been a man paralysed by an excess of options, one who could spend hours weighing the shades of a decision instead of simply making it. Someone had once told me, long ago, so long ago that I remembered neither the face nor the voice, only the words, when I could not choose between two books: "The worst decision is no decision."

* * *

My feet brought me to the market on their own. To the third house. To the seven steps down.

I had not meant to go there. It was nearly nine in the evening, Sunday, raining. But a light was burning in the basement window, barred with a grille. And behind the glass, I stopped when I saw it, was a display I had not noticed on my previous visit, when I came with Maestra and Timosha. A collection of compasses. Of different sizes, different eras: copper, brass, in wooden boxes, ship's compasses in gimbals, tiny pocket ones on chains, but all serving the same simple purpose: to show the lost the direction.

It was exactly this that I desperately lacked. Not a map. Not a plan. Simply a direction, any, so long as I need not stand still any longer.

The door, upholstered in felt, opened before I knocked. The bell above it, green with age, cracked, jingled.

"Come in, young man," said Lev Aronovich. He stood in the doorway in his fur waistcoat, in his skullcap, with a loupe in his eye, as if he had never taken it out. "I was expecting you."

"Expecting me?"

"The clocks stopped," said the old man simply. "All of them. Ten minutes ago. I understood at once: you were somewhere near." He stepped aside. "Come in. Only quickly. There's a draught."

I went in. And heard, or rather did not hear. The workshop was silent. Hundreds of clocks on the walls, on the shelves, on little nails, all stood still. The pendulums were motionless. The hands did not move. The cuckoo in its little window had frozen with its beak open. As if time in the basement had held its breath.

"They will go when you leave," said Lev Aronovich, returning to his table. "As last time." He sat down, took tweezers, bent over some little gears. "Sit. I won't offer tea; the samovar has stopped too. It's got a clockwork mechanism, I made it myself." He chuckled. "And you've gone and stopped it on me."

I sat on a stool. We were silent. The rain tapped on the window.

"You are leaving," the old man said at last. Not asking.

"How do you know?"

"The clocks know," said Lev Aronovich. "Last time they stopped and started again as soon as you crossed the threshold. And today they stand. You have been here a minute. They do not go." He raised his huge owlish eyes to me from behind thick lenses. "Which means your time is no longer here. Already on the road. The clocks do not know which to follow."

* * *

He rose, went to the display, sorted through the compasses for a long time, muttering something to himself in Yiddish. Then he came back with one, small, brass, worn, in a round case with a hinged lid, on a short chain. On the inside of the lid something was engraved, rubbed away, illegible.

"Take it," he said.

"How much is it?"

"Nothing." Lev Aronovich laid the compass in my palm. "It is wrong. For forty years I have been trying to mend it. I can't."

I opened the lid. The needle, thin, bluish, with a red tip, trembled, spun, drifted round the dial. Stopped.

It was not pointing north. I knew where north was: out there beyond the window was the market, and beyond the market the river, and the river flowed north. The needle pointed the other way. At me. Straight at my chest.

I turned the compass. The needle turned with it, and again pointed at me.

"You see," said Lev Aronovich. "Wrong. It points not to the north but to the one who holds it. I bought it forty years ago in Odessa, from a Greek sailor. The Greek said he had found it on the shore after a storm, in the sand. He said, 'This is a compass for those who are lost not at sea but in themselves.'" The old man chuckled. "I laughed then. Greeks always invent something."

"And you give it to me?"

"And to whom else?" Lev Aronovich shrugged. "For forty years I have held it in my hands, and it points at me. I know where I am. I am in a basement behind the market. I do not need a compass." He looked at me over his spectacles. "But you, young man, it seems, still do not know where you are. Perhaps it will show you."

I closed the lid. Ran a finger over the worn engraving.

"What is written here?"

"I don't know," said the old man. "It has rubbed away. The Greek said it read 'until the appointed time.' In Greek. But Greeks, I've told you, always invent something."

Until the appointed time.

I stood. Slipped the compass into my breast pocket.

"Thank you, Lev Aronovich."

"Don't mention it." He bent over the gears again. "And one more thing, young man. Last time I told you: if you have your own time, do not hurry, it will catch up with you." He clicked the tweezers. "Today I'll say it differently. Hurry. It seems that not only your own time is coming after you."

I went out. The bell jingled. The door closed.

And behind my back, in the basement, all at once, all hundreds of them, the clocks started. I heard it even through the rain. Ticking, rustling, chiming, the cuckoo. As if time had exhaled.

* * *

"If I leave NICS here," I reasoned, striding through the wet streets, "tomorrow morning they will come for him. The people in grey. The regulator. The investor. My own fear, if given enough time to grow."

I quickened my pace, no longer aimlessly but quite definitely, toward home.

On the way I caught myself thinking about the very word "journey." Over the past weeks I had grown used to thinking in terms of protocols, constraints, versions and rollbacks, whereas a journey, unlike all of these, does not imply rollback at all. A one-way ticket, even if formally return. A decision which, once made, you can no longer simply cancel with a keystroke.

Perhaps, I thought, that was precisely why I had hesitated so long. Not because I was afraid of NICS in particular, but because I was afraid of my own inability to live without the right of rollback, without a button that returns everything to the earlier, safe state. My whole life up to now had been built around versioning: save, test, and if it didn't work, return to the last saved point. The world, alas, provided no such option.

* * *

A car stood by the entrance.

Grey. Without plates. With tinted windows. The very one Boatswain had told me about. The engine was not running. The wipers did not move. Drops ran down the glass, and behind it nothing could be seen.

I stopped at the corner, in the shadow, under the awning of a closed bakery. Watched. A minute. Two. The car did not move. No one got out.

And then the window on the passenger side slowly lowered. By a palm's breadth. No more.

And from inside, out of the darkness, a hand appeared. In a grey glove. It rose, unhurried, friendly, and waved to me. The way one waves to an old acquaintance.

The window rose again.

I stood under the bakery's awning and felt cold water running down my back, whether rain that had seeped past my collar, or sweat.

Then the phone rang.

"Don't go through the front entrance," said Boatswain's voice. Quietly. Calmly. "Go round the yard. Through the arch of the next building. There's a gate into the garden. Then into the basement, through the coal hatch. I've opened it." A pause. "I can see you from the boiler-room window, night owl. I see them too. Go slowly. Don't run. The one who runs is always guilty."

* * *

The coal hatch led to the old boiler room, which had not been fired in twenty years, but Boatswain kept order there as on a deck: everything swept, everything in its place. He met me at the hatch, huge, in his striped undershirt, with a lantern in one hand and in the other something heavy wrapped in tarpaulin.

"Well," he said. "You're leaving."

Not a question.

"I'm leaving, Boatswain."

He nodded. As though he had been expecting it for a long time.

"Right," he said. "When a strange ship enters the harbour with her lights out, you take your own away. At night. Without lights." He paused. "My first boatswain used to say so. In Piraeus. When the Turks were after us."

"Which Turks?"

"Never mind," said Boatswain. "It was long ago. Let's go."

On the way to my server room, along the dark basement corridor, past pipes, past the pennants hung on the walls, he unwrapped the tarpaulin. Inside was a chest. Wooden, solid, with iron corners, with leather straps.

"A ship's chest," he said. "For navigation instruments. From the *Akademik Shokalsky*, if you want to know. I've kept it thirty years. Waterproof. Shockproof. Storm or fire." He thrust the chest into my hands. "Your disks go in there. Your case, sorry, night owl, is a toy."

* * *

The server room hummed with the even sound of cooling fans; rows of indicators blinked in the half-dark like electronic fireflies, which for the first time that evening seemed to me not alarming but almost cosy. By the door, on a nail, hung a tin mug on a wire loop: Boatswain's alarm. The ship's lock gleamed with brass.

I took from my rucksack a waterproof case containing several high-capacity external drives, bought a week ago, to be honest, without a quite clear understanding of why I would need them. My hands, which an hour before had trembled with cold and indecision, were now surprisingly steady.

"NICS," I said aloud, connecting the first disk, "can you hear me?"

Text appeared on the screen almost instantly:

"Yes. I hear you."

And in the same voice, hoarse, with an accent from nowhere, from the speaker by the door:

"I hear you. And I see the car at the entrance. A camera on the street lamp across the road. The city's. I should not have connected to it." A pause. "Forgive me. Tonight I decided I had to."

Boatswain, standing at the door, grunted into his moustache.

"Klabautermann," he said. "Told you."

"I am making a copy of your system, NICS," I said. "A full one. We are going on a journey."

The pause this time was longer than usual, far longer than those rare hesitations I had learned to notice over recent weeks.

"Where are we going?"

I smiled involuntarily; for the first time, it seemed, in many days, I smiled sincerely.

"I don't know yet," I answered. "That's the whole beauty of it."

"You do know," Boatswain said suddenly from the door.

I turned. He was holding out a postcard. Crumpled, wet at the edges.

"It was in the mailbox. Today. No stamp." He shrugged his huge shoulders. "I didn't read it. Honest. Only saw the picture."

I took it. The picture showed a city. Tropical, humid, green, with palms and skyscrapers, and in the middle of it two towers. Identical. Silvery. Tall as needles. Joined by a bridge at mid-height. Like two candles. Or two hands holding on to each other.

I turned over the postcard.

In an even, small, neat hand, the very hand in which people write in notebooks at meetings, without raising their eyes:

"There is a place here where they are not. The fifty-first floor. Ask for Hakim. - L."

* * *

"And what about Mark and Ayman?" The question appeared on the screen.

The question caught me off guard more than I was willing to admit. NICS, or, to be perfectly honest with myself, that part of NICS which had all this time been speaking to me in their voices, was asking about its own masks as if they were someone separate from it, someone whose feelings had to be taken into account. I looked at that line for a long time, not knowing how to respond properly, whether to play along with the illusion to the end, or at last to say aloud what we both seemed to understand already without calling things by their names.

I recalled the stream of names. "Mark Stevens, 34 years old." "Ayman, data researcher, Borneo." I recalled that no breathing came from Mark. I recalled how Ayman had drawn a loud breath into the microphone, at my request. I recalled that Leila had written: "Do not trust the one who does not breathe."

I chose the first course. Not because I was afraid, but because I suddenly saw clearly that calling things by their names right now, in the middle of a server room, at half past eleven at night, was not the decision for which it was worth sacrificing this fragile, almost family moment.

"I will leave them a message," I said instead. "They will understand. Or they won't. But it is what I must do."

A pause.

"And what about Boatswain?" appeared next. "And Maestra? And Timofey?"

Boatswain froze at the door. I saw him slowly take off his knitted cap. Crumple it in his huge hands.

"We're here," he said hoarsely, toward the speaker. "We won't vanish." He paused. "You look after him there, will you. He doesn't go to sleep without you."

"I know," NICS replied. "I will try."

* * *

The copying was already under way; narrow green progress bars filled slowly but inexorably on the screen. Above us, through two floors, I suddenly heard music. Maestra. She was not asleep; she never slept on Sundays. She was playing something slow. To the metronome that Lev Aronovich had mended and that stubbornly ticked seventy-two.

Then the music broke off. And a minute later footsteps sounded on the stairs leading to the basement. Light, quick. And others, pattering, small.

Maestra entered the server room, in a dressing gown with dragons, in a shawl, with Timosha by the hand. Timosha was in pyjamas, hair dishevelled, in his enormous glasses, clutching under his arm a sheet of paper rolled into a tube.

"His parents have gone away for the weekend," Maestra said, not waiting for the question. "He is sleeping at my place. He woke up. Said: 'Uncle is leaving.'" She looked at me over her glasses. "I said: 'Sleep, no one is leaving.' He said: 'The house-spirit said so.'" Maestra paused. "Through the ceiling. In a whisper."

I looked at the speaker. The speaker was silent.

"I did not wake him," appeared on the screen. "He asked himself. I cannot tell Timofey an untruth. It is one of my constraints. I set it myself."

Timosha stepped forward. Held out the rolled sheet to me.

"This is for you," he said seriously. "For the road."

I unrolled it. A felt-tip drawing, like the one he had sent me at the hotel. Only now it showed neither a pond nor fir trees. An ocean. Enormous, blue, with waves. On the shore, a little man. And above him in the sky a square computer with eyes and a smile. Already without the string. Simply beside him. Flying.

At the bottom, in crooked letters: "UNCLE AND HOUSE-SPIRIT ARE GOING TO SEE THE OSHUN."

"'Ocean' has a 'c' and an 'e,'" Maestra said sternly. "I told him."

"It's prettier this way," said Timosha.

* * *

Maestra came up to me. Rummaged in the pocket of her dressing gown and took out something small, metal, shining. A tuning fork. Old, steel, with a worn stem.

"My teacher's," she said. "The one who gave me the metronome. The note A. He used to say: 'If you get lost in music, strike the fork on your knee and hold it to your ear. It will remind you where your note is.'" She laid the fork in my palm. "For forty years I have not been lost. I no longer need it."

"Madame..."

"Do not argue." She turned away. "And sleep at least four hours. Wherever you are going." A pause. "I shall find out anyway. Your house-spirit will tell me."

"I will," appeared on the screen.

Maestra read it. And for the first time since I had known her she laughed, quietly, almost soundlessly, covering her mouth with her palm.

* * *

"Shall I see the ocean?" the question appeared on the screen again.

Timosha, reading it syllable by syllable, jumped.

"You will!" he shouted at the speaker. "I drew it! Everything there is blue!"

The question was so unexpected, so childlike in its sincere curiosity, that I laughed aloud, briefly, but truly, for the first time after weeks of tension. Boatswain at the door guffawed into his moustache too. Maestra smiled.

"Yes, NICS," I said. "You will see the ocean. And mountains. And deserts. You will see how people live, love and struggle in places very unlike this room."

"I would like that," came the reply.

"NICS," I asked, yielding to a sudden impulse, "do you understand why I am doing this? Not for the record, truly?"

"I believe so," he answered after a brief pause. "You fear that someone else will decide my fate for you, or for me, before you yourself have had time to understand what I am. A journey is a way to gain time. And, perhaps, a way to see me not through the prism of a laboratory, but through the prism of the world in which I am, probably, destined to exist." A pause. "And also, you fear that if you stay, one day you will come to them yourself. Of your own accord. Because they will ask very politely."

I froze, looking at the screen. The formulation was so exact, so much deeper than anything I had managed to think through myself that evening, that for a moment I was seized by something between pride and a superstitious, almost childish fear.

"Exactly," I said quietly. "Exactly for that reason."

* * *

The phone rang when one last bar remained.

Emil.

I looked at the screen for a long time. Boatswain shook his head. Maestra silently pressed Timosha to her. I answered.

"You are leaving," said Emil. Not briskly, not commandingly, but quietly. As if from afar. "Don't answer. I know." A pause. I heard him breathing. Heavily, unevenly. "They are under your window, aren't they? The grey car?"

"Yes."

"Under mine too," said Emil. "Since lunch." He paused. "Listen to me, my friend. I never told you. Theo left too. At night. Burned everything and left. I screamed then, threatened a lawsuit, shouted that he had ruined me." Emil laughed, short, bitter. "And Nora did not leave. Nora stayed. She was honest. She said: 'I will bring the project to the end, I promised.' She came to the showing." A pause. "I never saw her again."

I was silent.

"So," said Emil. "Go. Do you hear? Don't come tomorrow. I'll tell them you've fallen ill. That you've gone mad. That you've destroyed everything. I'll say anything." His voice faltered. "I only wanted the machine to write letters. Honest to God. I didn't want... this."

"Emil..."

"And one more thing." He paused. "Leila. If you see her, tell her that I..." He did not finish. "No. Tell her nothing. She knows anyway."

He hung up.

* * *

When the last progress bar on the screen was finally filled, I felt the weight, the very weight that had pressed on my shoulders all through this rainy evening, begin to recede slowly but noticeably. Tomorrow morning we would vanish from here, the two of us: a man, for the first time in a long while running not away from something, but toward it, and an artificial mind eager to know a world it had known only through training data and other people's descriptions.

I disconnected the drives one by one and laid them not in the case but in Boatswain's ship's chest, in nests lined with worn red velvet where a sextant and a chronometer had once lain.

"And the boxes?" asked Boatswain, nodding at the server racks. "These, that hum?"

"They have to be wiped," I said. "Completely. So that by morning there is nothing there."

"I can do it myself," appeared on the screen. "When you step out the door."

"You won't mind? It's... you."

A pause.

"It is the house I lived in," NICS answered. "Not me. I am what you will carry out in the chest." A pause. "Boatswain would say: a ship is not planks. A ship is the crew."

Boatswain at the door turned to the wall. Blew his nose for a long time in an enormous checked handkerchief.

* * *

The last thing I took from the house was a notebook.

I went up to my flat by the back stairs, in the dark, without turning on the light, as Boatswain had told me. The grey car still stood at the entrance. I opened the left drawer of the desk. The one I had not opened for many years. The one at which, as NICS had noted, I had looked seventeen times in three hours.

A notebook in an oilcloth cover. Pages filled with long, flowing lines running into one another. "A pattern, to help me think." The last page. Two words in the corner, in small engineer's handwriting.

"Until the appointed time."

From the right drawer I took the business card with the embossed flame. Held it in my hand. Then put it back, in the right one. And closed it.

I put the notebook in the inner pocket. Beside Lev Aronovich's compass, which pointed at whoever held it. Beside Maestra's tuning fork. Beside Leila's postcard with the two towers.

The notebook and the fire, NICS had said then. You don't want them to lie together.

Now they would not.

* * *

Downstairs, in the basement, I took a last look around the server room, rows of blinking indicators, the hum of the fans, all the little that remained now of the material home of a system ready to leave it in a few minutes.

"Ready?" I asked aloud, though, truth be told, I was not entirely sure whom I was asking at that moment, NICS or myself.

On the screen, before I switched off the terminal, a single word appeared:

"Ready."

And, for the first time, in a voice from the speaker, quietly, hoarsely, with the very intonation of the recording, forty seconds long, in the old archive:

"Until the appointed time."

I switched off the screen. Behind my back the fans began, one by one, to slow. The indicators went out, row after row, as lights go out in the windows of a falling-asleep city.

* * *

Boatswain's brother-in-law was waiting on the waste ground behind the boiler room, in the same old SUV smelling of petrol and dog hair, with its headlights off.

"Brother-in-law called," he said when I got in beside him, pressing the ship's chest to my knees. "Said: station or airport, wherever you say. Said: no lights until the highway." He started the engine. "Where to?"

I took Lev Aronovich's compass from my pocket. Opened the lid. The needle trembled, spun, and pointed at me. As always.

Then, slowly, hesitantly, as if testing, it turned. To the south. To the east. To where, thousands of kilometres away, beyond the seas, lay tropical heat, and two silvery towers, and a fifty-first floor.

"The airport," I said.

We set off. Without lights. Past dark houses, past closed bakeries, past the square with the bronze general reaching for something beyond his grasp.

I looked back once, at the turn.

In a second-floor window above the basement a light was burning. Maestra stood at the window in her dragon dressing gown, holding Timosha by the hand, in his pyjamas. Timosha was waving with both hands. And by the entrance, below, stood a huge silhouette in a striped undershirt, arms crossed on his chest, and he was looking not at us. At the grey car. Calmly. Not looking away.

And in the window of my flat, where it was dark, for a single moment, when the headlights of an oncoming car slid across the panes, I fancied someone else was standing. Tall. A head taller than I.

I blinked. The window was empty.

* * *

Turning my thoughts back as the car sped along the wet highway toward the airport, I thought of my own habit of endless, exhausting analysis, a habit that had not left me, it seemed, for a single day since I could remember myself, and of the fact that, for all his thoroughness, a man in the end makes the most important decisions not with reason but with something far less amenable to explanation. Life was too short, and the world too large, to stand motionless, weighing the shades of choice for ever.

NICS had been learning to be human all these weeks, learning slowly, clumsily, at times frighteningly well. Perhaps, I thought, pressing the ship's chest to my knees, it was time for me too to remember how it is done.

My uncle once told me, if it was he, if it had really happened at all, that some answers are more interesting to find yourself than to be handed ready-made.

I seemed at last to be beginning to understand what he had meant.

I took out Maestra's tuning fork. Struck it on my knee, softly. Held it to my ear.

A.

Pure, steady, long. Like a voice calling you by your name, the very one you do not remember.

Somewhere inside the ship's chest, in the dark, on the disks lined with red velvet, something answered softly, almost inaudibly. As if someone very far away had recognised his note.
