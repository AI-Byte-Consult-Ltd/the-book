Chapter XXXVII
The New Moon

There was no moon.

I went out onto the palace roof an hour before dawn, for the last time, as I thought, before the long road south should take me from these roofs, these palms, this city, and looked at the sky. The stars burned as brightly as they burn only on moonless nights: Sah strode in the west, lowering his belt toward the horizon, the Imperishable circled in the north, and above the eastern hills, low, almost touching them, shone Sopdet, white, shimmering, winking. The star of Isis. The one that always walks a step behind.

But there was no moon. None at all. Only darkness in the place where it should be, as though someone had taken it from the sky as an eye is taken out.

The new moon. The Eye that was once shattered had gone to gather itself anew. So the priests said. So Amenemope said. So Merit said by the pool.

"My Pharaoh," said a voice behind me.

Senusret. Of course. He was always at my back: on the roof, at the wicket, at the gates, at the hole in the tomb. I had ceased to be surprised.

"Seti has sent a messenger," he said. "In the night. He bade me tell you as soon as you wake."

"I have not slept."

"I know, my Pharaoh. That is why I came now." Senusret paused. "Seti says: the water has risen."

I turned.

"Risen?"

"By a finger," said Senusret. "One finger. Overnight." He paused. "For the first time in three years. Seti says he went down to the nilometer three times with a lamp. Three times he measured. A finger above yesterday." The old warrior looked at me, and in his stony face there was something I had never seen there before. Hope. "Seti bade me add: 'Tell the son of Ra, the river heard that he is leaving. And awoke to see him off.'"

* * *

The nilometer stood at the very edge of the water, at the southern tip of the island opposite the palace, a stone well going down to the river, with a stair winding along the wall and with marks on the wall, notches, signs, numbers. For forty floods Seti had descended this stair each morning. For forty floods he had recorded how far the water had risen.

He waited for me below, by the water itself, small, stooped, withered, with a lamp in his hand, and, seeing me on the steps, did not bow, but only raised the lamp, lighting the wall.

"Here, my Pharaoh," he said. His voice shook. "Look."

On the wet stone, a little above the dark, motionless water, was a fresh scratch. Seti had scored it in the night with a knife. And a finger's breadth below, yesterday's.

"A finger," the old man whispered. "Only a finger." He looked at me, and I saw that his eyes were wet. "My Pharaoh, do you know what a finger is, after three years of nothing? Three years I came down here and saw the same thing. Every morning. The same." He swallowed. "And today, a finger."

"It is not yet the flood, Seti."

"Not the flood," the old man agreed. "Not even the beginning of the flood. It is..." He searched for the word. "It is as if a sleeper in his dream had moved a finger. Not yet awake. But no longer dead."

He lowered the lamp over the water. Dark, heavy, motionless. The lamp's reflection trembled on it in a yellow patch.

"Nefer-Ka came last evening," Seti said suddenly. Quietly. Not raising his eyes. "As at the beginning of the drought. Before your messenger. Asked what the nilometer shows. I said, nothing." The old man paused. "That was yesterday. Yesterday it was nothing." He raised his eyes to me. "And today he did not come. Today I shall tell him nothing. Today, only you."

"Why, Seti?"

The keeper of the nilometer was silent for a long time.

"Because for forty floods, my Pharaoh," he said at last, "I have recorded what the river does. And never, not once, has the river risen at the new moon. Never. The water comes when Sopdet rises. Not before." He paused. "And today it rose. By itself. Without Sopdet. On the night when there is no moon. On the night when you leave for the south." The old man set the lamp on a step. "The High Priest will say: it is a sign that the god is pleased. That the festival is welcome. That we must sail." He looked at me. "But I am an old man who has measured water for forty years. And I think: a river does not see one off. A river warns."

* * *

Djehuty's messenger arrived when the sun was already above the hills.

He came on foot from the southern outpost, forty days' journey, because his horse had fallen at Abydos and boats did not run on the shallowed river. He was so thin he seemed a skeleton covered with sunburnt skin. He fell on his knees at my feet in the courtyard, by the very steps, and could not speak; he only held out to me something wrapped in dirty cloth.

Senusret caught him, Bata thrust a flask at him. The messenger drank long, choking. Then gasped:

"The Rock... of the First... Djehuty bade... only you... into your hands..."

I unwrapped the cloth.

A little box. Clay, small, palm-sized, sealed with ancient clay, so ancient that the seal crumbled at my touch. Dark, fired, with signs scratched on its sides. The script.

"We found it under the rock," the messenger wheezed. "Where the men in temple sandals were digging. Djehuty set a guard. At night the temple men came again. We drove them off. And in the morning..." He swallowed. "In the morning we found a pit. Deep. They had almost dug through. One night more and they would have reached it. Djehuty himself went down. Took this. Bade me run. Day and night." The messenger closed his eyes. "I ran."

I opened the box.

Inside was a sheet. Not papyrus. Something thinner, smoother, whiter; I had never seen such material. Folded in two. I unfolded it.

On the sheet, two lines. The upper, the script. Long, flowing, running together. The lower, signs. Ours. Ancient, angular, the same as above the bench in the round room beneath the tomb.

"Iti," I said. Without turning.

He was already standing beside me. As always when he was needed. He bent over the sheet. His lips began to move.

"'He who holds both,'" he read slowly, in a whisper, "'let him not enter with them...'" He faltered. Leaned closer. "'...into the southern house. Until there comes...'" He raised his eyes to me. "The rest is erased, my Pharaoh. The clay has crumbled. One word." He pointed. "The last sign. I do not know it. It looks like... like a man. Seated. With a raised hand."

"'Let him not enter with them into the southern house,'" I repeated. "Luxor. The southern house of the god."

"Yes, my Pharaoh," Iti whispered. "'Until there comes...'" He gazed at the last sign. "Who?"

No one answered.

* * *

"Nefer-Ka will give you the stones at Luxor," said Paser.

The council had gathered in the small hall, for the last time before the sailing. Paser with his staff. Ptahhotep with his basket of scrolls. Nebamun with his tablets. Merira with tattooed arms folded on his chest. Panehsy by the wall. Senusret by the door. Iti in the shadow, with his brush. Deaf Ani on the bench in the corner. And Khaemuas.

Khaemuas sat among the commanders, in his bracelets, nine, not ten, perfumed, with painted eyes, but he did not smile. He was looking at the floor. When I entered he raised his eyes to me, quickly, anxiously, and lowered them again.

"The casket of black wood," Paser continued, "has already been loaded onto the High Priest's barque. I saw it. Yesterday evening. Four priests carried it. On a litter. Under a cover." The old man paused. "At Luxor, on the last day, in the inner court, he will open the casket and hand you the Two Eyes. 'As the First Ones gave them.' So he said at the banquet. Before all."

"And the letter from the Rock says, do not enter the southern house with them," said Senusret.

"The letter from the Rock says, until someone comes," Paser corrected. "Who, is erased."

"Then we must wait," said Merira in his thunderous voice. "Not enter. Until he comes. My barques can sail slowly, my Pharaoh. Very slowly. The river is shallow. We can run aground. Once. Twice. Ten times." He grinned. "For thirty years I have been running barques aground. I can do it so that no one will understand whether it is on purpose or not."

"Shall I repeat that for the record?" Ani whispered loudly.

"Merira knows how to run barques aground, honoured Ani," Ptahhotep said patiently.

"Ah!" Ani nodded and scratched with his brush. "Wise. Very wise. I can too. In my youth I ran aground a beauty from Bubastis so thoroughly that to this day she cannot understand how she came to marry me."

Merira roared with laughter. Paser smiled into his beard. Even Nebamun allowed himself a dry smile.

And Khaemuas, Khaemuas suddenly raised his head.

"My Pharaoh," he said. His voice trembled. "Permit me."

All fell silent.

"Last night," said Khaemuas, looking at me, "Bakenren's man came to me. In secret. As he always came." He swallowed. "He passed word: Bakenren is alive. He is in Luxor. Already three weeks. He waits."

"For what does he wait?"

"He did not say, my Pharaoh." Khaemuas clasped his hands so hard that the bracelets chimed. "He said only: 'Our men must stand at the gates. Inside, all is ready. The masons have done what was needed.'" Khaemuas paused. "I asked, what masons? He laughed and said: 'Those who know how to lay stone so that no one finds the door.'"

In the hall it became very quiet.

"Masons," Nebamun repeated slowly. "My Pharaoh. Two moons ago six masons vanished from the royal workshops in Memphis. Masters. The best. Those who set secret passages in tombs." The keeper of the treasury looked at his tablets. "I thought they had run away from hunger. I struck their ration from the books." He raised his eyes. "Now I think they did not run away."

* * *

Pentu came after the council. Through the garden, at night, no, by day, but through the garden, as bidden, past blind Meru, who, they say, let him pass without a riddle and only said: "Today the riddle is at the river."

He was pale as flax. In his hands, a scroll. A copy. The last.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered, falling to his knees. "This is an inventory. Not of granaries. Of works." He unrolled the scroll with trembling hands. "The temple of Luxor. Works paid for by the Temple in the last three moons." He stabbed a finger at a line. "Here. 'The inner court of the god. The eastern wall. A secret niche. Six masters from Memphis. Stone from the old quarry. Payment, one hundred hekats of barley.'" Pentu raised his eyes to me. "My Pharaoh, this is not a repair. This is a niche. In the wall. Behind the columns."

"For what?"

"It is not written, my Pharaoh." Pentu's voice broke. "But at the end there is a note. In Bakenren's hand. I know his writing. 'To close it, when he enters.'"

I looked at that line for a long time. "To close it, when he enters."

Columns. Darkness. A hand rising in the dark. And then nothing. Darkness. As though someone had shut a door.

Or walled it up with stone.

"Thank you, Pentu," I said. "Go. And do not come again. It is dangerous."

"My Pharaoh," Pentu whispered, not rising from his knees. "My sisters. The field near Bubastis. You said it was transferred to the royal lands." He swallowed. "Is that true?"

"It is true, Pentu."

The young man covered his face with his hands. His shoulders shook.

"Then I shall go with you," he said through his palms. "To Luxor. As a scribe. A temple one. They take me, Bakenren ordered it, before he vanished." He raised his face, wet with tears. "I shall be near the niche, my Pharaoh. I shall see what they do." A pause. "I owe you. For the field. For my sisters. I owe."

* * *

Nanakhat was waiting for me by the river. In the same place as last time. On a flat stone at the very edge of the water. Facing south.

I came alone. Senusret stayed behind the palms, "looking for donkeys," as he said to Bata with a stone face. Bata snorted and got a clout on the back of the head.

The blind old woman did not turn when I approached. She sat leaning on her staff of white wood crowned with a lioness's head, and her white, sightless eyes were turned to the river.

"The water has risen," she said. Not asking.

"By a finger."

"By a finger," Nanakhat repeated. "The Eye has stirred. In its sleep." She paused. "Did you find what they sought on the Rock?"

"I found it." I sat beside her on the stone. Held out to her the sheet from the clay box. She did not take it. She could not see. But she passed her fingers through the air above it, slowly, tenderly, as one strokes a sleeping beast.

"'Let him not enter with them into the southern house,'" she said. Not reading. Knowing. "'Until there comes...'" She fell silent.

"Who, Nanakhat? The last word is erased."

The old woman was silent for a long time. The river lapped softly at our feet, shallow, dark, a finger higher than yesterday.

"Last time you asked me where two met," she said at last. "One walking to the sunrise, the other to the sunset. You answered, at home. Because the world is a circle." She turned to me her carved, ancient face. "I said: that is half the answer. The second you will speak when you return from Luxor."

"I remember."

"Here is the second half," said Nanakhat. "Those two are one." She paused. "The one who left, and the one who returned. The one who holds both Eyes, and the one who brought them. The one who will enter the southern house, and the one who must come before he enters." She stretched out a hand and touched my face with dry, hot, rough fingers. "The last word on the sheet is you. Not this you. Another. The one who will come."

"I do not understand."

"I know," said Nanakhat. "And you will not understand. Until you meet him." She lowered her hand. "At Luxor. Or after. Or in another place where there is no Luxor. It does not matter. What matters is not to enter the southern house with the Eyes before he has come."

"And if Nefer-Ka compels it?"

The old woman laughed, drily, toothlessly, as stone cracks in the sun.

"Nefer-Ka cannot compel anyone, son of Ra," she said. "He can only ask. Very courteously. Very beautifully. So that one cannot refuse." She paused. "Therefore, refuse."

* * *

She took something from her neck, a cord of goat's wool on which hung a little figure. A lioness. Of black stone. Polished to a shine by thousands of touches.

"Take it," she said. "It is from me. And from her." She nodded toward the south. "From her who went away once and came back. Let her go with you. If it is dark for you, she will see."

I took it. The figure was warm.

"And you, Nanakhat? Will you sail with us?"

"No," said the old woman. "I shall go home. South. On foot. Along the river." She rose, leaning on her staff. "I am a hundred years old, son of Ra. I wish to die at home." She paused. "We shall meet again. Not in this life. In another. You will be in another body. I too. But you will know me." She smiled. "By the staff."

And she walked away along the bank, tapping with her lioness staff, south, small, bent, ancient, past the palms behind which stood Senusret, "looking for donkeys."

Drawing level with him, she stopped again. Again turned her blind face to him. And said something.

This time Senusret did not drop his staff. He listened. Bowed his head. And stood so a long time, motionless, after she had gone.

"What did she say?" I asked, coming up.

The commander of the army raised his eyes to me.

"She said," he answered dully, "'You will remain at the gates. So it must be. But when the gates are shut, do not break them. Go to the river. The river will show where the other door is.'"

* * *

Iti I found in the Library.

He sat at the great table where we had read the Epic, alone, by lamplight, copying something. Fast. Not raising his head. Beside him a heap of scrolls. Tablets. Potsherds.

Seeing me he leapt up. Overturned the inkpot. The ink spread across the table in a black pool.

"My Pharaoh... I did not expect..."

"Iti," I said. "You are staying."

He froze. In mid-movement, with a rag in his hand with which he had been trying to wipe the ink.

"Staying?" he repeated.

"Here. In the capital. In the Library." I came closer. "You are needed here. The seventh scroll. The bilingual texts. Three words: keep, appointed time, house. You must find more. While I am gone." I paused. "And if I do not return, someone must know what is written there."

Iti looked at me. The rag in his hand was black with ink. Ink dripped on the floor.

"You will return," he said. Quietly. Not a question.

"I do not know, Iti."

"You will return," he repeated. Stubbornly. And his voice shook. "You pulled me out of the water. Four years ago. You said: 'You almost drowned, and you are thinking of a tablet.'" He swallowed. "I remember every word. You do not. So you owe me."

"Owe you?"

"You owe it to return," said Iti. "To remember." He threw the rag on the table. Reached into his bosom. Took out something small, flat, wooden, darkened with time, with chipped edges. "Here."

A tablet. That one. From the hunt. The one the cat Mau fished out of the channel.

"I promised to show it when you remembered," said Iti. "But you are leaving. And I..." He faltered. "Take it. Read it there. At Luxor. If it is dark, read it." He laid it in my hands. "Everything is there. The hunt. The birds. The hippopotamus. The night on the island. Seneb. The stars." A pause. "And what I wrote at the end. Then. As a boy."

I looked at the tablet. Small, neat, childish signs. How many boats. How many birds. The hippopotamus that went off with the harpoon. The Imperishable stars.

And at the very end, a line. Which I had already seen. Then, on the sand, at dawn.

"Pharaoh pulled the junior scribe from the water. The scribe promises to remember this all his life. Even if Pharaoh forgets."

And beneath it, another line. Which had not been there then. Fresh. The ink still glistened.

"The scribe remembers. The scribe waits. Pharaoh will return."

I raised my eyes. Iti stood before me, with an ink stain on his cheek, of course, a fresh one, and looked straight. Without fear. Without shame. Without reverence.

I did not know what to say. I, Pharaoh, a living god, lord of the Two Lands, stood in the Library before a junior keeper of scrolls, holding in my hands a child's tablet, and could not find a single word.

I stretched out a hand. And wiped the ink stain from his cheek. With my thumb. Slowly.

Iti did not move. He only closed his eyes.

"I shall return, Iti," I said.

"I know, my Pharaoh," he whispered, not opening his eyes. "I have written it down."

* * *

Amenemope caught up with me at the Library exit, shuffling, leaning on his black staff, out of breath.

"My Pharaoh. One moment." He thrust into my hand something, a small scroll, tightly rolled, sealed with wax. "A copy. Of the window from the seventh scroll. Where our signs stand beside theirs. And also what the boy found in the round room. All that we know." The old man paused. "If at Luxor they show you something written in the script, compare. Perhaps you will find a fourth word."

"Thank you, Amenemope."

"Do not thank me, my Pharaoh." The old man looked at me with faded eyes. "The High Priest asked me again yesterday whether I was not tired. Whether I did not wish for rest." He smiled. "I answered that I shall be tired when you return. He smiled." Amenemope paused. "My Pharaoh. When you sail, I shall hide the boy. Isiret will help. In the lower storehouse, among the jars, there are places no one will find." He paused. "Because when you sail, it will be dangerous for the boy here. He knows too many words."

* * *

The barques of the god left at noon.

The whole capital came out onto the bank. From the palace to the temple, from the temple to the river, along the whole embankment, on roofs, on trees, on walls, thousands, tens of thousands of people. In white, in festive dress. With flowers. With palm fronds. With sistra and tambourines. They shouted, sang, wept.

At the quay stood the barque of the god, not the portable one but the river barque, huge, long, gilded from stem to stern, with rams' heads at bow and stern, with a canopy amidships under which stood the shrine. It was called Userhat, "Mighty of Brow." Thirty years ago, they said, it had sailed to Luxor on a full river under sail, and all Egypt came out to watch. Now the sail was furled. The river was shallow. The barque was towed.

On the bank, along the water, stood the haulers. Hundreds. Men stripped to the waist, with ropes on their shoulders, thick, tarred, long, fastened to the bow of the barque. They stood in a row, knee-deep in water, and waited.

Merira, on the prow of the barque, with tattooed arms, with a voice that overtopped the roar of the crowd, gave the commands.

"Haul-l-l!" he thundered.

The haulers began to sing. Low, steady, in one voice, an old song which, they said, had been sung on the river since the time of the First Ones. And leaned into the ropes. And the barque of the god, huge, golden, heavy, slowly, reluctantly, with a creak, moved from its place. And sailed. South.

* * *

Behind the barque of the god came the barque of the High Priest, a little smaller but equally gilded, with the casket of black wood under a cover at the stern. Nefer-Ka stood at the rail, in a leopard skin, with a staff crowned with the Eye, and looked at the crowd. When his barque came level with mine, he turned his head. And bowed, exactly as far as custom required.

"A good journey, my Pharaoh," he said. Not loudly. But I heard. "Luxor awaits you."

"I know, Nefer-Ka."

"The water has risen," he said. "By a finger. Have you heard?"

"I have heard."

"The gods are pleased," said Nefer-Ka. And smiled. "The gods are waiting for you to come to their southern house. As they have always waited."

And his barque passed on.

And behind it, mine. The royal one. With Senusret at the rail. With Paser, clutching the handrails with a green face. With Nebamun, drily counting the jars of beer for the haulers. With Khaemuas, at the stern, not raising his eyes. With Bata and Inu at my feet. With the dwarf Djedu, on the cabin roof, pulling faces at the crowd and shouting: "The son of Ra sails south! The son of Ra! Look! Whoever does not see will regret it!"

And behind mine, the barques of the singers. Khemu among them, in white, with a sistrum. When our barques drew level she raised the sistrum and lowered it. Once. A sign: "I am here."

And behind them the barques of the nobles, the barques of the priests, the barques of the warriors. Dozens. Hundreds. A flotilla stretched along the river so far that from the first barque the last could not be seen. Merira called it "the slowest fleet in the history of Egypt."

"We shall take forty days to Luxor, my Pharaoh," he told me that evening, grinning. "Perhaps fifty. If I try, sixty." He winked. "Shoals, my Pharaoh. Shoals are my best friends."

* * *

Nakht, the young bearer of the god's barque, with his protruding ears, found me at sunset.

He rowed up to my barque in a small boat, secretly, while the priests on his barque were at prayer, and clambered aboard, wet, trembling.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered. "Today. When the barque was being loaded. The shrine." He swallowed. "It was light."

"Light?"

"Usually it is heavy, my Pharaoh. There are twenty-four of us, and still it is heavy." Nakht looked at me with huge, frightened eyes. "And today, as if empty. As if the god is not there."

Cold beneath the ribs. On the left.

"Have you told anyone?"

"No, my Pharaoh. No one." Nakht paused. "But the other bearers felt it too. I saw it in their faces. All are silent." He swallowed. "The chief bearer said: 'The god has gone ahead. To wait for us at Luxor.'"

* * *

At night I did not sleep.

I stood at the rail of my barque, at the stern. The flotilla had halted for the night at the bank, the barques tied to palms, the haulers asleep on the sand by their fires. Silence. Only the lapping of water. Only the crackle of the fires. Only the cry of a night bird somewhere in the reeds.

There was no moon. The new moon. Darkness.

In my hands I held Iti's tablet. Nanakhat's lioness. Amenemope's scroll. The sheet from the Rock of the First Ones. Tausert's potsherd, the mouse in the crown between two doors.

All that had been given me. People who did not know one another, and all said one thing: "Take it. You are the one. Remember. Return."

Senusret stood beside me. Silent.

"Senusret," I said. "What else did Nanakhat tell you? You did not pass on all of it."

The commander of the army was silent for a long time.

"She said," he said at last, "'When he enters and the gates are shut, he will not die. He will go away. These are different things. You will not understand. But remember.'" Senusret paused. "And also: 'In a thousand years he will come again. In another city. Where two rivers flow into one. And you will be there. In another body. With another sword. Standing at other gates.'" He turned to me. "My Pharaoh, I am a soldier. I do not understand these words. I understand only one thing." His voice grew dull. "I shall stand at the gates. Here. And there. As long as needed."

I did not answer. I laid my hand on his shoulder. As he had laid his on mine.

We stood so for a long time.

* * *

And then Bata, dozing on the deck at my feet, suddenly cried out in his sleep, and woke. Sat up. Looked round. His face was white.

"My Pharaoh," he whispered. "The water."

I looked at the river.

Dark. Motionless. Without the moon, without reflections. Only the lights of the fires on the bank trembled on it in yellow patches.

And the reflection of our barque. Dark, inverted. The side. The mast. The cabin. The figures at the stern, mine, Senusret's, Bata's, seated on the deck.

And one more.

Tall. A head taller than any of us. Standing at the very rail, beside me. Motionless. Faceless.

I turned.

The deck. Senusret. Bata. I. No one else.

I looked into the water again.

The reflection was in place. Four figures. Senusret. Bata. I.

And he. Beside me. Very close. As if he had laid a hand on my shoulder, as I had just laid mine on Senusret's.

"My Pharaoh," Senusret whispered. He too was looking into the water. His hand fell on his sword. "There..."

"I see," I said.

We looked. For a long time. The reflection did not move.

And then, slowly, very slowly, the tall figure in the water raised a hand. Not to me. To the south. To where, forty days' journey away, beyond shoals and sands, beyond palms and villages, lay Luxor.

And lowered it.

And the reflection smoothed. Became only water. Dark. Motionless. A finger higher than yesterday.

* * *

Bata sat a long time on the deck, hugging his knees. Then said, quietly, hoarsely, looking at the water:

"My mother used to say, my Pharaoh. When the god of the river wishes to warn, he shows in the water what is not there." He paused. "I threw her scarab into a ditch then. Thought it all a deception." He swallowed. "And now I think, perhaps not all."

"What did he mean, Bata?"

The young guardsman looked at me.

"I do not know, my Pharaoh," he said. "Perhaps: go." A pause. "And perhaps: wait."

Above the eastern hills, low, at the very horizon, a white star flared. Sopdet. The star of Isis. The one that always walks a step behind.

And there was no moon.

The new moon.

To Luxor, forty days. If Merira tried, sixty.

And somewhere there, in the southern house of the god, behind the columns, in the eastern wall of the inner court, six vanished masons from Memphis had already built the niche. "To close it, when he enters."
