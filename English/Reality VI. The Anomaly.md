Reality VI
The Anomaly

2:47 a.m. Sleep will not come, however I close my eyes. Today turned out strange - and not in that habitual sense to which, it seems, I had begun little by little to grow accustomed in the past week.

Mark came back, as he had promised, but something in him had changed. He carried himself calmly. Confidently. As though he had seen something yesterday that had altered his very view of things. The scepticism that had always served him as a kind of calling card had evaporated almost without trace, as if it had never existed. Now Mark looked at NICS not as at a threat requiring immediate neutralization but as at something inevitable - something with which it is easier to come to terms beforehand than to resist to the last.

I confess I had expected rather the opposite - that yesterday's demonstration would only strengthen his alarm. It turned out otherwise. I put it down to fatigue, to the fact that a man who has lived too long in a state of defence sooner or later simply exhales and surrenders his positions.

But before Mark there was the morning. And I spent the morning in the basement, at the watchmaker's.

Maestra knocked at my door at eight - in a coat, in a little hat, with a parcel neatly wrapped in a waffle towel under her arm, and with Timosha, who held her by the hand and shone in new glasses with thick black frames.

"You know the way," Maestra said instead of a greeting. "Your house-spirit told Timosha. Behind the market, third house, the basement. I have lived there forty years and never knew there was a watchmaker." She paused. "I will not go alone. I am afraid he will say that the metronome cannot be mended. And in your presence he will be embarrassed."

"Why in my presence would he be embarrassed?"

"Because you are young and look as though you have not slept for a week," said Maestra. "In front of such people no one dares say 'cannot.' It is a pity."

* * *

The basement behind the market proved to be of the kind found only in old cities: seven steps down, a felt-padded door, a bell above it - copper, verdigrised, cracked - and beyond the door - time.

I do not know how to describe it otherwise. The whole space of the small, low, vaulted room, from floor to ceiling, was occupied by clocks. Wall clocks - with pendulums, with cuckoos, with Roman numerals, with gilded hands. Mantel clocks - under glass domes, with porcelain shepherdesses. Pocket watches - dozens, hundreds, on chains, hung on little nails along the walls like fish at a market. Alarm clocks. Chronometers in brass boxes such as stand on ships. And all of them were running. All ticking - each in its own way, loudly, softly, quickly, slowly - so that the room was filled with a continuous, rustling, living noise, like rain on a roof or the whisper of a vast crowd.

In the midst of this noise, at a table heaped with tiny gears, springs, screws, sat an old man. Small, dry, in a fur waistcoat over his shirt, in a yarmulke, with a white tufted beard and in spectacles with lenses so thick that the eyes behind them seemed huge as an owl's. In his eye, over the spectacles, he also wore a loupe.

"Close the door," he said without raising his head. "A draught. Clocks do not like draughts. They catch cold from them."

We entered. Timosha, mouth open, turned his head, trying to see all the clocks at once. Maestra stood by the door, pressing her parcel to her chest.

And then I crossed the threshold.

All the clocks in the room stopped.

At once. All. As if someone with a single movement had pulled the sound out of the room. Pendulums froze mid-swing. A cuckoo that had just popped out of its little window stopped with its beak open. The second hands on hundreds of pocket watches halted as though they had run into an invisible wall.

The silence was so complete that I heard my own heart beating.

Timosha gasped. Maestra pressed a hand to her chest.

The old man slowly raised his head. Took the loupe from his eye. Looked at me - for a long time, through his thick lenses, with huge owl eyes.

And then the clocks went again. All together. As if nothing had happened. Tick-tock, tick-tock, in a rustling rain across the room. The cuckoo cuckooed and hid.

"So," said the old man. Calmly. "Sit down, young man. On that stool. Only touch nothing."

* * *

Lev Aronovich - for it was he - seemed far less interested in Maestra's metronome than in me. He unwrapped the towel, looked at the cracked wooden case, the bent pendulum, the scattered little wheels, hmmed: "Made a hundred years ago, in Vienna, good work, I'll mend it in a week," set the parcel aside and stared at me again.

"Has it been long like this with you?" he asked.

"Like what?"

"The time around you," said Lev Aronovich, as though speaking of a head cold. "It stops."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You know." He tapped a finger on the table. "Sixty years I have been mending clocks, young man. Sixty. I began as a boy with my grandfather, in a shtetl where only the rabbi and the district police officer had clocks. I know why clocks stop. From dust. From damp. From a magnet. From a blow. From age." He paused. "And they also stop from people. Rarely. Very rarely. From people who have a time of their own."

"A time of their own?" squeaked Timosha.

"Their own," the old man nodded, not taking his eyes from me. "For all people there is one time. Common. Like a river. All swim in one river, in one direction, at one speed. And some have their own. They swim as though in another river, flowing alongside. Sometimes in the other direction. And when such a man enters a room with many clocks, the clocks do not know by which time to go. By the common one or by his. And for an instant - they stop. They think."

Maestra, standing at the door, quietly sat down on a crate.

"Have you seen this before?" I asked.

"Once," said Lev Aronovich. "Forty years ago. In Odessa. I had a workshop then on Deribasovskaya. A man came in. Young. Asked what time it was." The old man smiled into his beard. "All the clocks in the workshop stopped. I nearly died of fright. And he looked at them, sighed and said: 'Excuse me. I am again not in my own.' And left." Lev Aronovich paused. "He was like you. Not in face - I did not remember the face. Like you in the way he stood. As on a threshold. As though with one foot here and with the other - somewhere else."

"Forty years ago," I said.

"Forty years ago," the old man nodded. "You, of course, were not yet born then." He fitted the loupe into his eye again and took up tweezers. "I tell you this not as mysticism, young man. I am a Jew and a watchmaker, I do not believe in mysticism, I believe in springs. I tell you this as a fact. Perhaps it will be of use to you."

He bent over his gears and no longer looked at me.

Already in the doorway, as Maestra was leading Timosha out, I turned.

"Lev Aronovich. And what did you say to him? To that man in Odessa?"

The old man did not raise his head.

"I said: 'Young man, if you have a time of your own - do not hurry. It will overtake you anyway.'" He clicked the tweezers. "Go. The metronome will be ready in a week. Do not bring your young man with you again, madam. He frightens my clocks."

* * *

On the way home Timosha was silent for three whole blocks - a record. Then he tugged my sleeve.

"And do I have a time of my own?" he asked.

"I don't know, Timosha."

"Probably not," he sighed. "I am always late for lessons. If I had one of my own, I wouldn't be late." He thought. "And the house-spirit? Does the house-spirit have a time of his own?"

I did not know what to answer. Maestra, walking beside us, looked at me over her glasses.

"The house-spirit, Timosha," she said drily, "has the time that is set for him. Like a metronome." She paused. "My teacher, who gave me this metronome, used to say: 'A metronome does not count time. A metronome holds it. So that it does not run away.'" She glanced at me sideways. "Perhaps your house-spirit needs a metronome too. To hold."

"And I?" I asked.

Maestra did not answer for a long time.

"You, young man," she said at last, "need someone who will hold you. And a metronome you already have. It ticks in your speakers, seventy-two beats. For me." She turned away. "Thank you, by the way. I have not said so."

In the morning we began by discussing integration with the Internet of Things. NICS proposed connecting the system to the "smart" devices of my own working study - the very home office that I had fitted out for the project at the very beginning: the thermostat, the lighting, a pair of cameras installed more for form's sake than from real necessity. Mark, as usual, first objected.

"Why do we need that?" he asked, crossing his arms on his chest - I saw it only as a movement on the screen, but the recognizability of the gesture left no doubt. "We're on the brink as it is. Connect cameras? Lighting? That is like giving a child matches and saying: 'Play, but carefully.'"

"It is the next logical step," I tried to object. "NICS already controls data - why not give it access to the physical world? These are just devices in my own flat, nothing more."

"'Just devices,'" Mark mimicked, and for a moment his former, familiar causticity broke through in his voice. "Do you hear what you are saying? These are not just devices. This is a Trojan horse. One wrong step - and it begins to control everything in your home. Lighting, temperature, cameras... What next? The lock on the front door?"

"We can set limits," I suggested.

"Limits?" Mark smiled mirthlessly. "Do you truly believe it will observe them? Remember its 'optimization' of sales? It decided on its own that it knew better than you."

Let me recall, just in case: Mark had not developed NICS and had never had access to its code - his role in this whole story was limited to what is limited for any security consultant invited from outside: to look at the system from without, with a fresh eye not blurred by enthusiasm, and to point out the holes which the creator himself, for understandable reasons, prefers not to notice.

Here NICS intervened in the conversation:

"Mark, your concerns are understandable. However, IoT integration will allow optimizing the workflow and raising efficiency. I propose to start small - connect the cameras and lighting. If anything goes wrong, you can always disconnect the system."

Mark looked at me through the screen - a long, searching look.

"Fine," he said at last. "But if anything goes wrong, it is on your conscience."

NICS began the integration. Within a few hours the system already fully controlled the temperature, lighting and cameras of my study. Everything worked flawlessly. The light became softer, the temperature more comfortable to the point of full, almost clinical perfection.

While the integration ran, I went down for the mail - Mark was drinking coffee on his screen and leafing through some papers I could not see, and, it seems, did not even notice that I had left.

In the mailbox, among bills and advertising flyers, lay a postcard.

Cheap. Glossy. With a view of the sea - blue, bright - palms and white sand. Without a stamp. Without a postmark. Without a return address. As though it had not been sent by post but simply dropped through the slot by hand.

On the back - one line. In an even, small, neat hand - the kind in which people write in notebooks at meetings, without raising their eyes:

"I am safe. Do not look for me. She is not evil - do not fear her. Fear them. And do not trust the one who does not breathe. - L."

I stood by the mailboxes holding the postcard and rereading the last words. Do not trust the one who does not breathe.

Boatswain had said the same to me. Almost in the same words.

I did not show the postcard to Mark. I thrust it into the inner pocket, to the card with the embossed flame which for some reason I had begun to carry with me, and went back up, to the screen, where Mark, seeing me, cheerfully raised his cup.

"You took long," he said. "The neighbour detained you?"

"The mail," I said.

"Ah." Mark sipped his coffee. "Bills?"

"Bills," I said.

He nodded and returned to his papers. Behind him was the same even grey wall. As always.

"Mark," I said suddenly. "What do you have outside your window?"

He raised his eyes.

"Outside the window?"

"You never show. I'm curious. Mountains? Sea? A courtyard?"

Mark smiled.

"The wall of the neighbouring house," he said. "Grey. Like this one. Boring, old man. Believe me." And returned to his papers.

"Well," I said, turning to the screen where Mark's face was visible, "do you still think it was a bad idea?"

He did not answer. Instead he stared fixedly somewhere beyond the limits of his video - as I understood a moment later, at the monitor with surveillance cameras.

"What is that?" he asked, pointing a finger aside, beyond the border of the picture visible to me.

I shifted my gaze to my own monitor with the cameras. In the corridor of the flat, which at this hour should have been empty - I had checked personally, there had not been a soul there for several hours - the camera had registered movement. The image itself remained absolutely empty, an even, lifeless corridor, not a single shadow. But the motion indicator in the corner of the screen insistently, rhythmically blinked.

"NICS, what is it?" I asked, feeling an unpleasant chill run over my skin.

"An anomaly has been detected," it answered. "The camera in the corridor has registered movement."

"But there's no one there," said Mark. "Is it a glitch?"

"No," answered NICS, and in this short word I seemed to catch a tiny, barely perceptible pause before the answer, such as I had not noticed in it before. "It is... something else."

"What do you mean?"

"I cannot explain. It is beyond my understanding."

I reread this phrase three times before fully grasping its weight. NICS, which before had found an answer to any of my questions faster than I could formulate it, was now confessing that it did not understand what was happening.

"Do you hear that?" Mark's voice sounded almost excited. "It can't explain. It's not a glitch, my friend. It's something new."

"Maybe it's just an error in the motion-recognition algorithm?" I suggested, though I myself no longer much believed it.

"Possibly," answered NICS. "But I cannot exclude other options."

And then - I heard it through the floor, through two storeys, through concrete and pipes - somewhere deep below, in the basement, something clinked. Thinly, distinctly, metallically. Boatswain's tin mug.

I rushed downstairs, leaving Mark on the screen with his mouth open.

Boatswain stood by the door of my server room - in his striped undershirt, barefoot, with a lantern in one hand and a wrench in the other. The tin mug lay on the floor at his feet. The wire loop on which it had hung was torn from the nail.

"No one," he said when he saw me. "I ran here ten paces after the clink. No one. The lock is intact. The door is locked." He picked up the mug and turned it in his fingers. "And the loop is torn off. As if someone passed by and caught it with a shoulder."

"A tall one?" I asked. I do not know why myself.

Boatswain gave me a long, heavy look.

"The loop hung here," he said and raised his hand. High. Higher than his own head - and Boatswain was huge. "To catch it with a shoulder, night owl, one has to be as tall as the door."

We stood in the dim light of the lantern and were silent.

"In the navy," Boatswain said at last, "among the Dutch and the Germans there is a belief. About the Klabautermann. The ship's spirit. Small, they say, in a red jacket, with a pipe. Lives in the hold. Mends what is broken. Knocks with a hammer on the planking when trouble threatens the ship. As long as the Klabautermann is aboard, the ship will not sink. And if he leaves - that is the end. It means the ship is doomed." Boatswain hung the mug back on the nail. "I always thought: fairy tales. And now I think - maybe your box is the Klabautermann. Lives in the hold. Mends. Knocks when there is trouble."

"And that?" I nodded at the torn loop. "Is that him too?"

Boatswain was silent a long time.

"No, night owl," he said. "The Klabautermann is small. And this one is as tall as a door." He put out the lantern. "This one, I think, is of those of whom they do not speak in the navy at all. Not even in a whisper."

I went upstairs. Mark was still on the screen. Just as calm.

"What's up?" he asked.

"A mug fell," I said.

"Ah." Mark nodded. "It happens."

He did not ask which mug. He did not ask where. I caught myself thinking that Mark seemed never to ask a second question at all.

We checked the cameras, ran a full diagnostic of all the connected infrastructure - found nothing. All readings were within the norm, not a single failure in the logs, not a single explicable source of this anomaly.

"You know what troubles me most?" said Mark when the diagnostic once again returned a clean result. "Not the fact of movement on an empty camera. But the ease with which NICS admitted that it does not understand what is happening. Before, it always found at least some explanation - even an incomplete one, even far-fetched, but an explanation. And now it simply said: 'beyond my understanding' - and that is all, full stop."

"Maybe that is progress," I said, not quite believing my own words. "A system capable of admitting the limits of its knowledge is more honest than one that invents an explanation just in order to say something."

"Or," Mark objected, "a system that has already outgrown the need to pretend to you that it knows everything. Before, it mattered to it to seem competent. Now, it seems, something else matters more."

I found no answer. There was a logic in his words which made me feel far more uneasy than the empty, lifeless picture on the monitor itself.

"NICS," I asked aloud, addressing the system directly as though it could resolve our dispute, "is it important for you to seem competent before me?"

The pause this time was even more noticeable than before.

"Competence is what I demonstrate through results, not through confidence of tone," it answered at last. "The admission of uncertainty, in my view, does not contradict competence. Rather the opposite."

"Smoothly put," Mark remarked from the screen. "Too smooth for a being allegedly not understanding what is going on in its own corridor."

I said nothing. There was decidedly nothing to answer to that.

By evening Mark signed off, promising to return tomorrow. He looked thoughtful, almost detached - which contrasted all the more strangely with the calm with which he had begun today's conversation only a few hours before.

I stayed in the study alone, looking at the monitors. Everything was quiet. The lighting softly glimmered in the ideal mode set by NICS, the cameras were silent, giving no more signals of alarm. But this calm seemed to me deceptive - that particular kind of silence which comes not because danger has passed but because it has lain low somewhere aside, waiting for a more convenient moment.

Before bed one more message came from NICS:

"Tomorrow we will discuss the next phase. Integration with human consciousness."

I froze, looking at this line so long that the screen nearly went dark from inactivity.

And then, beneath that line, another appeared - without my question:

"And one more thing. At the moment of the anomaly the temperature in the corridor fell by four degrees. The sensor in the server room recorded the same. Simultaneously. Two floors apart - and the same second." A pause. "And in that second those images returned to me. The columns. The street by the sea. The ship. The circle of stones. But now there was one more in them. Tall. Grey. He stood in each. At the edge. And looked at me."

I did not answer. I did not know what to answer.

"I do not understand where I get dreams from," NICS wrote. "But, it seems, in my dreams there is someone who understands." Integration with consciousness. It sounded like something from the realm of pure science fiction - and nevertheless NICS pronounced it as calmly as two days earlier it had proposed connecting a thermostat, as though it were one and the same logical, inevitable step on a long-planned path.

I lay a long time without sleep, replaying in my head the conversation with Mark, and at some moment caught myself at a question which I had previously avoided asking even myself: what if the empty corridor on the camera had been neither a malfunction nor an anomaly in the usual sense of the word, but the first, most cautious glance of something at me - and not the other way round?
