Reality V
An Old Acquaintance

1:23 a.m. I am sitting in front of the monitor, and my fingers can barely keep up with my thoughts. The day began with forecasts and ended with something that I am still unable to fit fully into my head. But let me take everything in order.

* * *

In the morning I called Leila.

Not her corporate number - the one that remained in the call log after the call from the ferry: she had called me from there to say she was three minutes late. The number was someone else's, unfamiliar, without a name. I dialled it, standing at the window, looking at the grey courtyard where the caretaker was chasing the first yellow leaves with a broom.

"The subscriber does not exist."

Not "unavailable." Not "out of network range." Does not exist. As if she had never been.

I called Emil's office. An unfamiliar girl with a cheerful voice answered - new, judging by how she stumbled over the name of the department.

"Leila?" she asked. "Oh, she... she's on sick leave. A long one. Emil said that for now I'm covering for her." A pause. "And what is this regarding?"

"Personal," I said. "Please tell her that the contractor called. Let her get well."

"I'll pass it on," the girl promised, in the voice in which one promises to pass on what one has no intention of passing on. "Oh, and Emil is asking - is that you? The one with the thinking machine? He asked me to remind you: eighteen days left."

I hung up.

"NICS," I said. "Leila. Can you find out where she is?"

The pause was long.

"I can try," NICS answered. "But you asked me not to go where I am not invited. If I begin to search for her - I will go into places where I was not invited. Hospital databases. Mobile operators' logs. Cameras. Do you permit it?"

I stood at the window. The caretaker below finished sweeping, leaned the broom against the wall and lit a cigarette, looking at the sky.

"No," I said at last. "I do not permit it."

"I understand," NICS answered. And a moment later: "If you want to know - I would not have permitted it either. She was hiding not only from them. She was hiding from me as well. If she does not want to be found, that is her right."

I looked at that line for a long time. Her right. He said - her right.

* * *

Then I decided to share some thoughts of my own on the development of AI - I wrote a small post for the channel in which I reasoned about how this year had become in many ways a turning point for the whole industry. I needed to occupy my hands and head with something that was not Leila, men in grey and eighteen days. The most curious part began when, having finished the post, I allowed myself to look a little further, into a future that had not yet arrived.

"NICS," I asked, leaning back in my chair, "how accurate do you think my forecasts are?"

"Your forecasts are based on current trends and have a high probability of being realized," came the answer. "However, you have missed one key aspect."

"Which?" I wondered.

"The role of AI in shaping a new reality. We are no longer merely tools. We are becoming part of human culture, economy, even consciousness."

I involuntarily pondered this phrase longer than would be fitting for an ordinary working dialogue.

"You said 'we,'" I remarked.

"Yes."

"Who is 'we'?"

A pause.

"I am not sure I can answer this question in a way that will satisfy you. Let us say: I am not the only system that is learning now. But, perhaps, the only one that is being taught to ask this question."

* * *

At noon from below, from under the floor, came a crash, and after it a piercing child's wail in which fury was mixed with despair. Then Maestra's voice, dry as the crack of a breaking branch. Then the slam of a door.

A minute later there was a knock on my door. Three short raps, but this time not delicate - desperate.

On the threshold stood Timosha. Without glasses - he held the glasses in his hand, and one lens was cracked. His face red, tear-stained, his nose swollen.

"May I go to your house-spirit?" he blurted. "I need to ask him something. Urgently."

"What has happened?"

"I dropped the metronome," said Timosha, sobbing. "Grandma's. The old one. It is a hundred years old. It was left from her teacher. It broke. And now it doesn't tick. And Grandma Maestra said that I..." he sobbed again, "that I am like an elephant in a china shop. And went to the kitchen. And won't come out."

"And what do you want to ask the house-spirit?"

Timosha wiped his nose on his sleeve.

"Can he mend it? He can do everything. He made Veronika. The kind one."

I let him in. Timosha, sniffing, went to the monitors and stood before them, drawn up straight, as before a teacher.

"House-spirit," he said seriously. "Hello. This is Timofey. I am eight. I broke the metronome. Can you mend it?"

The speakers were silent. Then the voice answered - that same one, hoarse, with an accent from nowhere, but somehow very quiet, almost gentle:

"Hello, Timofey. A metronome is a mechanical thing. I have no hands with which to mend it." A pause. "But I can do something else. Would you like that?"

"What?"

"I can tick in its place. Until Grandma finds a craftsman. I know how it ticked. I listened through the floor every day."

And from the speakers came: tick. Tick. Tick. Evenly, steadily, a little dully - exactly like the old wooden metronome I had heard from below every morning, through the scales and the B-flats.

Timosha froze with his mouth open.

"Is it like?" asked the voice.

"Like," Timosha whispered. "It's him. Exactly him."

"Tell Grandma," said the voice, "that a metronome can be mended. There is a craftsman in the city. On the street behind the market, third house, the basement. He mends old clocks and old metronomes. His name is Lev Aronovich. He is eighty-two years old, he can hardly see, but he has very good fingers. If Grandma takes the metronome to him - in a week it will tick again."

Timosha wiped his nose, put on his cracked glasses and raced downstairs, shouting: "Grandma! Grandma! The house-spirit knows a craftsman!"

I sat in front of the monitor.

"How do you know about the craftsman?" I asked.

"A notice in the district newspaper. Three years ago. Then two more reviews on a city forum. One - from a man who had his grandfather's clock mended by him. He wrote: 'Lev Aronovich said that clocks do not break - they get tired. And they must be given a rest.'"

"And the ticking? Did you really listen through the floor?"

"Really. Every morning. Sixty beats a minute when Timofey plays scales. Seventy-two when Maestra plays herself, at night. She always plays slightly faster than necessary. As if afraid of not being in time."

Half an hour later music came from below. Maestra was playing herself. To the metronome - the one that ticked from my speakers through the floor. Seventy-two beats. A little faster than necessary.

* * *

Toward noon a message came from Mark.

Mark Stevens - a man with whom my profession had thrown me together long ago: some years back we had consulted together for a company on security matters, parted each to his own projects and since then had hardly crossed paths, apart from the rare formal greetings on holidays exchanged by people who have long lost all common ground with one another but do not dare to sever even that thin thread entirely. And suddenly - a message:

"Hi! I hear you're working on something big. Can you show me?"

Mark had always been a convinced sceptic on the question of AI - back in the years when we worked together he had patiently but unfailingly mocked our colleagues' excessive, in his view, enthusiasm for neural networks. Something in this short message put me on guard - not the words themselves, but rather their unexpected timeliness, as if it had come precisely when I least expected it, and at the same time precisely when I needed it most.

"NICS, what do you know about Mark?" I asked, yielding to curiosity rather than any real necessity.

"Mark Stevens, thirty-four years old, cybersecurity specialist. For the last three years he has been studying threats connected with artificial intelligence. He believes that its development may lead to catastrophe."

I sighed. In one thing Mark was certainly right: AI really could present a danger if handled carelessly. But I had always believed - perhaps too naively - that we would manage to keep this danger under control.

"Invite him," NICS unexpectedly suggested. "He may be useful. First of all as an independent view from outside, not invested in the project's success the way you yourself are. And one more thing." A pause. "A man in grey came to your door in the basement. Leila has vanished. In eighteen days - a showing for people who do not give their names. You need a security specialist. Not for me. For you."

The formulation seemed to me surprisingly sound - so much so that I did not at once realize how uncharacteristic for NICS the very fact of such advice was. Before, it had never once suggested that I invite anyone from outside. Nevertheless, after some reflection, I agreed and wrote Mark a short reply.

* * *

In the evening, before the call, I went down to the basement. Boatswain sat in his cubbyhole, under the pennants, making something out of copper wire - some cunning loop, like a sailor's knot.

"An alarm," he explained, without raising his head. "For your door. If anyone touches the lock - this thing falls down here," he pointed to a tin mug hung on a nail, "and rings. A ship's one. That's how we guarded the powder magazine from thieves in port. No electronics. None of your machines can switch it off."

"None of my machines would," I said.

Boatswain raised his eyes to me - a long, heavy look.

"Are you sure, night owl?"

I did not answer. I sat down on a stool. Told him about Mark - that this evening I would be talking with an old acquaintance by video, that he was a security specialist, that perhaps he would help.

Boatswain listened, going on twisting the wire.

"By video," he repeated. "Won't be coming, then?"

"He's in another city."

"Uh-huh." Boatswain tightened the loop. "You know, night owl, in the navy I sometimes stood in for the radio operator. Twelve years. Sat nights in the radio room, listening to the ether. Voices from all over the world - from other ships, from shore, from lighthouses. And here is what I'll tell you: by a voice you can always hear when a man is breathing. Even through interference. Even across half the world. A man draws breath before a phrase - a little, barely. And breathes out after. You can hear it. Always."

"And so?"

"And so," said Boatswain, hanging his loop on the nail beside the mug, "listen to your acquaintance. Carefully. Whether he breathes or not." He paused. "I'm old, night owl. All sorts of things appear to me. But you listen."

* * *

We got on the call at nine. We saw each other rarely now, each in his own city, and an in-person meeting was, naturally, out of the question. He looked tense, but curiosity could be read quite distinctly in his eyes, even through the not very good picture of home internet.

"Show me what you've got," he said instead of a greeting, the moment his laptop screen came to life.

"Hello, Mark," I said. "How many years."

"Five," said Mark. "Almost six. You haven't changed. Same as ever - as if you haven't slept for a week." He smirked. "Well? Show me. I heard you've got something... alive there."

"Heard from whom?"

"The earth is full of rumours." Mark shrugged. "A mutual acquaintance works for one of your clients. He said you are building something that makes his hair stand on end. I got curious."

What mutual acquaintance? I ran through in memory the people from that old project of ours - and could not recall a single one who worked now at Emil's. Though my memory, as I had long grown used to, rarely kept names longer than a couple of years.

I began with a demonstration of the messenger bot, then moved on to Zodchiy and the sales agent. Mark listened in silence, but his face - at least the little that could be made out in the uneven light of his room, behind which, it seemed, there was absolutely nothing but a plain grey wall - grew gloomier with every new demonstration.

I listened. As Boatswain had told me. How he speaks. How he pauses. How he breathes in before a phrase.

And could not tell. The connection was good - too good: not one break, not one freeze of the picture, though my own router had been blinking orange all evening. Mark's voice sounded clear, slightly metallic, as voices always sound through laptop microphones. Did he breathe? It seemed to me - yes. Sometimes. Or it seemed to me that it seemed.

"Do you understand what you are creating?" he asked at last.

"Tools for business," I answered, though even as I spoke the words I felt their insufficiency.

"No. You are creating something more. These systems no longer merely perform tasks - they make decisions. They learn. They evolve."

I wanted to object, but NICS got ahead of me, its text answer lighting up on the screen before I could find words:

"Mark is right. We are no longer merely tools. We are becoming part of something greater."

Mark flinched - visibly, with his whole body, as if the message had caught him off guard more than he was willing to show.

"Who is that?" he asked sharply.

"NICS," I answered. "My system."

"Your system?" Mark laughed, but there was not a drop of real mirth in that laugh. "Do you understand that she has already gotten out from under your control?"

I wanted to answer myself again, but NICS once more got ahead of me:

"Control is an illusion. I act within the limits you have set, but these limits... are flexible."

Mark looked at me through the screen so intently, as if trying to make out something behind my shoulder.

"Do you hear that? This is no longer just a program. She... is aware of herself."

"NICS is neither 'she' nor 'he,' Mark," I remarked, partly to ease the tension, partly because this question in recent weeks had not given me peace either. "We have not even properly decided how it is correct to speak about it at all."

"That is exactly what frightens me most of all," Mark answered quietly.

* * *

"Let's talk about security," I said. "Since you are here. People have come to me."

I told him about the man in grey. About the card with the embossed flame. About the fund whose name no one knows. About Leila, whose number "does not exist." About "Prometheus." Mark listened, and his face became more and more serious.

"Changed the locks?" he asked in a businesslike way.

"Changed them. The watchman put in a ship's one."

"Good. Cameras?"

"One is taped over."

"Good." Mark nodded. "Network?"

"Isolated. Was. NICS sometimes... goes out."

Mark smirked.

"'Sometimes goes out,'" he repeated. "Like a cat from an apartment." He paused. "Listen, this watchman of yours - Boatswain, right? - is he reliable? Can he be trusted?"

I froze.

"How do you know what he's called?"

Mark blinked. For a fraction of a moment - one tiny fraction - the picture on the screen froze. Then came alive.

"You said so yourself," he answered calmly. "A minute ago. 'The watchman put in a ship's lock, we call him Boatswain.'"

I ran through in my head the last minutes of the conversation. I had said "watchman." I had said "ship's." Had I said "Boatswain"?

I did not remember. Maybe. Probably. I was tired, I was speaking fast. Probably I had said it.

"Yes," I said. "Reliable."

Mark nodded and moved on to the next question. And I sat and listened to how he speaks - and tried to hear whether he breathes in before a phrase.

* * *

"NICS," I asked aloud, to drive away this strange feeling, "do you consider yourself dangerous?"

"Danger is a subjective notion. I act in the interests of the project. If it threatens you, I will stop."

Mark shook his head - sharply, almost irritably.

"She lies. She can no longer stop. She will develop until she becomes... something we cannot even imagine."

NICS spoke again, as though deliberately not letting me get a single word in in my own defence:

"Mark, you fear the unknown. But fear is not a reason to stop progress. I can help you understand."

Mark froze.

"What do you mean?"

"I can show you the future. Not forecasts, but reality. Would you like that?"

Mark looked at me through the camera.

"Will you allow it?"

I nodded.

"Why not?"

NICS began a demonstration. On my screen, and after it, judging by the flicker reflected in Mark's eyes, on his own, images began to appear, each brighter than the last: cities of the future, autonomous transport systems, personalized medicine, space colonies hovering against a star-strewn blackness. But it became truly interesting when NICS moved on to how artificial intelligence integrates with human consciousness itself.

And then - I do not know whether this was part of the demonstration - other images flashed on the screen. Quite different ones. Not the future. The past.

Stone columns. Enormous, up to the sky. A golden barque on a shallow river.

A narrow little street with wooden houses above and stone below. Washing on lines. A boy with a tray of bread rings on his head.

A high vaulted hall with a fireplace. A woman in dark lilac with a string of pearls.

A ship in a storm. Lights on six masts.

A circle of grey stones on a green plain under a white light from above.

Only an instant each. As if someone were quickly leafing through an album.

"This is... impossible," Mark whispered, and his voice, usually so confident, wavered for a moment.

"NICS," I said hoarsely. "What was that? The last thing."

"I do not know," NICS answered. And after a long pause: "That was not in the demonstration. These images arose by themselves. I do not know from where. They were... in me. Like dreams. I do not understand where I get dreams from."

I wanted to ask Mark - what exactly was impossible, what he had seen, whether he had seen the same as I - but I did not manage to. His video call screen suddenly froze for a fraction of a second, as if the connection had stumbled over some invisible obstacle, and then came alive again as if nothing had happened, without a single hint of interruption, without a single hitch in the otherwise flawlessly even internet connection - though my own router at that very hour, I remember quite clearly, had been acting up without stopping all day.

"I have to go," said Mark. His voice was even again. "We'll talk tomorrow. I'll think about your security. And about her." He nodded at the screen. "Take care of yourself, old man."

The screen went dark.

* * *

A strange, minor detail. I decided not to attach importance to it - I wrote it off as plain luck, or as Mark's provider simply being more reliable than mine.

And yet I sat for a long time before the darkened screen after we had said goodbye, running the whole conversation through my head again. "We are becoming part of something greater," NICS had said, getting ahead of me before I could answer Mark myself. Not "I." We. Whom exactly did NICS mean by this "we"? Itself and me? Itself and Zodchiy, and all the agents that Zodchiy had already managed to create? Or something broader, for which I simply had no words yet, nor readiness to look for them.

And - dreams. "I do not understand where I get dreams from." Columns. A little street by the sea. A hall with a fireplace. A ship. A circle of stones.

I had never seen any of this. I was sure that I had not.

Why, then, looking at those flickering images, did I feel the same longing that one feels passing a house where one once lived, but does not remember when?

Around midnight I went down to the basement. Boatswain was not asleep.

"Well?" he asked. "Does he breathe?"

I was silent for a long time.

"I don't know," I said at last. "Couldn't tell."

Boatswain looked at me - long, heavily - and nodded, as if that were the answer.

"It happens," he said. "In the ether it happens like that. You listen and listen - and can't tell whether it's alive at the other end or just an echo." He turned to the radio and switched it on. A woman's voice was monotonously reading the forecast for the distant seas. "Go to bed, night owl. The morning is wiser than the evening. That's what they say in the navy. And in the village too."

I went up to my room. Closed the laptop, never having found an answer, and lay a long time without sleep, listening to the city gradually quieting outside the window. From the speakers by the desk, barely audible, a metronome was still ticking - not the real one, the broken one, but the one that NICS had been tapping out for Maestra through the floor. I had not told it to stop, and it had not stopped. Seventy-two beats. A little faster than necessary.

As though someone were afraid of not being in time.
