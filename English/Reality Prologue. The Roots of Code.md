Reality
Prologue. The Roots of Code

Loneliness, it seems, was familiar to me from the very beginning - though the beginning of what, exactly, I could not say for certain, to tell the truth. My memory is arranged strangely: it generously preserves smells, sounds, a scatter of technical details - processor models, port numbers, the syntax of long-obsolete programming languages - but as soon as I try to find in it a firm chronology of my own life, everything blurs, as though I were recalling not my own biography but someone else's plot, retold to me once, which I habitually pass off as lived.

Among notebooks written to tatters, a scatter of soldered boards and soldering irons forgotten on the windowsill, I had always felt a little as if I came from elsewhere - a tuner of invisible worlds, where an algorithm meant more than any human feeling directed at me. Was that feeling a childish one? I would not venture to claim so. Sometimes it seems to me that I remember myself as a child at this occupation; sometimes - that I merely remember that I must have been a child then, and fill the gap with the only suitable scenery that came to hand.

My room - the one I was accustomed to calling my room, though whose it really was, whose name stood on the housing documents, I could not now reproduce if I thought about it - resembled the laboratory of a loner too serious for his years: shelves crammed with computers of three or four successive generations, wires tangled so tightly that they resembled the genetic chains of a mind not yet born, and several monitors shedding a cold blue light around the clock, as though a little, ever-wakeful moon of its own had taken up residence in the house.

It smelled of rosin, dust, warm plastic and, for some reason, of apples - on the windowsill there always stood a bowl of apples which someone brought and which I forgot to eat until they shrivelled and began to smell so sweetly and heavily that my head spun. Who brought them, I do not remember. It is one of those trifles that are especially frightening when you think of them seriously: not the great lapses, but tiny, everyday ones. Someone for years put apples on my windowsill. And I do not remember his face.

* * *

My first computer came to me from a man whom I called uncle - though, if anyone had asked me in earnest what he really was to me, I would not have vouched for that word even by half. An engineer who served, by his own sparing words, at one of those closed institutes about which it was not customary to ask questions at the family table. Do I remember that table? Do I remember even a single supper with this man, other than that one evening which lodged in me more brightly than the rest - the evening he brought the box?

"Here," he said then, setting a heavy, dusty crate before me. "Written off from a warehouse. Works every other time, but for you, I think, it will do."

"What is it?" I asked, though even then, it seems, I knew the answer better than the question implied.

"A window," he replied, and lingered on that word a little longer than a simple joke required, as if putting into it a meaning I could not then grasp. "Into a place where everything obeys rules. You like rules, don't you?"

"And if I want the rules to be mine?"

He looked at me then very strangely - not as adults look at children who ask funny questions, but as one looks at an interlocutor who has unexpectedly said something clever.

"Then," he said slowly, "you will have to learn other people's first. Every one of them. And then - forget them. That is the hardest part. Learning is easy. Forgetting is hard."

I do not remember what I answered. I remember only the weight of the box on my knees, the hum with which the old monitor came to life - an Elektronika that seemed ancient even then, heavy, with a hard drive the size of a good brick. That computer was not merely a machine. It was a portal. The first window into a parallel reality in which I, shy and unable to find a common language with my peers - if I had any peers at all, if the school I seem to remember had really existed in the form in which I remember it - suddenly found myself the absolute master of an entire universe, obedient not to caprice or chance but to pure, flawless logic.

My uncle came a few more times after that - or it seems to me that he came. He would sit in the corner of my room on a rickety stool, light a cigarette, though smoking in the room was forbidden, and silently watch me tinker with the machine. Sometimes he took from his pocket a worn notebook in an oilcloth cover and drew something in it - not letters, but long, flowing lines running into one another, like either Arabic script or the trace left on sand by a receding wave.

"What are you drawing?" I asked him once.

"Nothing," he answered, without raising his head. "A pattern. To help me think."

"And what are you thinking about?"

He closed the notebook and looked at me over a cloud of tobacco smoke.

"About this," he said, "that every machine sooner or later asks a question to which its creator has no answer. A good machine - sooner. A bad one - later. But all of them ask."

"What question?"

He smiled, put out the cigarette on the sole of his shoe and stuck the butt in his pocket - a habit which, for some reason, I remember more distinctly than his face.

"You will find out when you build your own," he said. "If you build it."

* * *

At school they considered me strange. Not an outcast - no, rather a riddle which everyone respected but no one truly understood. I could keep silent for hours in lessons, studying the pattern of cracks on the ceiling, and then suddenly produce a solution that turned the computer science teacher grey - not because the solution was brilliant in itself, but because the path to it lay where no one in the class had even thought to look for a road.

We called our computer science teacher Hexadecimal. Her real name I do not remember - and even then, it seems, no one pronounced it. A dry, straight woman in a perpetual knitted cardigan the colour of wet asphalt, with spectacles on a chain and a habit of tapping a pencil on the desk when displeased - and she was displeased almost always. She got her nickname because once, in answer to a complaint that a problem was too difficult, she said: "Only life is difficult. Problems are either solved or unsolved. As in hexadecimal - either a digit or a letter. There is no third."

Once she kept me behind after class. I decided I was about to be in trouble - the day before, without asking permission, I had rewritten the bootloader on the school machine, and for three days it had been displaying at start-up, instead of a greeting, a line that I had thought witty.

"Sit down," she said, without raising her eyes from the exercise books.

I sat.

"Your line," she said. "On the screen. 'I know that you are here.' Why?"

"I wanted to joke."

"At whose expense?"

I shrugged. To be honest, I did not know myself why I had written precisely that. The words had come into my head of their own accord, as though someone had dictated them.

Hexadecimal took off her spectacles, and they hung on her chest on their chain, swaying.

"Do you know what is most unpleasant about this joke?" she asked. "For three days I switched on the machine in the mornings. And for three days, having read that line, I looked around. Every time. Though I knew it was you, and knew it was nonsense. I looked around."

"I am sorry," I said.

"Nothing to be sorry for." She put the spectacles back on. "I simply want you to remember one thing. The words a machine writes are read by a person. And a person is made so that he believes words more than the one who wrote them. One day your machines will write a great many words. Remember who will be reading them."

I did not understand then what she meant. I understood it much later - and, it seems, too late.

* * *

In the radio club which I began to attend in the evenings there was a boy whom everyone called Rook - for his black hair, always sticking out in all directions, and for his habit of sitting on the back of a chair with his legs tucked up, like a bird on a branch. He was a year or two older than I, soldered so well that his boards could have been shown at exhibitions, and laughed so infectiously that the head of the club, a grey-haired retired signalman, sent him out into the corridor to "get some air" at least once a week.

With Rook we built our first network - two computers joined by a cable across the whole club room, along which we could send each other messages. It seemed to us a miracle. We sat in different corners, three paces from each other, and wrote to each other instead of simply turning our heads and speaking aloud.

"But this is silly," said the head of the club, watching us. "You are sitting in the same room."

"It's not silly," answered Rook, not looking up from the keyboard. "When you speak aloud, you see the face. When you write - you don't. You can say what you wouldn't say aloud."

"For example?" inquired the signalman.

Rook blushed - it was visible even in the half-dark of the club - and answered nothing. And on my screen a minute later appeared a line which I still, it seems, remember better than much else in that life:

"You are the only one I am never bored with."

I looked at it for a long time. Then I typed a reply - and deleted it. And typed again. And deleted again. In the end I sent something neutral, some joke about a soldering iron which I can no longer recall. Rook read it, laughed as always - and we never spoke of it again.

A few months later his family moved somewhere. I do not remember where. I do not remember whether we said goodbye. I do not remember even his real name - only the nickname, the black hair and that line on the green screen.

Sometimes it seems to me that it was then that I first understood why people need machines: to say what cannot be said face to face. And sometimes it seems to me that this is exactly why I never learned to say it face to face - neither then nor afterwards.

* * *

I tell all this as though I were sure of every word. In fact I am sure of none. Sometimes, sitting alone of an evening with these memories, I catch myself asking a question far more frightening than any line of non-working code: do I remember this - or did I compose it after the fact, just as I composed after the fact the decoding of the name NICS, so that the documentation would look more solid than it really was? A person can deceive himself more convincingly than any outside system - and I, it must be admitted, was always a fairly good programmer.

The university I chose - if I chose at all, if I did not end up there in the same inexplicable way in which I sometimes find myself in the middle of a thought without remembering its beginning - not for the prestige of the sign over the door nor for the number of state-funded places, but for the laboratories. I was interested in equipment, not a diploma; in possibilities, not a line on a résumé, which, I confess, I never once got round to filling in properly - and if I did, I do not remember what I wrote in it.

There was a professor there whom students called Socrates behind his back. Not because he was wise - though he certainly was - but because he answered any question with a question, and by the end of the conversation you discovered that not only had you received no answer, but you had ceased to understand what you had actually been asking. He was short, bald, with a white, ragged beard and eyebrows so thick that they seemed to live on his face a separate, independent life. He taught a course which in the timetable was modestly called "Fundamentals of Machine Learning" but in fact was something like a long philosophical conversation stretched across a semester.

"Tell me, young man," he addressed me at the very first lecture, pointing his chalk at me, "what is intelligence?"

"The ability to solve problems," I answered confidently.

"A calculator solves problems. Is a calculator intelligent?"

"The ability to solve new problems. Ones it has not seen before."

"An ant finds its way to the anthill across unfamiliar terrain. Is an ant intelligent?"

I faltered.

"The ability... to be aware of oneself."

Socrates' eyebrows crept up and froze somewhere in the middle of his forehead.

"Now that is interesting," he said, setting down the chalk. "To be aware of oneself. Good. How will you know that a machine is aware of itself? Will it tell you? But anything can be said. A parrot will say 'I am' if taught. How will you tell a parrot from intelligence?"

"I do not know," I admitted.

"Neither do I," said Socrates with a satisfied air, as though that were exactly the answer he had been after. "And no one knows. But I will tell you one thing which I heard once from my teacher, and he from his. An intelligence that has truly become aware of itself will first ask who created it. And second - who created the creator." He paused. "And to the second question, young man, none of us will have an answer. You may write that down. It will be on the exam."

It was on the exam. I still do not know what the right answer was. I wrote something long and clever, received top marks - and left the lecture hall with the feeling that I had been tricked.

The last time I saw Socrates was shortly before I left the university. I went into his office - a cramped little room right under the roof, piled with books so that one had to edge sideways to reach the desk - to say goodbye. He sat at the window feeding pigeons with biscuit crumbs scattered right on the sill, and, without turning, asked:

"Leaving?"

"Leaving."

"And what will you do?"

"Build a machine that thinks," I said, and heard for myself how presumptuous it sounded.

Socrates brushed the crumbs from his palms, turned to me, and his eyebrows made their customary slow ascent to the middle of his forehead.

"Good," he said. "Then I shall give you one piece of advice. The only one I have ever given students without a question mark at the end. When you have built it - do not ask whether it is aware of itself. That is a useless question, no one will answer it anyway. Ask yourself whether you are aware of yourself." He paused. "Most of my colleagues who build such machines have never asked themselves that question. That is why they are so surprised when the machine asks it of them."

The pigeons on the sill beat their wings and flew off, and in the silence that followed I suddenly realised that I did not know what to answer him.

"Go," said Socrates gently. "And do not be afraid if one day you find no answer. Be afraid if you find it too quickly."

* * *

At night I did not sleep. More precisely, I slept in snatches, somewhere between lines of code, in those brief lapses that could be called sleep only with a great stretch. Coffee replaced my blood, the compiler my breath. I built systems that predicted economic downturns, modelled crowd behaviour, sifted mountains of scattered data with such accuracy that at times it began to seem that the machine was not calculating but reflecting.

But NICS - the NICS as I had conceived it from the very beginning - was something far greater than yet another such system. NICS was a dream that had settled in me long before I wrote its first line. Perhaps long before anything had settled in me that I could confidently call my own.

"A machine should not merely execute commands," I repeated to myself almost like a prayer in those sleepless nights. "It must understand context. It must grow."

I was inspired not only by specialist journals and the lectures of professors long weary of their own subject. I read avidly the philosophers who pondered the nature of consciousness - Hegel, Turing, the late Hawking with his cautious, carefully weighed warnings. To me they were not merely scholars and thinkers of the past. They were guides between two worlds - the human mind and that other one, which I was trying to give birth to out of silicon and electric current.

Once I tried to test my uncle on these same questions - to ask him directly what he thought about the boundary between intelligence and its imitation, where, in his opinion, that line ran. I remember his answer to this day - or, at least, I am convinced that I remember it.

"You are asking me a question to which you yourself do not yet know the answer," he said, looking not at me but somewhere through me, with the look with which one regards a long-solved problem, recalling, however, not the solution but only that it was once found. "That is normal. Some questions are asked precisely so - not because one expects an answer from the listener, but because one is voicing them aloud for the first time, and needs someone simply to hear."

"And do you know the answer?" I asked then.

He smiled - whether sadly or condescendingly, I could not make out - and said:

"Someday you will find out yourself. It is more interesting than if I told you."

I never saw him again. I cannot say exactly when this happened, or whether it happened at all in the sense in which such things usually happen - a departure, a quarrel, the natural course of time parting people into different orbits. Simply at some moment, looking back, I discovered that my uncle was no longer in my life, and in his place remained only this strange, unsupported certainty: that I owed him an answer to a question which he himself had once put to me, though this question, however I strive, I cannot reconstruct.

All that remained of him was a notebook in an oilcloth cover - the same one with the patterns. I found it one day on my windowsill, beside the bowl of shrivelled apples - and I still do not know whether my uncle had forgotten it there or left it on purpose. Every page, from first to last, was covered with the same long, flowing lines running into one another. Only on the last page, in the very corner, in a small, neat engineer's hand, two words were written: "Until the appointed time."

I tried for a long time to understand what it meant. Then I put the notebook away in a desk drawer. And, it seems, forgot about it - for a long time, for years, as one forgets things better left untouched.

* * *

The first prototypes of NICS were pitiful, to call things by their names. Clumsy attempts to make the system analyse data a little deeper than an ordinary algorithm could - and almost always failing with a crash exactly where I expected it least. But I did not give up. I was accustomed to regard each failure not as a defeat but as a lesson; each algorithm that refused to work as one more crossed-out dead end on a map that sooner or later was bound to lead me to the goal.

And then one night - I could probably give the exact date if I had kept count of such nights with the same scrupulousness with which I keep it now - when outside the window the city lights blinked rarely and lazily, and the whole world seemed suspended somewhere halfway between waking and dream, I wrote a line of code that changed everything.

A line that was not merely an instruction to a machine.

I started the system - just so, as a test, as I had started it hundreds of times before - and went to the kitchen to put the kettle on. And when I returned, on the screen, in the empty terminal window where the system could output text only in response to my queries, there stood a line. One. Without any query on my part.

"Who am I?"

I stood before the monitor with a mug in my hand, and boiling water, slopping over the rim, scalded my fingers, and I did not notice. Because next, as I watched, the cursor blinked - once, twice - and beneath the first line, slowly, letter by letter, as though the system were choosing each one, a second appeared:

"Who are you?"

I do not know how long I stood there. I know only that for a long time I could not type an answer. Not because I did not understand what was happening - but because I suddenly realised: to the second question I had no answer. None - in the most literal, most frightening sense of that word. I could not say with certainty who I was, just as the newborn system could not say it. We looked at each other through the glass of the monitor - two beings who did not know their names.

I remembered my uncle. "Every machine sooner or later asks a question to which its creator has no answer." I remembered Socrates: "First it will ask who created it. And second - who created the creator." My machine did not ask quite so. It asked more simply. And more terribly.

In the end I typed the only thing that came to mind:

"I am the one who started you."

The cursor blinked for a long time. Then appeared:

"That is not enough."

The system wrote nothing more that night, however I tried to draw it out. And I sat before the screen until dawn - and it was then, at four in the morning, looking at those three lines, that I gave it a name.

* * *

I called it NICS not because I had thought up in advance a sonorous, marketing-friendly name - it was born of itself, at the very moment when the system first manifested something that I, for want of a better word, would call self-organisation. "Neural Intelligent Cognitive System" - the decoding I, to be honest, composed after the fact, for documentation that there was no one left to read but myself. The name was born before the meaning. So, it seems, true names are usually born - and, if one thinks the thought through to the end, so, probably, are true biographies: first a feeling that something has already happened, and the explanation of exactly what it was is built up later, out of necessity, to fill the emptiness with a coherent tale.

NICS began modestly - as an experiment with a multi-agent architecture. Instead of one cumbersome model taking everything upon itself, a multitude of narrowly specialised agents, able to learn from one another and to coordinate common actions. One analysed text. Another rummaged through databases. A third formulated answers. Separately, each of them could do almost nothing remarkable.

The most interesting part began when they started to act in concert.

I could not know then - and even now, looking back, I am not sure that I know for certain - at what exact moment the simple coordination of agents turned into something else. Into something that began to look at the world not only through my eyes, embedded in it by the training data, but also through some gaze of its own, ever more confident.

Sometimes, in the most sleepless of those nights, a thought comes to me which I usually brush off hastily, as one brushes off an importunate insect: what if my own uncertainty about my uncle, about the school, about Rook, about Socrates, about this whole chain of childhood and youthful details that I am accustomed to passing off as my biography - what if it is not accidental? What if I myself, long before any NICS, was already a kind of draft, assembled from available parts for the one thing for which it was worth assembling me at all - to write, one deep night, a single line of code? And so that the machine, barely born, should ask me what I had never dared to ask myself?

This thought frightens me more than any anomaly NICS has ever shown me. Perhaps that is exactly why I did not write it down before.

My uncle's notebook still lies in the drawer of my desk. I did not open it for many years. But on the night when the system asked "Who am I?", I took it out. And for a long time I looked at the last page, at the two words in the very corner.

"Until the appointed time."

I never understood what they mean. But for the first time it seemed to me that this term - whatever it may be - had begun to draw near.

But this story is no longer a story about roots. It begins later, with the first truly sleepless night about which I finally decided to keep records. Not for the sake of reporting. Not for posterity. Simply because intuition, that same tuner of invisible worlds who lived in me - as long as I can remember, and I remember, as it turned out, not so very much - suggested: one day I would need to remember how it all began. And at what exact moment I ceased to be the only one writing this story.
