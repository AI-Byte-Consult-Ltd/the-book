Chapter V
The Labyrinth

The supper had tired me all the same - more than an ordinary supper ought to, even one so long and so rich in tales as the Colonel Mikhail Ivanovich knew how to make any family evening. My head spun, my arms and legs felt filled with lead, and for this, perhaps, my own boots were partly to blame - heavy, black, leather, yet surprisingly soft and comfortable, as if cut specially for long, aimless wanderings through corridors, such as I was now indulging in, left at last alone with my own thoughts and with the candle that Ignatiy Savvich had handed me at the door.

No, I corrected myself, it was not the supper and not the boots. It was the Colonel.

With his eternal tales I could never cope calmly - after them I invariably slept badly, and sometimes had nightmares in which gun salvoes, snowy passes and the faces of people I had never seen in waking life were mingled. Scraps of his stories kept flashing through my head: the Russo-Turkish War, the Caucasian campaign, some distant, half-forgotten expeditions to Asia, to the New World - Mikhail Ivanovich seemed to have managed to fight absolutely everywhere that wars had been waged in the last thirty years, and he could tell of each as though it had happened yesterday.

I had not gone twenty paces from the doors of the hall when hurried, ringing footsteps sounded behind me, and a familiar hand settled on my shoulder.

"Deserted," Konstantin stated, catching up with me. His cheeks were flushed from the cordial and from quick walking, his uniform unbuttoned at the top button - a liberty he permitted himself only out of the General's sight. "And left me to be torn to pieces by Kutasova. She is already considering whether to marry Lizonka off to me, since the General will not yield. I told her I had consumption. She replied that it was nothing, they cure it in the Crimea."

I laughed, and the laughter stabbed in my temples.

"Did you come out only for this? To tell me about consumption?"

"I came out," he said, growing a little more serious, "because you were pale as blancmange. And because Strauch all evening looked at you as though he intended to saw you open and see what was inside. Shall I see you to your room?"

"No need. I know the way."

"You know," Konstantin repeated doubtfully. "You, mon cher, find the way to your own bedroom on the third attempt when you are lost in thought. I once saw myself how you searched for the library for half an hour while standing with your back to it."

"That was once."

"That was last Thursday." He paused, looking at me, and in the half-darkness of the corridor his face, lit from below by my candle, suddenly seemed older and sterner than at supper. "Listen. If you get lost - shout. I have ears like a regimental dog. I shall hear."

"Through the whole Castle?"

"Through the whole Castle," he said firmly, as though it were the most ordinary promise in the world. And at once, restoring his customary mockery to his voice, he added: "Well, or at least through half. Good night, philosopher."

He clapped me on the shoulder - a little longer and a little more lightly than one claps in parting - turned and strode back toward the hall, from which came the sounds of the piano: Lizonka, evidently, had been prevailed upon to play again. I watched him go until his silhouette dissolved in the light of the open doors, and caught myself with a strange wish to call after him - not knowing why. I did not call.

* * *

Artemia Aristarkhovna, my aunt - if that was the right word, of which I had for some time, for some reason, ceased to be wholly certain - disliked the Colonel's stories no less than I did. They reminded her of her late husband, of years gone by, and of a Castle once far noisier, far fuller of life. Perhaps, I thought, behind all her balls and parties, behind the unending kaleidoscope of guests, music and talk, there stood only one simple attempt - to mask a loneliness which would otherwise have become simply unbearable.

Her rare guests, mostly young, did not linger long: either they set off on another expedition, to which the gallant General Pavel Nikolaevich invariably dispatched them, or my aunt herself politely but insistently saw them on their way home as soon as they abused her hospitality.

The General was a widower. He had lost his wife many years ago, on one of the Asian campaigns - she could not endure the climate there and died of one of the Eastern diseases which the doctors could neither recognise in time nor cure afterward. Pavel Nikolaevich came of a hereditary military line: his whole family, to the most distant ancestors, had served royal persons, and served in the highest ranks. He himself had risen to General of the Imperial Army, stood in excellent favour with the Sovereign and, to all appearances, did not think of retirement for a single second.

Vera Anatolievna, wife of the Colonel-raconteur, was a frequent guest of ours - together with my aunt she organised reading evenings at which their own small circle of ladies passed the time over fashionable novels, at times brought from abroad, from France or Britain. At one such evening they had made the acquaintance of Evdokia Gerasimovna - she travelled a great deal and often brought back book novelties, chiefly from Paris.

Besides this, Evdokia Gerasimovna was fabulously rich: she had received an inheritance from her parents, industrial magnates who had once founded metallurgical works in the Urals. Her husband, also a soldier, killed in one of the campaigns, had left her in addition a very round fortune. To this were added numerous landed estates, peasants and a solid pension from the Imperial Court - for her heroically fallen husband. Evdokia Gerasimovna could afford absolutely everything: no one had ever yet seen her twice in the same dress or hat, and legends truly circulated among the capital's ladies about the exquisiteness of her headgear - it was even whispered that she ordered them directly from Paris, trusting no local milliner.

Her nephew, Lieutenant Konstantin, had in effect been brought up in solitude - his parents had died of some terrible, swift illness when he was still quite a boy. What exactly bound us to one another so easily and so long, I could not have explained - perhaps merely an excess of free time that had fallen to us both in equal measure - but at times in his tales of childhood I seemed to sense something remotely, inexplicably familiar, as though we both bore the same pattern, only laid out differently. We readily spent our free time together, roaming the boundless expanses of the Castle or driving by carriage to the capital. He had only lately graduated from the military academy and therefore swaggered everywhere in his brand-new, spotlessly clean and pressed uniform, as though afraid to part for a single day with this, as yet, sole proof of his own adulthood.

* * *

Passing through the long, in places very dark corridors of the Castle, I kept meeting servants hurrying now in one direction, now in another: some carried things on trays to the hall, some bore away dirty dishes - life in the house, invisible to guests behind the parade facade of the dining room, seethed in its own special, businesslike course.

On the landing of the back staircase leading down to the kitchens I came upon Dunyasha. She stood with a tray full of dirty plates and was arguing hotly, in an undertone, with the kitchen boy Mitka - tousle-headed, freckled, about twelve, in a greasy apron three sizes too large for him. At the sight of me both fell silent so abruptly that it was as though they had been caught stealing jam.

"Master," Dunyasha hastily bobbed a curtsy, nearly dropping the tray. "Did you find the pie?"

"I found it, Dunyasha. Thank you. It was delicious."

She blushed. Mitka, taking advantage of the diversion of attention, tried to slip away down the stairs, but Dunyasha, without looking, seized him by the ear with her free hand.

"Now stop. Tell the master what you were spinning me just now."

"Wasn't spinning nothing," Mitka mumbled, wincing. "Told the truth."

"What truth?" I asked.

Mitka squinted at Dunyasha, then at me, sniffed and blurted out:

"There was a light in the left wing. Last night. I ran out to the yard to relieve myself, and look - a little flame in the windows of the left wing. Goes from window to window, slow like. As if someone's walking with a candle."

"Perhaps the watchman?"

"Watchman don't go there, master," said Dunyasha, lowering her voice. "No one goes there. Agafya Tikhonovna won't give out the keys. There, they say..." She hesitated.

"What do they say?"

"A lady walks there," whispered Mitka with dreadful relish. "A white one. The one who lived here before our mistress. Long, long ago. She's been seen in the mirror. Whoever looks in that mirror - to him..."

Dunyasha tugged his ear so that he howled.

"Don't blather nonsense to the master at night!" She turned to me, smiling apologetically. "Don't listen to him, master. He lies about everything. Last month he fancied a house-spirit in the dough tub. Go and rest, master. And..." She paused. "And don't go to the left wing. There are draughts."

"Ignatiy Savvich told me the same," I remarked. "Word for word."

Dunyasha looked at me, and in her round, guileless eyes something flickered which she at once hid behind another curtsy.

"Well, Ignatiy Savvich doesn't speak for nothing, master."

And, snatching up the tray and Mitka at the same time, she vanished below, into the warm half-darkness of the kitchen stairs, smelling of sour cabbage soup.

* * *

Descending the spiral staircase, I could not help recalling the General's tales of his Asian campaigns - I remembered especially the stories about Turkestan and about the mysterious, enchanting Kingdom of Siam. Perhaps one day I too would go there, I thought, see different countries, know different people, and not only those few who crowd evening after evening around my aunt's table.

At the foot of the staircase, in a deep window niche, in a cloud of bluish cigar smoke, sat Doctor Strauch. He had thrown open the window to the garden, and the night air - cool, smelling of mown grass and a distant river - drew the smoke out into the darkness. His gold spectacles glinted in the light of my candle.

"Ah," he said without surprise, as though he had been waiting for me precisely here. "The fugitive. Sit down, young man. I shall not detain you. Doctors must not smoke in front of ladies, and in front of patients still less, but you, thank God, are not yet my patient."

I sat down on the sill opposite. The doctor drew on his cigar, blew smoke out into the garden and was silent for some time.

"I must apologise to you," he said at last. "At supper I was tactless. Your aunt is perfectly right: a dinner party is no place for medical questioning."

"A trifle, Karl Fyodorovich."

"Not a trifle." He turned to me, and his face, usually mockingly dry, was grave. "You see, many years ago your aunt took my word. Never to question you about the past. About anything that was before..." He broke off and corrected himself: "About anything that was earlier. I gave that word and I keep it. I do not know why she demanded it. I never asked."

I felt my fingers, clutching the candlestick, go cold.

"And today?"

"And today," said Strauch slowly, "you forgot the patronymic of a young lady. A trifle. It happens to anyone. But in that instant I looked at your aunt. And I saw fear on her face." He paused. "Not for you, young man. Not the fear that relatives feel when a dear one pales at table. Another. One does not fear illness thus. One fears thus when one sees something returning that one believed long gone."

"What is returning?"

"I do not know." He spread his hands, and the glow of the cigar described an arc in the darkness. "I am a healer, not a clairvoyant. I treat what can be felt with the hands. And this cannot be felt." He drew once more. "But I shall give you one piece of advice. Not as a doctor. As a man who has lived long enough in the world to see a thing or two. Start a notebook. Write in it everything - what you did, with whom you spoke, what you saw. Every evening. Briefly. If one day you discover that the notebook holds an entry which you do not remember - come to me. If you discover that your memory holds something which is not in the notebook - come to me all the more."

"You frighten me, Doctor."

"Not at all," said Strauch, and his customary dry mockery cut through his voice again. "Ghosts frighten. And I am a German. Germans do not frighten, Germans compile lists." He threw the stub into the garden and rose. "Good night, young man. And, I beg you, go straight to your room. Do not turn left."

"Why does everyone tell me tonight not to turn left?" I could not help exclaiming.

The doctor stopped, having already walked off a few paces, and turned.

"Everyone?" he repeated. And, after a pause, added quietly, as if to himself: "Curious. Very curious."

And he went away, leaving behind the smell of tobacco and a window open on the night garden.

* * *

I too often hovered in such thoughts, as in fluffy clouds - and too often because of that habit fell into comic, and sometimes quite vexing situations. So now, wandering through the corridors of the family Castle, I climbed ever farther and deeper, going down and up countless staircases, turning now left, now right an unknown number of times - and with each new turn understood ever less where, in fact, I had got to.

At the turning into yet another corridor I met the last living soul in that part of the house - the housekeeper Agafya Tikhonovna, a small old woman, dry as last year's leaf, in a dark dress and a white cap, with an enormous bunch of keys at her belt, jingling at every step like the bells on a troika. She made her rounds of the house before bed, checking the locks - as she had made them, no doubt, for the past half century - and, seeing me, stopped, raising her lamp higher.

"Can't sleep, dear sir?"

"My head aches, Agafya Tikhonovna. I walk to get some air."

"Some air," repeated the old woman, studying me with faded but keen eyes. "You won't get air on this side, dear sir. The air here is stale." She shook her bunch. "I've checked all the locks. All locked. Except one."

"Which?"

"The one with no key." Agafya Tikhonovna pursed her lips. "To the black grille in the left wing. The key was lost in the late master's time. Forty years, near enough, since it was lost. I don't even look for it any more. The grille stands by itself, held up by rust. No one touches it, and it touches no one. So we live."

"And if the key were found?"

The old woman looked at me long and attentively, as though deciding something to herself.

"If it were found, dear sir," she said at last, "I would throw it in the well. Nothing to be done there. Not for the living, nor..." She crossed herself, leaving it unsaid. "Go to sleep, dear sir. Go. To the right, then down the stairs, then straight on - and you'll be at your own door."

Right. Then down. Then straight. I thanked her and went - to the right, as she had said. The jingle of her keys faded for a long time behind my back.

To this day I cannot explain how it happened that, having turned right, I found myself some minutes later not at the staircase leading down but in a corridor I had never seen before.

* * *

The candles in this part of the house burned more rarely and more dimly than in the parade halls - and soon ceased altogether, and thereafter I walked only with my own, shielding the flame with my palm from the draught - and so the portraits along the walls - a family gallery which, I confess, I had never before examined attentively - seemed lit from below, giving the faces of the ancestors an unpleasant, unsteady depth.

There were many of them here. More than I had ever seen in the parade part of the house. Ladies in powdered wigs and panniered gowns, with fans and beauty patches on their cheeks; men in kaftans, in armour, in the uniforms of long-abolished regiments; infants in lace in the arms of wet-nurses; an old man in a sable coat, with a beard to his belt, who looked from the canvas so sternly that he seemed ready even now to send someone off to Siberia. Under each portrait, on a copper plate, a name was engraved - I held my candle to several, but the names told me nothing. Or told me - but I at once forgot, just as at supper I had forgotten Lizonka's patronymic.

One especially stern gentleman in a powdered wig, who, judging by the inscription on the age-darkened frame, was my great-great-grandfather, if the family tree was to be believed, seemed to look not through me, as portraits usually look, but straight at me - and as soon as I looked away and turned back again, it seemed that his pupils had shifted a hair's breadth to one side, following my movement.

I smiled at my own suspiciousness. Candles, shadows, fatigue, headache - that was the whole solution, I told myself, and yet I quickened my step past that corridor, not wishing to test the guess with a second look.

At the very end of the gallery hung a portrait draped in black crape. Such crape is hung as a sign of mourning - and is usually removed after a year, after two. This one, judging by the thick layer of dust lying in its folds, had not been removed for decades.

I stopped before it. I ought not to have touched it - I knew this as clearly as I knew that I ought not to turn left. And yet my hand reached of itself for the edge of the cloth.

The crape slid down, raising a little cloud of dust that made me cough.

Beneath it was a portrait of a young man. I could not make out the face - the paint on it had darkened, cracked into a fine net, as though the canvas had been kept long by a fire or, on the contrary, in damp, and the features had run into an indistinguishable dark blot. But all the rest had survived surprisingly well: a dark frock coat of old-fashioned cut, a light neckcloth tied in an intricate knot - I involuntarily touched my own neckerchief - and hands. Hands folded on the knees, and in the palm - two small, smooth objects. One black. The other white.

There was a copper plate beneath the portrait. But the name on it had been carefully scraped away with a knife, down to the bright copper.

I stood a long time before that portrait, and the candle in my hand trembled so that the shadows darted about the gallery like a flock of frightened birds. I did not know who this was. I did not know what the objects in his hand were. I did not know why, looking at them - two tiny flecks of paint, black and white - I felt in my palm a strange, phantom weight, as though I myself were holding something I did not have.

I lowered the crape back. It lay unevenly, crookedly, and the dust on it was now wiped away by my fingers. In the morning, I thought, someone - Agafya Tikhonovna, Ignatiy Savvich - would notice that it had been touched. And would understand by whom.

* * *

A little farther on, in a shallow niche between two doors which I had likewise not noticed before, hung an old mirror, darkened with time, in a heavy carved frame. I paused before it for a second - merely to straighten the neckerchief at my throat, no more - and at that very moment my reflection seemed suddenly to hesitate: my hand had already dropped from the collar, while the hand in the mirror, as it seemed to me, lingered for a barely perceptible instant at the throat, as if lagging behind me by one single, tiny heartbeat.

I froze. I peered. The reflection, of course, looked at me in complete accord with my every movement - no delay, no falseness, an ordinary tired face in dim glass. And yet the unpleasant chill that had settled somewhere between my shoulder blades would not disperse as swiftly as the illusion itself had dispersed.

"She's been seen in the mirror," I recalled Mitka's whisper. "Whoever looks in that mirror - to him..."

He had not finished. Dunyasha had not let him.

"This castle is older than it seems," I recalled next my aunt's words, spoken in parting, "and it has places that are better left in peace."

At the time, at table, I had taken those words for ordinary social coquetry, a light, frightening impromptu of a hostess who wished to add a touch of mystery to the house before her guests. Now, standing alone before the old mirror, in a corridor I did not remember before, those words for some reason sounded different - heavier, weightier, not at all like a joke. Ignatiy Savvich. Dunyasha. Doctor Strauch. Agafya Tikhonovna. Four people in one evening had told me the same thing in different words: do not go left. And four times I had not listened.

I shook my head to drive off the illusion and moved on, not entirely understanding why, instead of looking for the way back to my own rooms, I continued with every step to penetrate deeper into this unknown part of the house. As though someone were leading me by the hand. As though my feet - as, for some reason, it had seemed to me more than once, somewhere, sometime - knew the way better than I did.

* * *

Having turned several more times and lost all count of the turns, I suddenly came upon something which I definitely did not remember from my earlier, far more careful strolls through the Castle - a strange black grille that barred the corridor from the floor to the high vaulted ceiling.

Who had placed it here, and for what purpose, amid the family corridors, I wondered. Surely not my aunt - but she had never before mentioned this place by a single word, however much I had questioned her about the distant, locked wings of the house.

Approaching closer, I made out on the massive metal plate that held the grille in its frame a fine tracery, almost invisible in the meagre light - whether ornament or inscription, executed in a script so whimsical and unfamiliar that I could hardly associate it with any alphabet known to me. Its lines bent into one another with that deliberate intricacy with which, it seems, they write only in the most distant, most mysterious corners of the world - and for one brief, instantly elusive moment it seemed to me that I had already seen something similar, though I could not have recalled where or when.

"An Eastern whim, most likely," I decided, too tired at that moment to dwell on this trifle longer than a second. "Perhaps one of my ancestors brought this grille from one of his campaigns, together with other curiosities that adorn our house."

This explanation, I confess, suited me far better than any other - and other explanations at that hour, amid dimly burning candles and attentive portrait eyes, I decidedly did not wish to seek.

Catching myself in this convenient thought and resolving not to delay another second, I examined the grille more closely and discovered a lock on it - small, opening only a small part of it, a narrow door, enough for one person to squeeze through.

The lock hung open.

Not broken open - the shackle was intact, the rust lay on it in an even, untouched layer. Not smashed. Simply open - as though someone had unlocked it with the key that had been lost forty years ago, and left it so, inviting entry. As though he knew I would come.

"The key was lost in the late master's time," Agafya Tikhonovna had said. "I would throw it in the well."

I stood before the grille holding the candle, whose flame suddenly lay on its side, stretching toward the door - though there was not, I was ready to swear, the slightest draught. Somewhere far behind, in the parade part of the house, the piano still sounded. Konstantin had said: shout, I shall hear. I did not shout.

Yielding either to fatigue, or to a simple, inexplicable stubbornness, or to something third for which I had no name, I lifted the open lock from its hasp and pushed the door.

The door creaked unpleasantly - the way iron creaks that has rusted not over years even but over decades of silent, undisturbed repose - but nevertheless yielded and swung open, admitting me into new, entirely unexplored corridors. No sooner had I passed the threshold than the grille behind my back slammed shut of itself, with a still more unpleasant, grating sound - a sound remotely like a dull blow - and that sound rolled away down the corridor, echoing from wall to wall, receding farther and farther until it died somewhere in the most distant, most hidden labyrinths of the Castle, leaving me - now on the other side of the grille - in a silence so complete that my ears rang, as from a long, unbroken stillness.

The piano could no longer be heard.

I turned. The lock, which I had just held in my hand, hung on the hasp of the grille - locked.

And at that very instant the candle in my hand, without any wind, went out.
