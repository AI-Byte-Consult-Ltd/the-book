Chapter XXIII
The Hunt in the Papyrus Thickets

Four Years Before the Drought

The Nile came early that year and came generously.

I remember it as clearly as I seem to remember nothing else of those years - as though my memory, which gives up everything else so reluctantly, had kept that year in a separate casket wrapped in fine linen, and took it out only so that later, in the drought, there would be something to compare with. The water rose in a single night - the keeper of the nilometer, Seti, ran to the palace himself, without a messenger, in nothing but a loincloth, out of breath, barefoot, and shouted from the very gates so that the whole court heard: "Sixteen cubits! Sixteen! The barley will be fed to the donkeys!" He was four years younger then, and still knew how to run.

Sixteen cubits. A perfect flood. The kind that comes once in a generation. When the water covers all the fields but does not flood the villages. When the earth, laid bare after the ebb, becomes so black and fat that a grain thrown into it sprouts in three days.

The whole Valley was green that year. Not scorched brown, as now, not cracked, but green - lush, thick, moist, to the very horizon where the green butted against the yellow wall of the desert as the sea butts against a shore. The canals stood full to the brim. Dragonflies flew over the water - blue, green, gold. Lotuses bloomed so densely that on some channels no water could be seen. And in the Delta, in the north, where the river splits into seven branches before going out into the Great Green, there in the Delta stood thickets of papyrus taller than a man, and in them lived birds.

Millions of birds.

* * *

"Does my Pharaoh deign to shoot or to throw?" asked old Mehi, the huntsman, not raising his head from the boat he was inspecting - finger by finger, stalk by stalk.

The boat was made of papyrus - bundles of stalks tightly bound with cords of palm fibre, with the prow and stern curved upward, light as a leaf, flat as a palm. In such a boat one could pass where no wooden barge could go: through shallows, through thickets, along channels two cubits wide.

Mehi was old even then. Dry, sinewy, smoked by the sun to the colour of old leather, with hands covered with small scars from fishhooks, and with one ear - the other, as he told everyone who asked, a crocodile had bitten off when Mehi was twelve. "I found him afterward," he always added. "Thirty years later. Knew him by a tooth that was missing. My tooth. I knocked it out when he bit off the ear. We looked at each other a long time. Then he swam away. I didn't touch him. He was old already. Like me."

"Throw," I said. "Like the ancestors."

Mehi grunted contentedly and handed me a throwing stick - curved, flat, of heavy black wood, with the end carved in the shape of a snake's head. Such sticks had lain in the tombs of Pharaohs since time immemorial - with them birds were struck in the thickets, thrown so that the stick, spinning, flew over the water and brought down two or three at once.

"And the cat?" I asked.

"The cat is ready, my Pharaoh." Mehi nodded toward the stern.

In the stern, on a rolled mat, sat a cat. Huge, spotted, reddish-grey, with tufts on its ears and such green eyes that it seemed the thickets were reflected in them. His name was Mau - simply "cat," as, it seems, all hunting cats in the Delta are called. He looked at the thickets, twitching his tail, and from time to time made a quiet, throaty, impatient sound - not a mew but rather a growl.

"He knows better than I where the birds are," said Mehi proudly. "I throw - he fetches. Twenty years like that. He is my third Mau. The first was better. The second - lazy. This one - sly. Sly is better than lazy. Worse than the best."

* * *

There were many of us that day. Too many for a hunt, Mehi said, grumbling under his breath - birds take fright when there are more than three boats on the water. But I was Pharaoh, and Pharaoh did not hunt alone.

In the next boat - Senusret. Not yet commander of the whole army, then, but commander of my personal guard - younger than now by four years, with fewer wrinkles and the same stone face. He did not hunt. He sat in the stern with a spear on his knees and looked not at the birds but at the thickets around me - at every shadow, at every stirring of the reeds.

"Senusret," I said. "Throw a stick at least once. For pleasure."

"My pleasure, my Pharaoh," answered Senusret, not taking his eyes from the thickets, "lies in your returning from the hunt with the same number of ears you have now. Unlike the honourable Mehi."

Mehi giggled, not in the least offended.

In the third boat - Paser. Not yet an old man with a staff, but a sturdy, greying man, commander of the chariotry, who, the moment he stepped off a chariot, felt on water as uneasy as a fish on sand, and sat in the boat clutching its sides with both hands, with a face greenish as young papyrus.

"Paser," I called. "Are you unwell?"

"I, my Pharaoh, am always unwell where there is no ground underfoot and no horses ahead," Paser answered gloomily. "Horses at least understand where they are being led. And this bundle of reeds..." he slapped the side of the boat with disgust, "this bundle of reeds thinks only of how to drown me."

"It will not drown you, honourable one," said his oarsman, a young fisherman with white teeth. "Papyrus does not sink. It may overturn. But it will not sink."

"Comforting," muttered Paser.

And in the fourth boat, the smartest, with gilded sides and a canopy of fine linen - Khaemuas. Then he was some twenty, perhaps a little more. Young, handsome, cheerful, the son of a noble family from the Delta, who had just received his first post at court - junior charioteer - and was immeasurably proud of it. His bracelets already jingled on both arms, though not as many as now. He laughed, joked, threw his stick - clumsily, but with such zest that spray flew in all directions - and I, looking at him, thought that what the court lacked was precisely such men: light, carefree, not afraid to laugh before Pharaoh.

I could not know then what he would turn into in four years. No one could. And if someone had told me, I would not have believed it.

Though perhaps I should have. Because even then, on that sunny day, in a pause between throws, Khaemuas, laughing, said to his oarsman - loudly enough for me to hear:

"My father says we owe the temple of Ptah so much that the priests will soon take our fields. And I say - let them take them! Then I'll have a new bracelet. For a third arm!"

The oarsman laughed. Khaemuas laughed. I smiled too, then - the joke was merry.

Only now, four years later, recalling that day, I do not smile.

* * *

And in the smallest boat - narrow, old, with peeling sides - behind everyone, almost unnoticed, sat a boy.

I paid him hardly any attention that day. A boy of thirteen or fourteen, thin, dark-eyed, in a plain linen apron, with a writing board on his knees and a reed brush in his hand. A junior pupil of the scribes, sent by the senior scribe of the Library to record the hunt - how many birds, which, by whom struck - as is the custom to record all that Pharaoh does. The boy wrote with his head bent, and every so often rubbed his cheek with the brush, forgetting that the brush was inked, so that by midday his face was all black spots, like Mehi's spotted cat.

I remembered him only because at some moment - when the boats stopped in a quiet channel to wait out noon - he suddenly raised his head and looked at me. Straight. For a long time. Not as one looks at Pharaoh - with lowered eyes, with reverent fear - but as if he wanted to remember. As if he were drawing me with his gaze on that board lying on his knees.

And then he saw that I was looking at him - blushed to the roots of his hair and buried his nose in the board.

I did not know his name then. I did not ask.

* * *

The birds rose at noon.

Mau suddenly arched his back, flattened his ears, made a thin, ringing sound - and in that same instant out of the thickets, ten paces from us, with a deafening noise, with splashing, with cries, a flock burst up. Ducks, geese, herons, ibises, some small motley birds - hundreds, thousands - the sky over the channel darkened with wings.

"Throw, my Pharaoh!" cried Mehi. "Now! Throw!"

I threw. The stick left my hand - spinning, humming, snake's head foremost - and I saw it pass through the flock, saw two ducks fall into the water, tumbling. Mau leapt from the stern before they touched the water - straight into the thickets, into the reeds, into the mud - and a moment later surfaced with a duck in his teeth, wet, plastered with slime, happy.

Khaemuas threw, missed, laughed, threw again. Paser clutched the sides and shut his eyes. Senusret did not stir - he watched the thickets, where no one but the birds moved.

And the boy in the last boat wrote. Quickly, not looking at his board. He looked at the sky, at the flock, at me - and wrote.

And then happened what I afterward recalled many times.

One of the herons - huge, grey - darted right over the last boat, low, almost touching heads with its wings. The boy threw up his hand, shielding himself - the boat rocked - the board slipped from his hands - he reached for it, leaned over the side - and the light, peeling little papyrus boat overturned.

He went under without a single sound.

I do not remember how I came to be in the water. I remember only that the water was warm, muddy, full of silt, that papyrus stalks lashed my face, that somewhere behind me Senusret was shouting - "My Pharaoh! My Pharaoh!" - and that my fingers felt in the murky water a thin arm and clutched it.

I pulled him out. The boy was light as a reed. He coughed, choked, spat out water and silt, and his face, covered with ink spots, was white as linen. I held him above the water, standing chest-deep in the channel among lotuses and reeds, in clothes that cost more than his whole clan - and he looked at me with huge dark eyes.

"The board," he rasped. "My Pharaoh... the board..."

"What board?" I laughed, not knowing why myself. "You nearly drowned, and you think about a board?"

"It's written there..." He coughed again. "It's written there how many birds my Pharaoh struck. If it sinks - no one will know. As though the hunt had never been."

Senusret was already paddling up to us in his boat - white as the boy, with his spear ready. Mehi was shouting something. Khaemuas was roaring with laughter. And I stood in the water holding in my arms this absurd, ink-smeared child who, having nearly drowned, worried that my hunt would be forgotten - and suddenly felt something I could not name. As if I had held him like this once before. Long ago. In other water.

Mau, wet, plastered with slime, surfaced beside us out of the thickets. In his teeth was the board.

* * *

We saw the hippopotamus toward evening.

Mehi took us there on purpose - to a broad, deep backwater on the edge of the thickets, where the water was dark and still, and above it, like black boulders, backs protruded. Three. No, four. Huge, grey, glistening, with small ears which from time to time twitched, shaking off flies.

"They trample the fields," said Mehi, lowering his voice to a whisper. "In the last flood four villages were left without a harvest. These beasts eat in a night what a village eats in a month." He paused. "The priests say - this is Set. The evil god. He turns into a hippopotamus. When Pharaoh hunts a hippopotamus - he is like Horus striking Set. For his father. For Osiris."

"Do you believe that, Mehi?"

The old huntsman shrugged.

"I believe that a hippopotamus killed my brother, my Pharaoh. Overturned the boat and bit him in half. Whether Set was in the hippopotamus - I don't know. But my brother is gone." He held out to me a harpoon - long, with a copper head, tied to a coil of rope. "Horus struck Set for eighty years. My Pharaoh can finish in a single evening."

I took the harpoon.

I know how this was supposed to look - like on the walls of tombs: Pharaoh stands in the prow of a papyrus barge, proud, calm, with a harpoon raised over his head, and below, in the water, the vanquished beast writhes. That is how my predecessors were drawn. That, no doubt, is how I shall be drawn.

In fact it looked otherwise. The hippopotamus rose from the water right in front of our boat - suddenly, soundlessly, huge as a house - and opened its jaws. The jaws were pink. I remember that. Pink as an infant's, and in them two tusks, long, curved, yellow, each as long as my arm. The wave rocked the boat. Mehi cried out. Senusret, in the next boat, leapt up with his spear.

I threw the harpoon.

I do not remember whether it hit. I remember only that the rope pulled taut, jerked, burned my palms, that the boat was dragged, spun, that Mehi hung on the rope together with me, that the water around turned red - and that the hippopotamus, uttering a roar that shook the thickets, went into the depths and did not show itself again.

The rope went slack. At its end, when Mehi hauled it from the water, there was no harpoon.

"Gone," said Mehi. Without disappointment. Almost with relief. "Wounded, but gone." He looked at me. "My Pharaoh, this is good."

"Good?"

"The priests say: Horus strikes Set but does not kill him. Never. Eighty years he strikes - and does not kill. Because if Set dies - the world loses its balance. There will be only order, only Maat. And the world cannot live on order alone. Chaos is needed too. A little. So that order remembers why it is needed." Mehi spat into the water. "So the priests say. I say: the hippopotamus is gone, and everyone is alive. That is better than the hippopotamus dead and someone missing an ear."

* * *

We spent the night on an island in the middle of the Delta - a sandbar overgrown with palms, where servants pitched tents and lit fires.

There, by the fires, I first saw Nefer-Ka as High Priest.

The old High Priest had died half a year before that flood - quietly, in his sleep, at ninety. Nefer-Ka had received his staff only three moons earlier. He sailed to the island at sunset, on a separate barge, accompanied by junior priests - to wish Pharaoh a good hunt and to bring the blessing of the gods. He was four years younger then, but looked exactly as he does now: tall, straight as an obelisk, with an immobile face and with eyes in which nothing could be read.

"My Pharaoh," he said, bowing exactly as far as custom required. Not a finger lower. Even then. "I hear you plunged into the water to save a child."

"A scribe," I corrected.

"A scribe," Nefer-Ka agreed. "Noble." He paused. "But careless. Pharaoh is a living god. Son of Ra. Incarnation of Horus. If you had drowned in a channel saving a scribe-boy - what would the gods have said?"

"That Pharaoh saved a scribe," I answered.

Nefer-Ka gave me a long look. Then smiled - with that same smile which I afterward came to know so well: condescending, patient.

"The gods, my Pharaoh, speak in other words," he said. "But I will convey yours to them." And, already turning away, he added quietly, as if to himself: "A living god who forgets that he is a god. Curious."

He sailed away at dawn. I attached no importance then to his words.

* * *

But the main thing that night happened not by the fires, but afterward, when all had fallen asleep.

I did not sleep. I lay on a mat at the very water's edge, behind the tents, where it was quieter, and looked at the sky. The sky over the Delta that night was such as I seem never to have seen again: black, bottomless, and in it - stars. So many stars that between them there was hardly any blackness. And across the whole sky, from edge to edge, flowed a pale, shimmering, misty river.

"The Heavenly Nile," said someone beside me.

I turned. On the sand, two paces from me, sat an old man in the long white robe of a priest, with a shaven head, with some wooden rod in his hands - with a slit at one end and a plumb line on a thread. I had not heard him come.

"Seneb," he introduced himself, neither rising nor bowing, which by day would have been insolence, but at night, on the sand, by the water, somehow did not seem insolent. "Observer of the night hours from Heliopolis. I was sent with the High Priest - to watch the stars over the Delta. Here they are seen better than in the city." He raised the rod, squinted through the slit at some star. "Does my Pharaoh not sleep?"

"My Pharaoh does not sleep," I said. "Tell me about the sky, Seneb."

The old man lowered the rod. Was silent.

"The sky is Nut," he began slowly, as one begins a long-learned lesson, but his voice was warm, without any boredom. "A goddess. She stands over the earth, arched, resting her hands on the west, her feet on the east. The stars are ornaments on her body. And every evening she swallows the sun. Ra enters her mouth in the west and all night goes inside her body - through twelve hours, through the twelve gates of the Duat. And in the morning she bears him again - in the east. Red. Like an infant."

"Every day," I said.

"Every day, my Pharaoh. Since the beginning of time." Seneb raised his hand and pointed to the misty river across the sky. "And this is her milk. Or the Heavenly Nile, along which souls sail. Depending on who is telling."

He showed me three bright stars in a row - a belt - and around them several more, composing the figure of a man striding across the sky.

"Sah," said Seneb. "Osiris. The god whom his brother killed, tore into pieces, scattered across Egypt. And Isis gathered him. Piece by piece. And revived him - for one night, to conceive Horus by him. And then Osiris went away. Became king of the dead." He paused. "But every night he rises in the sky. There he is. Striding. Never stopping."

"And behind him?"

Seneb moved his hand lower, to the left, to where above the very horizon, at the edge of the earth, a star shone - brighter than all, white, shimmering, as if winking.

"Sopdet," he said softly, almost tenderly. "Isis. The wife. She always follows him. Always one step behind. Never catches up. But never lags." He paused. "Each year she vanishes from the sky for seventy days. And then returns - at dawn, just before the sun, over the eastern horizon. And on the day she returns, the flood comes." He smiled. "This year she returned right on time. And the Nile came to sixteen cubits. A good year. A very good year."

I looked at the white star by the horizon. Isis, who always walks one step behind. Never catches up. Never lags.

"Seneb," I said. "Do you believe that Pharaoh is a living god?"

The old man was silent for a long time. So long that I thought he had fallen asleep.

"My Pharaoh asks a dangerous question," he said at last. "If I say 'no' - I may be executed. If I say 'yes' - I shall lie. Because I do not know."

"Say what you think. There is no one here. Only we and Nut."

Seneb smiled.

"I think, my Pharaoh," he said, "that gods are like stars. They can be seen only when it is dark. By day, in the sun, no one looks at the sky. Everyone thinks of bread, of beer, of taxes. And at night, when it is dark, they look up and see gods." He paused. "Pharaoh is the one at whom people look when it is dark for them. When there is drought. When war. When plague. They look at Pharaoh and see a god. Because they need to see a god." He raised the rod to his eye again. "And whether there is truly a god in Pharaoh - that I do not know. Perhaps Pharaoh himself knows."

"Pharaoh does not know," I said.

Seneb lowered the rod and looked at me - for the first time in the whole conversation directly, attentively.

"Then he is a good Pharaoh," he said. "The bad ones always know."

* * *

He showed me one more thing - just before dawn, when Sah was already going down behind the horizon and the sky in the east was beginning to grey.

"Do you see, my Pharaoh?" Seneb pointed north, at stars standing high above the horizon. "These do not set. Never. All the others sink in the west, die, are born again in the east. And these - circle. Around one point. All night. All their lives. Ever wakeful."

"The Imperishable," I said.

"The Imperishable." Seneb nodded. "The ancients said: after death Pharaoh becomes one of them. That is why in the oldest tombs there are shafts directed straight at them. Narrow as a needle. So that the soul of Pharaoh knows where to fly." He paused. "But my teacher, the old observer, told me something else. He said that the Imperishable are not those who have gone. They are those who have not yet come. Who circle round the earth and wait for their hour. To descend. As the First Ones descended."

"The First Ones, who came down from the Heavens," I said.

"You know the Epic, my Pharaoh," Seneb nodded. "Of course. You are of them, after all."

I flinched.

"What does it mean - of them?"

The old man shrugged - as if he had said something self-evident.

"All Pharaohs are of them, my Pharaoh. So it is written. The blood of the First Ones. Descendants of those who descended." He paused, looking at the sky. "Though my teacher said it is not quite so. He said - not descendants. He said - the same ones. That the First Ones do not die. They go to the Imperishable - and descend again. In another body. In another time. And each time forget who they are." Seneb smiled. "My teacher was a strange man. He was afterward sent to observe the stars in the farthest temple by the southern cataracts. For heresy."

By the southern cataracts. The rock that herdsmen give a wide berth.

But then, that night, I knew nothing yet of any Rock.

* * *

The sun rose from behind the eastern edge of the Delta - huge, red, wet, as if Nut had just given birth to it. The lotuses on the channel opened their petals one after another to the light - white, blue, pink. A mist rose over the water. Somewhere in the thickets birds were crying.

Seneb had gone to his barge, never having bowed. The camp was waking. Mehi was grumbling over the boats. Khaemuas, sleepy, was laughing, splashing water on his face. Paser looked gloomily at the river, plainly calculating how many more hours he would have to spend on a bundle of reeds. Senusret - I knew without looking - stood somewhere behind my back with his spear.

And at the very water's edge, on the sand, sat the scribe boy. With the board on his knees which Mau had fished out for him. He was writing. Noticing that I was looking at him, he blushed again, but this time did not look away.

I went up to him.

"What are you writing?"

He silently held out the board to me. On it, in neat, small signs, everything was recorded: how many boats, how many birds, by whom struck. The hippopotamus who had gone with the harpoon. And at the very end - a line that did not relate to the hunt.

"Pharaoh pulled a junior scribe out of the water. The scribe promises to remember this all his life. Even if Pharaoh forgets."

I raised my eyes to the boy.

"Why 'even if he forgets'?"

The boy lowered his head.

"Because Pharaohs save many," he said quietly. "And the scribe was saved once. Pharaoh may forget. The scribe - will not."

"What is your name?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

"My Pharaoh!" Senusret's voice rang out. "The barges are ready! The High Priest has sent word - they are waiting in the capital!"

I turned to the voice. And when I turned back - the boy was already being called by the senior scribe, and he, bowing hurriedly, ran off to his peeling little boat, pressing the board to his chest.

I never learned his name. I forgot his face within a moon - among the thousands of faces that passed before the throne. I forgot him altogether.

And only now, four years later, in the drought, sitting in the dark Library beside the junior keeper of scrolls who always has ink on his cheek - only now did I suddenly remember that day. The channel. The warm muddy water. The arm, light as a reed. And the dark eyes that looked at me as if drawing me on a board so as not to forget.

"The boy from the Library remembers a great deal for you," old Amenemope had said. "More than you think."

The Nile that year came to sixteen cubits. And I did not remember. Four years - I did not remember.

But he remembered. All four years.
